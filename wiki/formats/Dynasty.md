# Dynasty

Rosters carry over year to year; players are held as long-term assets, not
just season-long pieces. Second-priority format for the wiki owner.

## What matters here
- Age curves, contract/draft-capital signals, and long-term role over
  current-year points
- Rookie/prospect evaluation (this is exactly Matt Waldman's specialty — see
  [[Matt Waldman]])
- Trade value and rebuild/contend timeline framing

- 2024-03-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-23]]), solo cast on the 2024 RB class: a historical hit-rate framework directly informing rookie-draft strategy. Reviewing pre-draft grades against outcomes for the 2016-2023 RB classes, he finds the true number of multi-year successful starters per class is consistently just one to three (e.g. two of 23 graded 2016 prospects — Ezekiel Elliott and Derrick Henry — became sustained starters; the loaded 2018 class produced only Saquon Barkley and Nick Chubb as "true survivors"; 2017's six hits, headlined by [[Christian McCaffrey]] and [[Alvin Kamara]], is "a complete outlier"). His conclusion: "it's probably best to presume that most running back classes are bad classes," including 2024 — which he grades as having a wider (24-player) but shallower group of plausible contributors than 2023, with only two grading at true immediate-starter level versus five or six the year before. Explicit strategic recommendation: place a higher rookie-draft priority on receivers and quarterbacks, then trade for established veteran running backs once a team has built a talent stockpile elsewhere — rather than reaching for running backs early on the (statistically unlikely) bet that a given prospect beats the class's own base rate.

- 2024-09-05 — According to Adam Harstad (Footballguys co-host, not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-05 (Film and Theory)]] [Dynasty]: the most common dynasty approach — be competent everywhere and have no weaknesses — is backwards, because leagues are not won by the team with the fewest weaknesses but by the team with the most strengths. Waldman's own construction is anchor-based — pick the positions your scoring system rewards most and stockpile them (receivers and linebackers in his IDP leagues), then trade surplus into the rest. Harstad anchors by player rather than position, wanting as many players as possible he is confident will still produce in three years, since their production is effectively free.

- 2024-09-12 — According to co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman]]'s show ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-12]]) [Dynasty]: the dynasty market has become materially more efficient. When he started there were no dynasty sites and barely any rankings, so a back could be ranked anywhere from 8th to 52nd depending on who you asked; DLF's monthly dynasty ADP and then, over the last five years, KeepTradeCut and FantasyCalc produced descriptive community consensus values that most managers now rationally use. He still encourages active trading for anyone who can do it competently, but warns it takes far more work and consensus-building than it used to, and that you should trade around consensus price rather than your own valuation. - 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31 (Tapping Out, Archetypes and Modeling)]]) [Dynasty]: co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) gave a framework for dynasty trade discipline. Edges are thin — a 55-45 preference is 'massive' and 60-40 'gargantuan', and a manager who can stack 55-45 edges has the most dominant team in the league — so giving one up on an emotional reaction to a single game is malpractice. His worked example was the 2023 [[Drake London]] panic after a zero-catch Week 1, when a Ryan McDowell dynasty poll had the majority taking [[Zay Flowers]] and real trades were happening. His timing test: if you preferred Flowers before that game you should have traded before it; if you preferred London before it and flipped after one game, that is bad process irrespective of the outcome. He also named the emotional mechanic honestly — 'I paid to acquire him, I started him, I got a zero, now we are mortal enemies' — and said he likes that side of fantasy but it should not price your assets.

- 2024-11-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-14 (Age Cliffs and Dented Cans)]]) [Dynasty]: dynasty valuation should use age cliffs rather than age curves (see [[Aging Curves and Career Longevity]]), because the two models can produce the same expected value while implying very different risk profiles — and in a win-first-of-12 format the high-variance profile is worth more. - 2025-09-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-04 (Film & Theory Sequences, Signal, Steelers)]]) [Dynasty]: co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) explained why dynasty trade markets stay liquid while redraft ones do not — in dynasty, managers want different things (some chasing a title, some rebuilding, some just making the playoffs), and rosters go badly unbalanced in a way redraft rosters do not, so a manager with four quarterbacks, one running back, eight receivers and no tight ends is naturally open to a deal. His method is to hold firm values and transact whenever the other side is paying more or selling for less than his number, before week one, after week one, whenever. He extended the fumble analogy to trading: if you never lose a trade you are not trading enough, and a 90-10 trade is pure profit even though it lowers your success rate. He keeps a list of his own atrocious trades — A.J. Green and Demaryius Thomas for Maurice Jones-Drew, Davante Adams for Dion Lewis — as a discipline against pretending the process was bad just because the outcome was.
