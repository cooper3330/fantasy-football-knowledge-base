## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-11-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-11-13 (Paper Champions and All-Timers Across Eras)]]): untracked co-host Adam Harstad's framework is that the amount of talent in any given draft class is essentially random — some years are good receiver classes, some good quarterback classes (2004), some good running back classes (2017) — and that occasionally randomness stacks several loaded classes in a row. The early 2000s stacked roughly eight loaded running back classes, producing a surplus that, in his words, warped fantasy football for decades: managers concluded the key to winning was rostering two elite backs, which worked only because 15 or 16 of them existed at once. His evidence is the 2004 fantasy scoring list — Shaun Alexander, Tiki Barber, Tomlinson, Curtis Martin, Edgerrin James, Corey Dillon, Westbrook, Portis, Priest Holmes, Warrick Dunn, Ahman Green, Jerome Bettis, Deuce McAllister, Fred Taylor — with Jamal Lewis's 2,000-yard season roughly the tenth-best back in the league. The trap: treating a random clustering as the normal baseline for a position.
- 2025-12-15 — According to Patrick Doherty of Rotoworld (untracked guest on [[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-12-15]]) with [[Chris Harris]]: 2025's tight end class is deep in quantity but not quality -- Doherty says 'there's not a whole lot of difference some weeks between the tight end 8 and even the tight end 24,' making recent-performance recency a legitimate tiebreaker between otherwise interchangeable options like [[Dallas Goedert]], [[Mark Andrews]], [[Hunter Henry]] and [[Colby Parkinson]]. Harris independently makes the same point about the Rams' tight end depth chart, saying there's effectively no gap between Hunter Henry at TE14 and a player like A.J. Barner at TE22.
