# NFL Combine and Pro Day Skepticism

## Definition

Matt Waldman's recurring position that combine workouts and pro days are
overrated as team-evaluation tools relative to film. In his framework, these
events are genuinely useful mainly for filling information gaps on
small-school or lesser-scouted prospects — not for moving the needle on
players teams have already scouted thoroughly on tape. He separates this from
the entertainment value of the events, which he doesn't dispute.

## Expert Takes

- 2024-02-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-26]]): the combine's real value to NFL teams is filling gaps on players they haven't seen enough of — smaller-school prospects, or players whose measurable athletic traits the tape doesn't clearly show — plus in-person interviews. He does not consider it a legitimate tool for re-grading players teams have already scouted extensively, and calls any team claiming a workout changed its mind on an established prospect "nonsense." Cites [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] skipping all combine workouts (and going without an agent) as the correct call for a prospect who's "done all that he needs to do" on film — losing weight to look faster in a drill unrepresentative of his actual game would only cost him development time elsewhere. Relays (from a prior *Going Deep* episode) [[Brandon Angelo]]'s first-hand critique of combine testing reliability — as a former Purdue track athlete and performance trainer familiar with the actual equipment used, Angelo has found the machines inconsistently calibrated and the resulting numbers "very frequently not nearly as accurate as they're made out to be." Recounts a historical Alex Smith pro-day anecdote (via the late Gil Brandt/*Sports Illustrated* reporting, corroborated independently to Waldman by a scout who was in the room): a St. Louis Rams front-office executive brought scouts an edited highlight tape of Smith's pro day and told them to disregard their own completed scouting reports, nearly causing a fistfight among the scouting staff — offered as concrete evidence of how scripted and gamed pro days can be, the same dynamic Waldman says overrated Zach Wilson and Mitchell Trubisky (neither tracked) as prospects. Endorses Kent Lee Platt's Relative Athletic Score (RAS) — Platt is not a tracked expert, an external analytics account — as a worthwhile secondary reference, with the explicit caveat that film should "trump" the measurement. Applies the same logic to combine invitations for lower-profile 2024 running back prospects: see [[Deshaun Fenwick]] (invited, unresolved measurables) vs. [[Blake Watson]] (not invited, read as a limited-team-interest signal rather than a talent verdict).
- 2024-02-27 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host — not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-27]]): a more skeptical companion take to Waldman's full-throated defense one day earlier of [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] skipping combine testing. Harmon isn't opposed to skipping the combine itself (several 2024 quarterbacks are doing the same), but flags Harrison also skipping his Ohio State pro day as a real outlier — "the trifecta," beyond what healthy non-quarterback precedents like DeVonta Smith and Jalen Waddle did. Says it doesn't change his own evaluation, since teams already have access to Harrison's private testing numbers (GPS, bench, shuttle, broad jump) regardless of a live workout — but reads the decision as partly about managing a media narrative (avoiding a bad 40 time becoming a talking point) rather than a pure football calculation.

- 2024-03-01 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-01]]), relaying [[Brandon Angelo]]'s explanation in detail: there are five ways the 40 gets timed, from least to most reliable — pure hand-timing; the Combine's own "Zybek" system (electronic start, hand-timed finish); RFID/GPS chip tracking; and single- or dual-laser timing (used in Olympic track). Angelo's estimate for the Zybek's standard deviation is 0.24–0.3 seconds — meaning a player who "really" runs a 4.45 could plausibly be officially timed anywhere up to a 4.69, which Waldman says is the practical gap between a first-round and a "third day at best" wide receiver grade. Applies this directly to [[Quentin Johnston]] as a player whose agent likely saved him real money by skipping the 40. Separately argues the error matters most from roughly the fifth round of the draft downward: executives and coaches who don't understand the statistical noise treat a bad time as confirming a talent doubt, and give the player fewer reps as a result — a feedback loop that suppresses opportunity independent of actual ability.
- 2024-03-01 — **Adam Harstad** (co-host on *RSP Film and Theory* — not a tracked expert), same episode: reframes the debate through four types of measurement validity. Agrees the 40's *construct validity* (how accurately it measures 40-yard speed specifically) is weak given the timing-system noise, and that "urban legend" times — Larry Fitzgerald's reported 4.63, Jerry Rice's reported 4.71 — clearly don't reflect how those players actually played. But argues the other three forms of validity hold up: *face validity* (the fastest-40 leaderboard — Tyreek Hill, Chris Johnson, Bo Jackson — matches obvious NFL speed), *content validity* (the 40 isn't meant to be a standalone speed measure; combined with the other combine drills it captures the fuller concept), and *criterion validity* (predictive value for NFL success), which he says is well established independent of construct-validity concerns, and has been since the Combine's founding by the Cowboys in the 1960s specifically to standardize athletic measurement across schools. Distinguishes random error (washes out with more trials/pro days) from systematic error (bad surface, weather) as the more genuine risk. Notes the financial stakes are asymmetric to how a GM's office should actually value the noise: since the draft-pick salary pool is fixed, one class of players losing value because of test-day variance means another class is gaining it.
- 2024-03-01 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode: separately from the timing-error argument, frames good scouting as identifying *where* a player's speed shows up rather than trusting a single measurable — endorses RAS-style compiled athletic scores as a useful distillation but says the real skill is knowing where a player wins and loses on tape. Cites (as retired, non-current examples, not evaluated for present-day fantasy relevance) a player whose timed speed never translated to on-field value, versus one whose modest timed speed came with enough quickness and decision-making to start productively in the NFL regardless.

- 2024-03-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and **Bob Harris** (co-host — not a tracked expert) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-04]]), *Feel It or F**k It*: both "feel it" on prospects skipping combine drills — most drills "teach to the test" rather than reflect real football skill (the broad jump and gauntlet drill singled out as low football-specificity examples), and endorse [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]]'s stated reasoning for opting out. Also reads [[Caleb Williams]] withholding his medicals from the ~31 teams that won't draft him as a rational use of increased pre-draft player leverage, not an evasion — "he's made a lot of money playing college football... he doesn't necessarily need to play the game."
- 2024-03-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode: extends the skepticism to workout numbers that come in *faster* than tape projected, using several 2024 RB prospects as examples — [[Ray Davis]]'s 4.52 fell right inside the 4.5–4.55 range Waldman had already projected for him; [[Trey Benson]] and [[Kimani Vidal]] (whom Waldman has as a top-10 RB) both surprised him; Isaac Guerendo's time "surprised pretty much everybody." Lays out his process for handling an outlier time: don't automatically bump a player's tier off the stopwatch alone — go back to the tape looking for plays where that speed actually shows up decisively, and separately check whether a player who looks slow on tape despite a fast time is a slow decision-maker rather than a slow athlete. Rejects moving a player up a tier without being able to point to specific evidence — "it's kind of like giving an employee a promotion because he wore a nice tie."

- 2024-04-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-04]]): flags a "weird reckoning" between GPS-tracked in-game/drill speed and traditional stopwatch 40 times, using [[Keon Coleman]] as the live example — he ran a slow 4.61 40 at his pro day but was reportedly the fastest receiver through the combine's GPS-timed gauntlet drill. Notes the same split showed up with [[Puka Nacua]] (fast GPS speed, slower 40) and [[Cooper Kupp]] (a slow Senior Bowl 40 paired with a high GPS top speed) — "it's two very different things... being fast doing wide receiver things" versus "running a track drill." Adds that there's no public database for GPS/tracking speed the way there is for 40 times, which makes it hard for outside analysts to weigh consistently.
- 2024-04-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-18]]): Testing translated to tape for Xavier Legette (4.39 speed visibly shows up on film) but was more ambiguous for Troy Franklin, who ran a merely average 4.41 for his 176-lb frame yet 'plays faster than the watch' on film — Harmon's takeaway is that testing numbers need film verification in either direction, not just to confirm elite marks but also to override underwhelming ones.
- 2024-04-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-23 (Evaluation vs. Valuation)]]): Waldman warns against combine and box-score numbers overriding tape evaluation. He cites BYU tackle Kingsley Suamataia as an example — a strong 40 time masked clear footwork issues on tape, particularly his recovery once he's leaning outside and needs to reset against speed-to-power edge rushers, which Waldman thinks makes him a late-second/early-third-round value rather than a first-rounder despite testing well. He raises a parallel caution about simple counting stats, using edge rusher Chop Robinson's low sack total as an example of a number that lacks role context — Robinson mostly played the side of the line where right-handed quarterbacks can see him step up and unload, which suppresses sack counts independent of pass-rush ability. In both cases Waldman argues raw workout or box-score numbers need tape context before they should move a valuation.
- 2024-11-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-18]]): Waldman used Sean Payton's admission that Denver 'didn't know how athletic' [[Bo Nix]] was to make the inverse point about testing. Nix did not run or do movement work at the combine or his pro day because of turf toe, so the workout data did not exist — but Waldman's response was to ask whether the Broncos listened to their own scouts, because the athleticism was plainly visible on tape in how Nix moved and ran around the pocket. His conclusion: the absence of a workout number should never be read as the absence of the trait, and a team treating it that way is choosing testing over film.
- 2024-12-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-12]]): [[DK Metcalf]] is the standing rebuttal to translating combine drills to the field — the pre-draft take from football Twitter, Harmon included in the self-deprecation, was that Metcalf's three-cone time proved he was stiff and could not move off the line of scrimmage. On tape and against press he has always shown that lateral quickness, a point guest Patrick Peterson (former NFL cornerback, not a tracked expert) confirmed from having covered him: Metcalf has legitimate releases and clearly worked at the release skill that most big receivers lack.
- 2025-01-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-23]]): Waldman describes the combine's role in his process as targeted cross-checking, not as a source of grades. He watches six to eight games per player first, scores each into a range, and deliberately holds him at the bottom of that range until there is definitive proof to move up; going in, he notes 20 to 30 of the 67 receivers he has watched have at least one and as many as three things that could dramatically change their score. Combine work exists to spot-check those specific pre-written questions — whether [[Andrew Armstrong]] shows the explosion that was on tape before his hamstring injury, whether [[Konata Mumpfield]] runs 4.55 or better, whether [[Nick Nash]] and that tier can run fast enough to be more than what they are. He gives allowances for players timing faster than they looked, which is what would move his count of top-12-caliber 2025 receivers from three to five or six.
- 2025-02-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-24]]): recording pre-combine, Waldman says he deliberately underestimates athletic ability until he sees testing — 'every score I see, I basically shave', adding time to forty estimates and discounting huge vertical leaps — and guest Dwain McFarland (Fantasy Life, NOT tracked) says he does exactly the same. Waldman's framing: both are optimists in life and pessimists about this. McFarland adds that athleticism barely factors into his wide receiver model at all, matters a little for running backs, and matters most at tight end, which is why combine results could move [[Harold Fannin Jr.]] more than most. Concrete pre-combine estimates they put on record: 4.45 for [[TreVeyon Henderson]], 4.6 at 232 pounds for [[Damien Martinez]], and a 4.5-versus-4.6 make-or-break for [[Konata Mumpfield]].
- 2025-02-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-26]]), reporting live from Indianapolis: the combine is 'basically canceled' for headliners — Shedeur Sanders was not throwing, [[Ashton Jeanty]] was not working out, and [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] had skipped drills the prior year. Both hosts accepted the players' logic (only downside if you fail to measure up; player empowerment first) while calling it a lost information opportunity for outside evaluators. Harmon argued there are exceptions worth taking: a placement-and-accuracy quarterback like Sanders, whose knock is a merely adequate arm, could benefit the way [[C.J. Stroud]] did by throwing in a class where evaluators are splitting hairs, and he wanted Jeanty in pass-catching drills to answer whether he can catch. Harmon also said quarterback throwing sessions yield little because you are throwing to unfamiliar receivers, but that running back, receiver and defensive drills still carry real signal. Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) self-identified as 'a combine bro' who loves the measurements; Harmon recalled that watching [[Bijan Robinson]] work out, despite having nothing to prove, was itself clarifying — 'that guy's different'.
- 2025-03-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-03]]): Waldman still 'feels' the Combine, but not for the reasons people think — its value is the team interviews, the medicals, and specific drills a coaching staff wants a particular player put through, plus the handful of players who genuinely need to show something. For prospects whose draft range is already cemented within two rounds, he says it's 'f**k it,' and points out that among running backs barely anyone ran the 20-yard shuttle or three-cone, which he considers more valuable than the 40. Beyond that he sees a glossy marketing circus for the NFL and NFL draft media. He endorsed Cecil Lammey's line (Lammey is not a tracked expert) that with every GM and coach taking the podium, the Combine is the last truth before 'the season of lies' begins — noting that several 2025 podium statements came true within hours or days ([[Travis Kelce]]'s return, the [[Deebo Samuel]] trade). His standing reminder: every team has exactly one first-round pick, so 30 players linked to 32 teams is mostly smoke. Bob Harris (not tracked) added the counterweight that a narrow Combine impression can still be a useful lead worth following up — his own catch being [[Mason Taylor]] in the gauntlet drill.
- 2025-03-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-07]]): Harmon flags how quickly one testing number reorders a board. He did not see 4.29 speed on [[Matthew Golden]]'s tape, Golden ran it anyway, and 'now it's like Matthew Golden could be the top receiver drafted this year — it's just what happens after the 40 time.' Harmon says he loves the 40 but refuses the conclusion: Golden is 'not the top receiver in this class, not even close', while still being a first-round player. He applies the same doubt in advance to [[Emeka Egbuka]], reported by Daniel Jeremiah (not a tracked expert) to be running 4.3 at his pro day, saying 'I don't see 4.3 on the tape' — while acknowledging he was wrong about Golden's speed, so the skepticism is about the ranking consequences, not the timing itself.
- 2025-03-10 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-10]]): three applications of testing skepticism in one episode. On [[Harold Fannin Jr.]], the market jumped off after a roughly 4.71 forty when it expected 4.5-4.6, but Waldman argued the 20-yard shuttle and three-cone matter more for that archetype and Fannin's are nearly identical to [[Sam LaPorta]] — so he moved up while others moved down. On [[Andrew Armstrong]], he praised a receiver who declined to test at all on the stated grounds of becoming a better receiver rather than performing to a test. On [[Thomas Fidone]], he treated a 15-pound cut to 241 pounds for testing as ambiguous evidence — possibly weight made for the test and regained, possibly a real decision about knees and role — rather than a clean data point.
- 2025-03-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-18]]): Waldman lays out his tiering method as an antidote to raw workout comparison. Drill results are graded against baseline tiers — a 'committee' tier meaning good enough to see the field if schemed around, and a 'starter' tier meaning the offense can run as-is — so the question is which tier a number lands in, not whose number is bigger. On that basis [[Harold Fannin Jr.]]'s 4.71 forty, a hundredth outside starter tier, is effectively starter tier, and his shuttle, three-cone and vertical sit in the same tiers as [[Sam LaPorta]]'s despite the raw gaps. Waldman says the 40 result 'shut down a lot of the pre-draft love for Fannin in the public eye' and should not have. The second half of the method is fit: compare the data to the player's style of play and the demands the game will place on him. His example is [[Dalvin Cook]], whose metrics fell below baseline in areas the drills measured, but whose curvy, linear cutting style none of those drills actually captured — grading him on them, he says, is like grading a pastry chef on how well he cooks a steak.
- 2025-04-02 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-02]]): the annual weigh-in obsession is 'exhausting' because playing weight is a moving target — a prospect's combine number is one measurement on one day, taken while he may be cutting for a 40 time, off the weight room, or unable to lift through an injury, and yet it defines him 'for the rest of his life.' Harmon cited [[Marquise Brown]], who is not the 166 pounds he weighed at the combine, and argued modern receivers are simply leaner than they used to be — [[Justin Jefferson]] and [[CeeDee Lamb]] are slight-framed and nobody suggests they need to beef up. He also noted top-five prospects increasingly do not test at all ([[Ashton Jeanty]] cited), so he expects no athletic testing on [[Travis Hunter]] and does not need it. Koh (not tracked) partly dissented: he agreed the number is overrated for projecting play, but held that frame matters for durability if Hunter is to play both ways at sub-180.
- 2025-04-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-18]]): a 4.80 pro-day forty from a 5'10", 210-pound slot is close to disqualifying — he cannot name a successful NFL receiver at that time, citing Jarvis Landry in the 4.7s (also reportedly injured), Keenan Allen at 4.7 and [[Cooper Kupp]] around 4.6. His cautionary case is Rashard Higgins, whose film and Reception Perception profile he loved but whose 18th-percentile broad, 12th-percentile vertical, 7th-percentile 10-yard split and 9th-percentile forty effectively disqualified him as an NFL athlete. Harmon still gives [[Xavier Restrepo]] partial benefit of the doubt on a hamstring that tightened pre-run, saying the tape does not look 4.8, while co-host James Koh (not tracked) argues you cannot be that productive in college on tight-end speed — noting [[Harold Fannin Jr.]] led FBS with 1,555 yards on a 4.7. Neither buys the mid-4.4s claim; both land around 4.5-4.6.
