# Scouting Bias and Player Archetypes

## Definition

The tendency to assign players narrative archetypes ("gritty technician",
"raw athlete") that track appearance and pedigree rather than actual measured
traits — and the evaluation errors that follow.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-14]]): argues these archetype labels are frequently applied backwards, and that they are visibly correlated with player appearance rather than play. His thought experiment: obscure every receiver head-to-toe at the Senior Bowl and have people identify them from movement alone — he expects the standard characterizations would invert. Cites Jordy Nelson (4.51 speed, routinely described as a possession/technique receiver) as a case where the label didn't match the athletic profile.

- 2023-12-14 — **Adam Harstad** (guest — not a tracked expert), same episode: gives the cleanest example — the reputations of Stefon Diggs and [[Adam Thielen]] in Minnesota were effectively swapped. "The player everybody thought Thielen was, that's who Diggs was, and the player everybody thought Diggs was, that's who Thielen was." Diggs was cast as the raw athletic YAC machine and Thielen as the gritty precise route-runner; both were closer to the reverse.

- 2023-12-18 — **Bob Harris** (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]: a first-person account of the box-score version of the same error. He had "dialed back" on [[Jordan Addison]] through a long stretch of poor fantasy output, and in doing so missed a real role change — Addison had moved up out of the No. 3 spot in the rotation. "I was just dialed back looking at the outcomes and not looking at what was going on on the field... he's very good, eyeball test."

- 2023-12-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]): extends the "label doesn't match the trait" point to speed specifically — "you don't always have to have blazing speed to be a top wide receiver." Names [[Keenan Allen]] and [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] as top-5 receivers whose value comes from winning against both man and zone and from inside/outside versatility, not athletic testing. See [[Zone vs Man Route Running]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Adam Harstad** (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21]]: the archetype penalty is era-dependent, not fixed. In a talent-centric league — which he argues is where the NFL sat in December 2023 — teams build around what a player can do, which produces "a diversity of builds that's greater, less of a homogenization at certain positions... a good time to be an atypical running back, or an atypical anything." In a scheme-centric league the same players get told "we're doing what we're doing, I don't care what you can do." His casualty list from the previous scheme-centric stretch: Percy Harvin, LaMichael James, Colin Kaepernick, Pat White, Vernon Davis. Practical version: when the label "he doesn't have a position" is priced into a prospect, check which way the league is currently swinging. See [[Scheme vs Talent]] and [[League Trend Cycles and Market Inefficiency]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Harstad**, same episode, the reverse error: after [[Derrick Henry]] broke out, the archetype ("big powerful back") was treated as the transferable thing, when the player was "a one of one." Extrapolating a body type from one outlier is the same category mistake as extrapolating a scouting label from an appearance.

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21]]), on his own process: describes rebuilding his wide receiver evaluation system after the Hakeem Butler miss (he had Butler graded near Julio Jones), spending a couple of years studying wide receiver coach Drew Lieberman's instructional work to get more granular about which traits to prioritize. His stated position on misses: "don't worry about your misses, worry about just continuing to get better... they may not turn out to be misses after just one year or two years." Notable because his critics cite one or two named misses while he says he could name more himself.

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]: the cleanest statement of the bias in the wiki so far, on [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] — "I don't think he's as good of a player as he is a prospect." His complaint is about the aura rather than the evaluation: people discuss Harrison "as if he is already there," which prices a projection as a fact. [[Matt Waldman]]'s parallel framing, same episode: his criticisms are "concern relative to the buzz people are giving," not concerns in absolute terms. See [[Prospect Pro-Readiness vs Ceiling]].

- 2024-02-01 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-01]]): a program-level version of the archetype argument, applied to [[Drake Maye]] — North Carolina's system has produced a specific quarterback archetype across multiple recruiting classes (Mitch Trubisky, Sam Howell, Maye — "not too far apart on the same wavelength"), and that archetype/brand of football "has not been successful in the NFL for long periods of time," independent of any one player's individual talent grade. Distinct from the usual version of this bias (mislabeling a player by appearance) — here the label risk is structural, attached to the system a prospect came from rather than to how he looks.

- 2024-02-22 — [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-22]] [Dynasty]: applied to ball security specifically, as a caution against over-weighting any single measurable trait when projecting 2024 running back prospects [[Rasheen Ali]] and [[Marshawn Lloyd]]. Angelo's framing: ball security is close to the least predictable trait to project forward, because the outcome doesn't track consistently with the college profile — Sony Michel (not tracked) had bad ball security at Georgia and it was fine as a pro, while Ameer Abdullah's (not tracked) was fine in college and then became a real issue once he got to the league ("got into his head"). The counter-cases, where poor ball security did sink a talented back's career, are David Wilson and Stevan Ridley (neither tracked). Angelo's rule: "you don't want to overemphasize ball security, but at the same time you don't want to throw it away either." Same conversation, a distinct instance of the underlying archetype-driven-perception error: Angelo's read on [[Marshawn Lloyd]] is that public perception of him has been inflated by a handful of highlight Caleb Williams option-pitch plays in open space, without a separate look at what the fuller tape shows about his third-down role, decision-making maturity, and ball security.

- 2024-03-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-04]]): argues the recurring "next Patrick Mahomes" media label — previously hung on Zach Wilson and Drew Lock (neither tracked), now on [[Caleb Williams]] — is less about actual play-style similarity and more a way for analysts who missed on Mahomes pre-draft to avoid admitting they were simply wrong. Says Williams's game reads closer to an "aspiring Aaron Rodgers." Backs the underlying claim with an inside-scouting anecdote: Chiefs scouts (including Mark Schofield) were reportedly "giggling like school kids" watching Mahomes dominate scout-team reps as a rookie behind starter Alex Smith — evidence the team knew he was special from day one, "not because Andy Reid coached him up."

- 2024-03-08 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-08]]): applies the archetype-bias framework to age-based scouting filters, using [[Bo Nix]] and [[Michael Penix Jr.]] as the worked examples — both knocked in media analysis for being older, more experienced prospects. Compares blanket age discounting to "that ill-advised wide receiver model from a decade ago," where an analytics-minded team filtered out every receiver under 6'2/210 — a filter that would have excluded Isaac Bruce, Steve Smith, Antonio Brown and [[Stefon Diggs]]. His position: "the game slows down for older players when they earn playing experience" is a valid theory, but scoring age as a variable in practice is "more ham-handed than football personnel evaluators at an all-you-can-eat pig fest," because it doesn't specify which skills actually improve with experience, or whether they improve with positive experience, negative experience, or both. Self-awareness specifically, he argues, tracks a player's skill and desire more than his age or reps — "raw and inexperienced" and "raw and unskilled" get treated as synonyms when they describe very different players (his contrast case for true rawness: Malik Willis, not tracked in this wiki).

- 2024-03-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-21]]): applied to [[Marquise Brown]]'s KC signing — "I don't want to typecast guys because of size... I don't want to assume, oh, this size, whatever, like he won't be able to do this." Notes size does show up in Brown's game in specific, measurable ways (broken down on first contact often, below-65% success rate vs. press every charted season) rather than as a blanket verdict on his ability, distinguishing a size-driven trait limitation from a size-driven archetype label.

- 2024-03-28 — [[Matt Waldman]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-28 (Going Deep - If We Were NFL GMs)]]): applies the hyper-athletic-bust pattern to two 2024 "do not draft" receivers, [[Adonai Mitchell]] and [[Devontez Walker]] — elite testers whose lack of consistent route intent/detail is the actual risk, not their athletic grade. Historical precedent cited: Denzel Mims and Terrace Marshall Jr. (neither tracked), hyper-athletic receivers who never developed a mature enough NFL skill set. Waldman's framing: he can "go on for days" listing hyper-athletic receivers who haven't panned out for exactly this reason.

- 2024-04-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-04]]): applied to [[Keon Coleman]], names Jonathan Mingo and [[Treylon Burks]] as the "glaring examples" of the risk in the "poor college separator" archetype — a coaching staff sees a big, physical ball winner and simply plugs him in outside as an X, which historically has not worked for this profile; the path to hitting requires a staff willing to move the player into the slot instead (his historical hit-rate study above formalizes this). Also makes a broader point about outcome-based "binary right/wrong" draft analysis being misleading — his lower-than-consensus pre-draft grade on Burks looked "right" in hindsight only because Tennessee played him at X; the same evaluation could have looked equally sound with Burks miscast correctly as a slot receiver and succeeding, since the underlying analysis (limited separator, needs a slot role) would have been identical either way.

- 2024-04-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host — not a tracked expert) on *Reception Perception: The Show* ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-11]]): applied to [[Adonai Mitchell]]'s "lazy"/loafing narrative — Harmon pushes back hard, arguing the whole Texas receiver corps (including [[Xavier Worthy]]) jogs through some routes on film, so singling Mitchell out reads more like a personality-driven narrative (he gave a candid, non-standard combine answer about pacing himself across drives, and some teams reportedly have personality concerns) than an actual effort/talent signal. Also flags a distinct, geography-based version of the same bias: a "Big 12 discount" some evaluators apply to receiver production/success rates from Texas/TCU/Oklahoma-style offenses — Harmon is sympathetic to that discount given how often those offenses feature stripped-down route trees and jogged-through routes, but separately pushes back on applying an analogous "Pac-12 discount" to [[Rome Odunze]], calling that version "jumping the shark."

- 2024-04-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-11 (Going Deep - Fave Evals, Coach-Killer Candidates, and High-End Career Outcomes)]]): names a specific fantasy-community bias — treating every good WR2 as an underrated WR1. Applied to [[Tee Higgins]]: "players like T don't understand — they are a better Robin than a Batman, and that's totally okay... stop listening to the fantasy analyst telling you you're a wide receiver one." Ties it to a broader NFL scheme trend toward two-alpha receiver rooms (citing Brian Callahan's Titans core of Hopkins/Ridley) rather than one true X funneling all the volume — meaning "Robin"-type receivers are increasingly a legitimate, valuable archetype rather than a lesser one. Separately, [[Brandon Angelo]], same episode, names the underlying social-proof bias in his own [[Keon Coleman]]-to-Anquan Boldin comp: describes talking himself out of a take he can't fully justify with tape ("this is dumb, but I love it"), then framing it as the phenomenon where a "little brother" observation gets dismissed until a bigger-profile analyst says the same thing.

**Why this matters for drafting:** archetype labels leak into ADP. Where a
player's reputation and measured profile diverge, the reputation is often what
is priced — which is where value appears.
- 2024-04-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-23 (Evaluation vs. Valuation)]]): In an extended framework discussion with NFL.com's Chad Reuter (guest, not tracked), Waldman argues public draft grading over-indexes on outcome-based hindsight rather than what was actually knowable at the time of the pick — the go-to example being fans asking 'how could you take Jalen Reagor over Justin Jefferson' with no acknowledgment the two were evaluated as different archetypes at the time (Jefferson wasn't purely the slot receiver public perception assumed). He cites the Patriots under Bill Belichick as a cautionary data point, saying he has seen statistically significant data showing teams that reach well above a player's consensus valuation tier have a worse track record than teams that stay within tier — undercutting the popular 'just draft the guy you like regardless of slot' take. He stresses that grading picks fairly requires setting an evaluation baseline right after the draft, citing his own 2023 'C' grade for the Lions taking [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] and Jack Campbell — both above their consensus draft-board range — as good players who nonetheless represented a valuation reach at the time.
- 2024-04-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host, not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-25]]): discussed an Adam Schefter/ESPN research stat that only 27% of first-round receivers since 2000 have signed a second contract with the team that drafted them (vs. 92% for centers), calling receiver the most volatile first-round position and noting recent classes like 2016 and 2015 were largely flame-outs while 2014 hit. They flagged a flaw in the study -- a draft-night trade (e.g., a hypothetical [[Brandon Aiyuk]] deal) still counts as a "miss" even when the pick worked out for the drafting team. Separately, they discussed size-based typecasting risk: [[Xavier Legette]] and [[Johnny Wilson]] both risk being funneled into ill-fitting X-only or even tight-end roles because of frame alone, and used the Ted Ginn "deep-ball specialist" label to describe how [[Troy Franklin]] may get pigeonholed despite more nuanced route-running ability than that label implies.
- 2024-06-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-06-03]]): Waldman argued media evaluators apply a double standard to dual-threat quarterbacks, using [[Anthony Richardson]] as the example. Richardson takes criticism that he must 'evolve' and become a more careful pocket passer after taking hits, while [[Matthew Stafford]] absorbed comparable punishment and was simply called 'brittle' — never told he needed to become 'a more intellectual quarterback.' Waldman connected this to the broader tendency to slot mobile QBs like Richardson and [[Lamar Jackson]] into a lazy Cam Newton-style 'runs, can't throw, can't manage a pocket' archetype rather than evaluating their actual pocket management and processing on tape, which he says is strong for both.
- 2024-07-02 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-02]]): Harmon revisits the backlash to [[Rashee Rice]]'s poor SMU Reception Perception prospect grade (1st percentile versus man), noting that despite the ugly separation numbers Rice still produced 1,355 yards and 10 TDs in college -- exactly the gap RP is designed to expose between counting stats and actual route-winning ability. He argues Rice's translation wasn't a case of the profile being 'wrong,' but of Andy Reid recognizing the same underlying weaknesses and building a completely different, slot/screen-heavy role around them (only 10.3% of routes against press as a rookie versus 83.6% of snaps outside in college). Harmon frames this as a repeatable historical pattern -- non-collegiate separators like Juju Smith-Schuster, [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] and hopefully [[Keon Coleman]] who get moved inside and simplified in the NFL rather than forced into an X role they can't handle.
- 2024-07-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-04]]): discussing [[Javon Baker]]'s reported draft-board fall over character concerns tied to his father, the hosts argued evaluators with certain cultural biases tend to overreact to players who don't fit their expectations, drawing parallels to past unfounded character narratives around Colin Kaepernick, Jimmy Smith (wrongly flagged for medicating with marijuana), Patrick Mahomes' father, and Lamar Jackson's mother. They characterized the Baker situation as 'smoke, not fire' for now, noting he is a Day 3 pick without the standing to meaningfully disrupt a franchise even if the concerns are real.
- 2024-07-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-22]]): fantasy players get hooked on the speed guy who 'should somehow get more carries,' then abandon him at maximum speed when the carries don't come — his example is [[Tony Pollard]], beloved as the fast change-of-pace back until he became the guy defenses keyed on. He applies the same corrective to [[Joe Mixon]], whose worst-in-class breakaway rate is being used against him while the volume signal is ignored. The related bias runs the other way with [[Audric Estime]] — a back people over-project off college tape into an NFL archetype (A.J. Dillon) that has not been producing. Waldman's counter-frame is the all-around 'doubles and triples' runner: Mixon in the Ezekiel Elliott mold, and [[Deebo Samuel]] as the elite gadget archetype whose ceiling is capped precisely because he cannot win the tight-coverage moment.
- 2024-07-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-23]]): the episode surfaced an explicit size-bias disagreement. Koh describes himself as 'a big-time sizeist,' especially for outside receivers, and weights contested-catch ability heavily — which is why he questions [[Tank Dell]] ranking ahead of [[Tee Higgins]] and asks how much of Dell's production is downstream of [[Nico Collins]] drawing coverage. Harmon's counter-frame is transportability by archetype rather than size: he argues a [[Puka Nacua]] role does not exist on every offense, whereas nearly every offense has some form of boundary X, so what matters is whether a player's archetype maps onto the offense he is in. He applies the same lens to reject miscasting — [[Elijah Moore]] as a gadget create-a-touch player and [[Jerry Jeudy]] as a vertical slot are both, in his view, archetype errors by their coaching staffs — and to say Higgins needs a quarterback with arm strength and nerve to be maximized, a point Koh rejected outright by arguing Higgins elevates any offense league-wide.
- 2024-09-05 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-05 (Going Deep)]]): the NFL is catching on to slight-framed receivers, and the physics explain why — decelerating mass is easier when there is less mass to decelerate, and re-accelerating is easier too. That gives smaller receivers an edge in short-area separation, twitch, precision and re-acceleration after the catch, and lets them position themselves in ways a 205-to-210-pound receiver cannot. Angelo names [[Tez Johnson]], [[Tank Dell]], [[Greg Dortch]], [[DeVonta Smith]] and [[Jordan Addison]] as the archetype, arguing size is treated as a demerit when it is actually an advantageous tool, and that it is a main reason players like Dell stick and why Smith has posted near-1,000-yard, 100-target seasons. He ties the payoff to two integral situations: short-area separation and low-red-zone, high-leverage work.
