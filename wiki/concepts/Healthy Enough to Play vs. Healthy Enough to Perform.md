# Healthy Enough to Play vs. Healthy Enough to Perform

## Definition

A distinction between clearing the bar to suit up and clearing the bar to play
at the level a player was drafted/paid to play at. Most "healthy" comeback
seasons that disappoint are explained by this gap rather than by a lingering
injury being hidden from the public — the player passed his team's threshold
to play, not his own threshold to perform.

## Expert Takes

- 2024-01-08 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (a performance specialist who works with pro athletes, and a former Purdue running back), relayed by [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-08]]: the framework stated directly, applied to [[Tony Pollard]]'s disappointing 2023 — "there's being healthy enough to play, and that's what most guys are... but not healthy enough to play at the level that they were drafted to be." Waldman's application: Pollard's two significant injuries likely left him in the first category all season, which is a materially different — and more recoverable — problem than a talent decline.
- 2024-02-01 — [[Brandon Angelo]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-01]]: applies the same framework to pre-draft scouting, not just NFL comebacks — one of the specific things he watches for during Senior Bowl/Shrine Game week for players coming off injury is not just whether the talent still looks real, but "not only healthy, but can they make impact plays against impact players." Names a recently-ACL-recovered prospect who looked good in this year's process (a name too heavily garbled by the transcript's ASR to confidently resolve — see the source page's "Not given pages" note) and last year's [[Prospect Pro-Readiness vs Ceiling]]-adjacent comp, Tyjae Spears, as a positive precedent for the same test.
- 2024-05-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-06]]): Two 2024 injury cases illustrate the gap between being active and being effective -- [[Kirk Cousins]] may be on the field Week 1 without the leg torque needed for full accuracy/velocity on his throwing motion, and [[Nick Chubb]] may be 'available' per the Browns' vague messaging without being the same explosive back given the severity and rarity of a second same-knee three-ligament tear.
- 2024-07-04 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-04]]): asked about big, boundary-dominant receivers like [[Tee Higgins]] and [[Mike Williams]], Angelo argued larger-framed players generally carry more mass and force through their joints and tendons and are more prone to soft-tissue injury than smaller receivers such as [[DeVonta Smith]], though he cautioned that prior injury history and movement fluidity matter as much as raw size. He cited a colleague's old prediction that [[DK Metcalf]] would eventually 'run himself right out of his ligaments' given his size, noting Metcalf has so far avoided that outcome, and stressed that practice-load management (e.g., limiting daily running mileage) is now central to how teams try to keep bigger receivers healthy.
- 2024-07-12 — Discussing Tony Pollard's disappointing 2023, [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-12]]) raised Brandon Angelo's framework distinguishing 'healthy enough to play' (able to be on the field and trusted to execute) from 'healthy enough to perform' (fully back to pre-injury form) as a possible explanation for Pollard's down year, in a conversation with guest JJ Zachariason.
- 2024-07-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-25]]): the habit of treating a return to the field as a return to form is one of the biggest analytical errors in receiver evaluation. His two examples are [[Michael Gallup]], who was a high-end number two before his 2020 ACL tear and, per anyone embedded with the Cowboys, never recovered his confidence — retiring in July 2024 — and [[Rashad Bateman]], who told reporters after the season that he had not expected to play at all in 2023 and considered being on the field a miracle. Harmon applies the same lens to [[Chris Godwin]], whose success rate vs man fell to 66.9% and vs press to 61.5% in 2022, his first year back from an ACL, from marks above 72% and 76% respectively before it — hoping, but not assuming, the pre-injury version is still available.
- 2024-07-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-29 (Feel It or F-It)]]): camp rehab milestones are not evidence of performance readiness. For [[Nick Chubb]], straight-line running and cone drills say little; the test is scrimmage jump cuts, pressure cuts and absorbing the two or three knee-bending hits a game, and for a knee like Chubb's the contact threshold that ends a season may be far lower than for a healthy player. For [[Kirk Cousins]] he restates [[Brandon Angelo]]'s Going Deep point that a 36-year-old post-Achilles may be cleared to play yet unable to drive off the foot for the velocity his route tree demands — and warns Cousins's tough-guy instinct could keep him on the field while failing. Bob Harris (not tracked) offers Steve Smith's still-visibly-smaller repaired calf years later as evidence these recoveries run longer than clearance dates suggest.
- 2024-08-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-05]]): Waldman applied this framework to both sides of the Rams receiver question — the returning [[Cooper Kupp]] may not have been fully himself when he came back, and a [[Puka Nacua]] cleared from a knee tweak is not automatically the player who earned 13 targets a game in September. His mechanism: coaches want players back on the field to be assignment-sound and available for the layups, which is not the same as being able to win at the level their talent implies. He extended the same logic to conceptual readiness rather than health, citing [[Ricky Pearsall]] in San Francisco — more than enough athlete to be a top receiver, but reportedly not separating on Chris Conley or Juan Jennings because he does not yet know the offense.
- 2024-08-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-12]]): he applies the distinction directly to [[Puka Nacua]] and [[Cooper Kupp]] — being healthy enough to be on the field and execute assignments is not the same as being healthy enough to produce at your top level of athletic skill, and he says the community is only starting to get hip to that. Even with Nacua possibly back Week 1, he projects a minimum two-to-four-week window, potentially longer, in which Nacua is not quite there, and treats that window as a concrete reason to raise the healthy teammate rather than to fade the injured one permanently. He raises a related durability version of the idea with [[Jerry Jeudy]], whom he simply does not trust to stay on the field.
- 2024-08-22 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-22]]): with Achilles injuries specifically, in his experience confidence returns around the 18-to-24-month mark — [[Kirk Cousins]] is not even at 12 months, and [[Aaron Rodgers]] practice clips still show a favored left leg. The performance question is not availability but whether a body that is short of 100 percent at game speed degrades drop-back depth, progression reads, time to throw and compensation mechanics. [[Matt Waldman]] applies a parallel version to running backs, citing [[J.K. Dobbins]] admitting he had returned before he was fully there and only realizing it once he started playing, and to [[Jayden Daniels]], where taking hits as a runner threatens games played even if per-game production is high — which is why Waldman prefers [[Bo Nix]]'s 17 starts over Daniels' higher average.
- 2024-08-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-26]]): the [[Nick Chubb]] discussion sharpens the distinction between rehab milestones and restored athleticism. Relaying a Senior Bowl conversation with Gene Bramwell, Waldman explains that sewing three ligaments in one knee to exactly the right tension is very difficult, and that most athletes end up too loose or too tight and never fully trust the knee when they have to perform at a high athletic level — and that doing the surgery a second time on the same knee is materially harder than a clean first repair. He contrasts this with Adrian Peterson, whose surgeon described opening the knee to find 'the ligaments of an infant' in a straightforward procedure. His practical rule: video of a player lifting proves strength, not dynamic movement, so treat return-to-work content as insufficient evidence and prefer to be wrong on the side of not paying.
- 2024-09-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-19]]): Harmon pushes back on the annual habit of asking why a player is not producing while ignoring that he is either playing through or returning from injury. His anchor case is [[Tony Pollard]], who told Harmon at the Super Bowl that he did not feel like himself until Week 11 — and Harmon's point is not the box score after Week 11 but how the player feels physically and mentally. He applies the same patience to [[Tank Dell]], who is back from a serious rookie-year injury and was also shot in the offseason, and to [[DeAndre Hopkins]] playing on a torn MCL: being active and taking three full practices is not the same as being right. Running routes on Instagram, he adds, is different from playing football.
- 2024-10-10 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-10]]): the public conversation fixates on joints — knee, hip, ankle — and lumps hamstrings in as soft tissue that lingers but is not serious, which is why [[Rashee Rice]]'s hamstring tendon repair was treated as a footnote to the LCL. Angelo's severity ladder runs Achilles rupture, patellar tendon rupture, then hamstring tendon rupture, with quad tendon nearby; he says he would take an ACL tear over a hamstring tendon rupture any day. The distinction he draws is between return to play and return to prior performance: an LCL is six months to play, eight to perform, while a hamstring tendon rupture can be 16-18 months to perform, with force production and the neurological recovery lagging longest. In fantasy terms, that means buyers should wait until a returning player has actually played a couple of games — the [[Tank Dell]] example this season — rather than paying for the return date.
- 2024-10-15 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-15]]): the recurring error with returning receivers is that as soon as a player is back on the field, everyone wants him to immediately be who he was — but it often takes a full year, sometimes more, to get the old traits back. He applies it two ways in the same episode: [[Chris Godwin]] saw his Reception Perception data against man coverage dip in his first year back from the ACL and is only now showing both the man-beating and the yards-after-catch elements again, while [[Tank Dell]] is still averaging under 10 yards per catch and is 'not even close' to last year's explosiveness despite a touchdown that had people chirping at Harmon on Twitter. The lesson he draws is to grade the traits on film rather than the box score in a returning player's first year back.
- 2024-10-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-21 (Feel It or Fk It 10.21.24)]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: two ramp-up warnings in one episode. On [[Christian McCaffrey]], even granting a possible Week 10 return, 'we don't know what his role will be when he comes back or how up to speed he'll be. Remember, we've seen players at wide receiver who didn't get a full month of August take a little bit of time to get up to speed. Christian McCaffrey hasn't been on a field since July' — so temper the league-winner expectation. On [[Nick Chubb]], he refused to say Chubb is back off one game: 'we need to see more touches. This was more of getting his feet wet, getting him acclimated, see how his body responds to game conditions and how well he recovers.' The shared principle is that the return date is the start of the evaluation, not the end of it.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31]]): [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] is the live example — two weeks from a planned IR placement to starting, and on tape Angelo sees a player who cannot come back to the ball, cannot keep his feet in routes and looks like he is running in mud. The lesson for fantasy managers is that an active designation says nothing about capability; Waldman traces the cause to a win-now organizational mandate (a weak AFC South, [[Nico Collins]] and [[Stefon Diggs]] hurt in Houston, Jacksonville and Tennessee in disarray) that pushes hurt starters onto the field and then blames the quarterback for the resulting passing game. Angelo extends the same logic to [[Audric Estime]] in Denver — two significant injuries in twelve months make it genuinely unclear whether he reaches his developmental peak.
- 2024-12-02 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-02]]): the length of the absence, not just the injury label, predicts how quickly a returning player performs. Discussing [[Isiah Pacheco]] returning after roughly nine weeks and immediately breaking a 20-plus-yard run, Waldman contrasts that with players out closer to nine months or a full year — [[Kirk Cousins]], [[Aaron Rodgers]], [[Tony Pollard]] (still affected in Week 10 by a January injury), [[Christian McCaffrey]] — who come back visibly slower. His framing: a shorter absence usually implies a less serious injury and a shorter rehab, so a faster ramp back is reasonable to expect, while the open question with any returning player is how quickly he recovers between games. Bob Harris (not tracked) raised the time factor and agreed it explained the divergence.
- 2025-02-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-04]]): [[Tank Dell]] is the case study. Harmon's in-season Reception Perception charting showed Dell's 2024 profile was 'not kind', worst early in the year and only partly recovered late, and he says the rookie-year player never reappeared even once Dell was cleared — so being on the field told you almost nothing about being productive. His planning rule for a receiver two devastating lower-body injuries deep is to treat any production as gravy rather than build the offense around it, and he would not be surprised if Dell misses all of 2025. Koh (not tracked) frames the same point as a question of which version comes off PUP.
- 2025-04-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-28 (Feel It or F-It Post-Draft)]]): his heuristic is procedure-based — where there is an established procedure with observable success rates, feel better about a return to form; where there is not, be far more cautious. His cautionary case is Kirk Cousins, who was declared ready while critics called the [[Michael Penix Jr.]] pick foolish, but whose Achilles was not fully healed: Atlanta over-protected him, he could not throw with velocity, and they relied on his decision-making instead. [[Brandon Angelo]] called that in advance and has issued the same warning on [[Rashee Rice]]. The optimistic counterpart, via Gene Bramble: an isolated ACL tear with no meniscus, tendon or other ligament damage is a long recovery, but the knee returns to what it was even after multiple tears — which is how Waldman reads [[Kurtis Rourke]]. He applies the same procedure-precedent logic to [[Isiah Pacheco]] via [[Tony Pollard]]'s week-10 timeline.
- 2025-05-15 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-15]]): he names the three parameters that decide whether a post-surgical player holds up — volume over time, contact over time and fatigue over time — and says medical teams optimize for returning a player to the field, not for all-pro performance in week 16. Rehab highlight clips are 'garbage' evidence because they test none of the three: these are not track athletes, and the surgical site gets angry and the neurological component unwinds only under repeated contact with fatigue. He applies it to [[Rashee Rice]], who he expects to look great in weeks one and two with week 10 the real question, and to the rehab videos of [[Stefon Diggs]]. His completed precedent is [[Kirk Cousins]] in 2024: not that Cousins could no longer play, but that he could not do it in a repeat fashion, so his efficiency collapsed and he was benched for [[Michael Penix Jr.]]. The decisive variable is whether the team affords the player the bandwidth to be integrated slowly — and Angelo says for most athletes the answer is no.
- 2025-06-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-09]]): the sharpest statement of this idea yet, relayed from [[Brandon Angelo]], his [[Going Deep]] co-host and a trainer — shooting speed and workout videos is one thing; absorbing the equivalent of someone taking an aluminum bat to your knee and leg for 16 weeks is another, and a limb only recently rebuilt is not necessarily structurally sound enough to handle it. Waldman applied it to fade [[Rashee Rice]] at WR20 despite clean OTA reports and [[Patrick Mahomes]]'s praise. He applied a related availability lens to [[Marquise Brown]], insisting it is not about predicting injury but predicting availability, and Brown has been 'predictably unavailable'. The inverse case was [[Isiah Pacheco]], where Waldman treated a documented return to combine testing numbers, 4.37 at 216 pounds, as real evidence after the Chiefs limited him to about five carries a playoff game post-injury.
- 2025-07-10 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-10]]): a boot in July means one of two things — protecting a surgical site, or a stress-related lower-limb injury — and organizational silence about the reason points to surgery. He applies it to [[Joe Mixon]], who likely rushed back from a high ankle sprain in three or four weeks on a six-to-eight-week injury and then needed offseason ligament stabilization, and warns that rushing back can do more harm than good, invoking [[David Montgomery]]'s playoff return as the same pattern. Injury layered on top of an age-29 or 30 running back is what removes the ceiling. Angelo also applies the framework to [[Colston Loveland]]'s grade-three AC joint dislocation: no contact until camp, then a ramp, so no 100% until perhaps midseason. [[Matt Waldman]] adds the reporting tell — 'should be ready by the first week of camp' regularly becomes PUP and then weeks more, as with [[Christian McCaffrey]] in 2024.
- 2025-07-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-21]]): the distinction to hold onto with long-rehab players is that being fit enough to run fast in a straight line now says nothing about whether a player has healed enough to absorb weekly punishment and regain burst and speed each week — which is why players so often say a year to eighteen months later that they were not truly ready at the time. He applies it to [[Stefon Diggs]] coming off PUP, and stacks it onto [[Rashee Rice]], citing [[Brandon Angelo]] — a trainer and his co-host on the RSP network's other show, who did similar work on [[Kirk Cousins]] and [[Tony Pollard]] — that Rice's knee was a more difficult issue than was publicly let on. Practical consequence: news that a player is 'ready' inflates public price without changing the real risk, so Waldman becomes less interested as the price rises.
- 2025-08-07 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-07]]): camp video of a returning player running routes at full speed tells you almost nothing, because the real test is contact — how the joint responds and how the player reacts psychosomatically when contact is imminent against people trying to hurt him, which practice cannot replicate. He argued you do not know until hits accumulate over a season, and cited [[Kirk Cousins]] in 2024 as the pattern: strong early, degrading as the hits piled up, with Atlanta course-correcting by not asking him to throw with velocity. A receiver gets no such course correction — 'your tree is your tree,' and a primary option's tree is expansive. He applied this to [[Rashee Rice]], warning of a possible strong three-to-five-game start followed by significant late-season degradation. Both hosts agreed no receiver is truly 100% in year one back from an ACL; [[Matt Waldman]] said Adrian Peterson is the only counterexample he can recall.
- 2025-08-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-18]]): a recurring theme of the episode — analysts have to get their heads around the fact that even superhuman athletes who train recovery year-round sometimes need longer to heal than expected, and that a full extra year is often the real timeline. He applied it three ways: [[Kirk Cousins]] regaining velocity a second year off the Achilles, which is why Waldman buys the improved practice reports; [[Breece Hall]], whose yards-per-carry and speed-per-carry downticks on analytics sites are explained by a knee injury he played through and by returning early from an ACL the year before; and older players generally, whom Waldman distinguishes from 'senior' players. Bob Harris (not tracked) endorsed the framework and said he would keep raising it on future shows, since it is where dynasty buy-low value comes from.
- 2025-08-22 — According to guest Dr. Deepak Chona, orthopedic sports surgeon of SportsMedAnalytics (not a tracked expert), on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-22]]: return date and return to production are two separate questions, and his model prices the gap explicitly as a ramp-up in target volume and consistency. A clean wide receiver ACL averages about 10 months with a four-game ramp ([[Stefon Diggs]], projected at 85 percent initially and mid-90s by mid-season); an ACL plus MCL runs about 11 months with a six-game ramp ([[Brandon Aiyuk]], roughly 90 percent only by the final third of the season, and a very up-and-down middle third); a fibula fracture with ankle ligament tears takes about 12 months to reach 90 percent ([[Chris Godwin]]). Inputs to the projection are the injury itself, pre-injury athleticism and production, and age. His practical conclusion is a draft-board one: the useful question is which week the player becomes trustworthy, not which week he is active.
- 2025-09-01 — According to Bob Harris (co-host, NOT a tracked expert) on ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-01]]): the tell is silence. Harris argues that a team saying a player is scheduled to practice means little until he is actually seen doing it, invoking [[Christian McCaffrey]] at the same point the previous year — good to go until he wasn't — as the pattern to watch for with [[De'Von Achane]]. [[Matt Waldman]] applied a related version to [[Breece Hall]]: a player can be healthy enough to be on the field but perceived as not playing hurt well, and coaching staffs sometimes act on that perception by shifting the job elsewhere, either ignorantly or knowingly.
- 2025-09-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-09]]): Harmon drew an explicit line between being play-ready and being performance-ready in the case of [[A.J. Brown]], who was not on the Week 1 injury report but missed most of training camp with a hamstring and only returned very late in the process. His charting showed rounded-off breaks, unsharp cuts and a 44% success rate versus man, and the go routes early in the game were run at a jog rather than full speed — which is itself the tell, since the hosts asked why you would ask a hamstring-compromised receiver to run nine routes at all unless he was a decoy. Harmon's questions coming out of the game were whether Brown should even be out there and whether the issue is physical or engagement.
- 2025-09-25 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-25]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: on [[Chris Godwin]]'s likely return, Harris warned against assuming activation equals production — he called the foot injury gruesome and disgusting, noted how long the recovery has taken and the question of whether a cleanup procedure was needed, and said nobody knows whether Godwin can be the same player. Guest Ben Gretch (Stealing Signals, not tracked) initially treated Godwin as an ancillary piece in game one back who would not run 90% of routes, then allowed that with [[Mike Evans]] hurt Tampa may scale him up quickly out of need, before conceding nobody knows usage beforehand. The disagreement mattered because Godwin's route share directly governs whether [[Cade Otten]] is startable, which was the point of the segment.
- 2025-09-29 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-29]]): several Week 4 cases where the availability signal and the performance signal came apart. [[Jaylen Warren]] was declared active-bound by Adam Schefter on Saturday and was seen warming up, then was inactive — Harris reads the warmup as evidence it was genuine, not shenanigans, and as a sign he plays after the bye. [[Puka Nacua]] walked off with trainers over a blocked-hand injury and was back running deep digs 30 seconds later. [[Davante Adams]]'s hamstring was invisible on tape. [[Stefon Diggs]] being sent downfield on man coverage reads to Harris as a better sign for his knee than any snap count. Guest Jamie Eisenberg (CBS, not a tracked expert) adds the contract angle: [[Jauan Jennings]] is playing through ankle, shoulder and rib issues almost certainly because his deal is tied to playing time, and might not be if he had guaranteed money. On [[Lamar Jackson]] both settle on part pout, part hamstring, with Eisenberg saying he hates being a Zapruder analyst about injuries.
- 2025-10-02 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-10-02]]): Harmon and co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) apply the distinction to [[Rashee Rice]], who by Week 5 had not played meaningful football in over a year between suspension and a knee injury. Koh's framing is that people are penciling in the exact 2024 player and should instead price a ramp-up period; Harmon says he has felt on an island for raising it all summer and that he is 'hoping' Rice is immediately the guy from 365 days ago but there is no guarantee. The precedent he leans on is [[Tank Dell]]'s 2024 return, where availability and reported health outran actual on-field effectiveness for weeks. Harmon adds a specific aggravator for Rice: he is a role-catered player, so the offense also has to rebuild the right usage around him. The one mitigator is that Rice gets six additional weeks of healing rather than dropping in at Week 1.
- 2026-08-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and Ian Hartitz (untracked co-host) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-08-03 (Feel It or Fk It with Ian Hartitz)]]): discussing [[Daniel Jones]]'s Achilles recovery, both invoked Dan Marino's stated concern for Kirk Cousins's own Achilles comeback -- a right-handed quarterback's plant foot is his power foot -- and Waldman detailed how Cousins, even once medically cleared, couldn't make throws beyond roughly 15 yards downfield and needed a pistol-heavy scheme without under-center snaps for an extended stretch, despite ultimately winning games. Both expect Jones's Colts offense to be more exposed than last year's hot 10-week start suggested, especially after Pittsburgh's blueprint of taking away the run and attacking Jones's coverage-reading limitations directly, and worry a compromised plant foot undercuts exactly the deep-ball element that made the Colts' passing game work early -- illustrating that clearance to play doesn't mean clearance to perform at a prior level.
- 2026-09-03 — According to [[Chris Harris]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-09-03]]): the hosts used the 49ers' 2026 camp -- multiple soft-tissue injuries to [[Mike Evans]], questions about whether [[Christian McCaffrey]] can stay right again, and Green Bay pushing [[Jayden Reed]] to play through an injury he probably didn't need to -- as evidence that a team's health outlook each season is closer to a coin flip than a plan, even for a well-coached roster. Waldman wondered whether it's bad luck or points to something about training methods; neither host had a confident answer.
