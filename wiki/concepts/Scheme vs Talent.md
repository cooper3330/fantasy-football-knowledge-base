# Scheme vs Talent

## Definition

How much of an offense's production belongs to the players and how much to the
system and play-caller around them — and therefore how much of a breakout to
price into a player when he changes teams, or when his coordinator does.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-18 — **Bob Harris** (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]: uses Houston as the test case. Case Keenum stepping in for [[C.J. Stroud]] and the offense still functioning — [[Noah Brown]] posting 8/89/1, [[Devin Singletary]] running for 120+ — suggests the engine may be the Shanahan-tree system that arrived with head coach DeMeco Ryans and offensive coordinator Bobby Slowik. He is explicit about not calling Stroud a system quarterback: "I'm just going to say there's a system that seems to be favorable to creating offense."

- 2023-12-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]): agrees it's multi-causal rather than either/or — the Shanahan scheme helps, there is good young surrounding talent, and there is a **novelty component**: "this is the AFC and they're taking a successful NFC scheme and putting it in there where a lot of the AFC teams haven't seen it enough, at least the way it's executed with little wrinkles." That last part implies some of the edge is temporary and decays as opponents get film.

- 2023-12-18 — Related application, same episode: both hosts treat the play-caller as a live variable in 2024 projections rather than a fixed input — [[Jahmyr Gibbs]]'s outlook depends on whether Ben Johnson stays in Detroit, and [[Bijan Robinson]]'s on whether Arthur Smith returns to Atlanta. **Bob Harris** reads Atlanta's 2023 backfield split as a coaching decision on a team that knew it wasn't good, not a verdict on the player.

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]: names the failure mode in one line — Atlanta is running "scheme over personnel," not personnel over scheme, and that is why a top-eight pick in [[Bijan Robinson]], "a rhythm runner who gets better with the more touches he gets," was splitting work with Cordarrelle Patterson. His generalization is about coaches rather than systems: "you get stubborn in terms of, well, my philosophy is going to be the same way because I coach in the NFL." Corroborates and sharpens Bob Harris's 12-18 reading of the same backfield. See [[Player Development and Coachability]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, a caution about the *player* side of the label, using [[Lamar Jackson]]: the popular framing that a player is "great without structure, bad within it" is usually wrong. "They're so good without structure that they don't need structure — it's not that they're not good in structure." Worth holding against the scheme-dependency calls elsewhere on this page: dependence on a system and inability to operate inside one are different claims.

### The league-wide swing (2023-12-21, *RSP Film and Theory*)

- 2023-12-21 — **Adam Harstad** (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21]]: the central framework of the episode — the NFL alternates between two modes. In a **talent-centric** swing, teams "find our players and then create something that best suits their specific skills"; in a **scheme-centric** swing, teams have "a dominant scheme that we know works and defenses haven't caught on to yet, so we're going to find players that fit that scheme." He reads the mid-1990s as scheme-centric (defenses hadn't solved the West Coast offense) and **December 2023 as firmly talent-centric**: "by and large, if you can do something good, somebody is going to find you and find a way to use you and put you on the field." His stated consequence is a wider diversity of builds and less homogenization by position — "a good time to be an atypical running back, or an atypical anything." This is the same cycle logic as [[League Trend Cycles and Market Inefficiency]], applied to players rather than fronts.

- 2023-12-21 — **Harstad**, same episode: names the candidate for the next scheme-centric swing — if teams work out how to copy the Kyle Shanahan offense, "then everybody's looking for a Deebo Samuel type and a George Kittle type, and we enter this scheme-focused period again." He is explicitly skeptical that it is copyable. Note this is the mirror image of the [[C.J. Stroud]] / Houston discussion three days earlier: novelty is the asset, and it decays.

- 2023-12-21 — **Harstad**, same episode, the fantasy-adjacent version: a talent swing lets players exist who previously got wasted. His comps for the current era's atypical producers are Percy Harvin (a "better Deebo Samuel" who could do everything Deebo does plus more, but never got the commitment), LaMichael James (an [[Austin Ekeler]] or [[James Cook]] archetype stuck in a big-back 49ers scheme), Colin Kaepernick and Pat White for the [[Lamar Jackson]] skill set, and Vernon Davis, whom San Francisco turned into "a sixth offensive lineman to keep Alex Smith alive." The shared cause is not player quality but offenses saying "we're doing what we're doing, I don't care what you can do."

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21]]): scheme dependence "shouldn't be considered a pejorative thing as much as it is just saying these are his skill sets. If you're smart, you're going to leverage them — and that's more on your organization than it is on your player." A scheme-dependent player in the right building can be more desirable than anyone. His two worked examples are [[Deebo Samuel]] and, contrarily, [[Lamar Jackson]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Harstad**, same episode, on the label itself: "I hate the system quarterback designation, partly because I don't think people know what they're saying when they talk about it" — it conflates *dependence on* a system with *benefiting from* one. His test: chart three games from a quarterback's career in three different years a half-decade apart and ask whose offense looks most alike. The answer is Peyton Manning, who "basically ran 12 plays from five formations for 18 years and told the NFL to stop him, and the NFL couldn't." By that measure Manning is the biggest system quarterback in league history and was plainly not system-*dependent* — which is Harstad's point that every quarterback is a system quarterback and the interesting question is a spectrum of creativity within the system, not a binary. He credits Cam Newton's viral segment from the prior week with having made essentially that point.

### A new year, two new cases (2024-01-01)

- 2024-01-01 — Both hosts on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-01]]: the Cleveland Browns as a fresh test case. Credit the 2023 turnaround under [[Joe Flacco]] mainly to offensive line coach Tom Cable, who built a functional run-blocking unit without [[Nick Chubb]] for most of the year — which in turn let head coach Kevin Stefanski finally run the pass-first, full-field-read offense he reportedly wanted all along. Line coaching and scheme fit rank above the individual quarterback in their telling, contrasted with Deshaun Watson's struggles in the same building.

- 2024-01-01 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode: [[Kyren Williams]] as the season's clearest scheme-over-talent running back. "Is he a top-10 talent at the running back position? Hell no. Is he worthy of top-10 production with the run game that they have? Absolutely." Credits the Rams' offensive line and receivers for opening creases, and treats the read as contingent on the coaching staff and line staying intact — see [[Kyren Williams]] for the stated risk (a free-agent power-back addition).

### The 2024 coaching carousel as a live test (2024-01-30)

- 2024-01-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-01-30]]: three separate coordinator hires treated as fresh scheme-vs-talent cases in the same episode. Atlanta hiring Zach Robinson (ex-Rams OC) is graded as a probable talent-unlock for [[Bijan Robinson]] and [[Drake London]], translating a scheme that already worked for comparable skill sets in Los Angeles. Tampa Bay losing Dave Canales to Carolina is the inverse case — Harmon explicitly credits Canales's own scheme design, not just talent, for "unleashing" [[Mike Evans]] and finally correcting a "gigantic mistake" alignment misuse of [[Chris Godwin]], which makes his departure a real downgrade risk independent of any of the players' own play. Philadelphia hiring Kellen Moore for [[Jalen Hurts]] is the uncertain middle case — real scheme upside (under-center play-action) but unresolved whether it fixes a personnel gap (no complementary weapons beyond A.J. Brown/DeVonta Smith) or a structural quarterback fit issue.

- 2024-02-01 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-01]]: the fullest single case study in this wiki, built entirely around process vs. results. Harmon's explicit framing on Arthur Smith's hire in Pittsburgh: most of the public reaction to Smith is "only thinking about results," not process — his Atlanta failure was fit-for-the-big-chair (decision-making, public conduct), not scheme quality, and his underlying offensive philosophy (heavy volume to a team's best players, route concepts built around the middle of the field) is exactly what modern NFL offenses want. Backed with hard charted evidence rather than just argument: [[George Pickens]] ran dig/slant routes on just 7.7%/10.7% of snaps under Matt Canada in 2022, versus [[Drake London]]'s 16.6%/24.5% under Smith the same year — a direct, numbers-based prediction of what Pickens's route diet should look like once Smith calls plays. Also cites Smith's Tennessee volume-funneling history (Derrick Henry's 300-carry seasons, A.J. Brown/Corey Davis's target concentration) as the mechanism, contingent on a "results" partner — Mike Tomlin, comped to Mike Vrabel's Arthur Smith-in-Tennessee role — who forces the scheme to actually reach the good players. See [[George Pickens]], [[Najee Harris]], [[Jaylen Warren]] and [[Diontae Johnson]].

### A generalized "transcend the system" test, off-topic origin (2024-02-02)

- 2024-02-02 — **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert)
  on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-02]], from an otherwise off-topic
  Hall of Fame discussion of Andre Johnson (not a tracked player, retired,
  no page): explicitly names this wiki's own recurring debate — "it's like
  the Brock Purdy discussion... people will say it's all system, it's all
  scheme, whatever — you have to be really good to take advantage of that
  scheme in that system, but also, inarguably, there are tailwinds here
  helping him." His generalized test for resolving it: did the player
  **transcend** a weak supporting cast rather than merely benefit from a
  strong one. Logged here as a reinforcing, cross-era restatement of the
  same framework applied throughout this page to [[Brock Purdy]],
  [[C.J. Stroud]], and the Cleveland/Rams cases, not as new evaluative
  content about any current player.

### Arthur Smith's Atlanta offense, the quarterback-constraint explanation (2024-02-05)

- 2024-02-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-05]]): offers the specific mechanism behind Atlanta's underwhelming 2023 passing game, ahead of Arthur Smith's move to Pittsburgh — not a talent or play-calling failure so much as a quarterback constraint. [[Desmond Ridder]] "was so bad at being able to read the field with routes that work dynamically between zones" that Smith reportedly simplified the route concepts to routes that end statically and break back to the quarterback, because routes developing across the field visibly confused Ridder about when to release the ball. States this directly against the fan complaints about [[Bijan Robinson]], [[Drake London]] and [[Kyle Pitts]] not getting used correctly — the offense was simplified *for* the quarterback it had, and Waldman expects "more dynamic offense" for Atlanta's skill talent once/if the Falcons upgrade the position, while separately hoping Pittsburgh (with Mike Tomlin managing him) gives Smith a "make the game plan as dynamic as it can be" mandate. See [[Kyle Pitts]] and [[Desmond Ridder]].

### A prospect-level case: Texas's offense masking Adonai Mitchell (2024-04-11)

- 2024-04-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on *Reception Perception: The Show* ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-11]]): argues Texas's 2023 offense actively suppressed [[Adonai Mitchell]]'s production profile — shaky quarterback play, layered RPOs and half-field reads left him with clips of well-run, open routes that were simply never thrown to, which Harmon says he has saved specifically to break down on video. Frames the fix as scheme/quarterback-dependent rather than talent-dependent: names Kansas City or Buffalo as ideal landing spots because an aggressive, patient quarterback (Patrick Mahomes or Josh Allen) is the type of passer who would actually reward Mitchell's downfield route-winning on tape.
- 2024-05-02 — According to Brandon Angelo ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-02]]): Angelo said he weighs NFL draft capital differently by position — largely discounting it at quarterback (he estimates the position's draft-capital hit rate around only 33%) and weighing it heaviest at running back, where a first-round-ish pick typically signals a real year-one workload share; at wide receiver he weighs it least because, per scouts he's spoken with, WR value varies most by team need. Separately, both hosts framed 'infrastructure' — offensive line, weapons around a rookie, ownership stability, and a GM willing to invest — as a top-down evaluation layer as important as the player himself, contrasting [[Caleb Williams]]'s well-resourced Bears landing spot with the dysfunctional 2023 Panthers situation (no blocking, no run game, no receivers, ownership interference) [[Bryce Young]] inherited as the range of outcomes the same draft slot can produce.
- 2024-05-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-20]]): gap-scheme running back projections hinge on the offensive line's football IQ, not just backfield talent — he cautioned that both [[Antonio Gibson]] in New England and [[Tyrone Tracy Jr.]] with the Giants need "smart, versatile, mobile" blockers to make gap runs work, and questioned whether either line qualifies yet. He made the same scheme-inflation point about receiving stats: Gibson's college receiving numbers were built in a zone-heavy Memphis scheme, not against man coverage in a true slot role, so they overstate his NFL receiving translation.
- 2024-06-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-06-18]]), using [[Chris Olave]] as the example: elite RP separation data can be masked by poor scheme fit. Pete Carmichael's Saints offense never used motion and force-fed Olave a vertical-only route tree (nines/posts/corners/curls, almost no slants) despite pairing him with checkdown-happy [[Derek Carr]], who graded poorly on exactly those deep route types (36-50% success on nines/corners/posts). Harmon compares the mismatch to [[Calvin Ridley]] in Jacksonville -- a technically detailed route runner miscast on a vertical-heavy tree instead of being deployed over the middle -- and is hopeful new OC Clint Kubiak's Shanahan-style motion offense corrects this for Olave and other Saints vertical receivers like [[A.T. Perry]].
- 2024-06-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (untracked co-host) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-06-25]]): The hosts frame several 2024 coordinator changes through the lens of scheme mattering as much as talent. New Orleans' offense scored top-10 in points last year under Pete Carmichael despite being a bad offense by almost every underlying measure (dead-last in full-speed motion usage), which the hosts attribute to Sean Payton's system being hard to replicate rather than to Carmichael's own design; new OC Clint Kubiak, previously with the motion-heavy 49ers, should change that. By contrast, they describe new Steelers OC Arthur Smith as a 'system above talent' coach who doesn't tailor route concepts to personnel, creating a schematic mismatch with George Pickens' deep-sideline skill set. Kliff Kingsbury's track record (a losing record at Texas Tech with Patrick Mahomes at QB, a stale/horizontal Cardinals passing game, and Caleb Williams' regression at USC under him) is cited as reason for skepticism in Washington despite a track record of well-designed run schemes. Shane Steichen's success maximizing Michael Pittman Jr. in Indianapolis is offered as the positive counterexample of scheme elevating a player.
- 2024-07-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-15]]): Waldman used [[Rachaad White]] as a case study for scheme masking efficiency stats -- Tampa Bay's duo-heavy run scheme produces tight, in-line creases rather than the big gap/outside-zone holes a back like [[Christian McCaffrey]] runs through, so a modest yards-per-carry figure (White's 3.6) reflects the blocking concept more than the runner's own talent level and shouldn't be read as a standalone red flag.
- 2024-07-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and guest OL analyst Brandon Thorn (not tracked) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-18]]): across a division-spanning breakdown of six offensive lines (Washington, Green Bay, LA Chargers, LA Rams, Buffalo, New Orleans, Tennessee), the pair repeatedly framed offensive-line quality and scheme identity (run-first, play-action-heavy systems under McVay, Harbaugh/Roman, Brady, LaFleur) as the ceiling-setter for whether a receiver room can be 'maximized,' independent of individual receiver talent -- e.g., [[Puka Nacua]]/[[Cooper Kupp]] benefiting from an elite Rams run-blocking interior versus [[Chris Olave]]/[[Rashid Shaheed]] being capped by a shaky Saints line reliant on one player's (Ryan Ramczyk) health. Harmon noted this is a recurring framework on the show: an efficient run game and favorable down-and-distance 'can hide a lot of weaknesses' up front and create manufactured or schemed-open touches for receivers who might not consistently win on talent alone.
- 2024-07-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-25]]): deployment and pre-snap answers can matter as much as receiver ability. He argues [[Derek Carr]] was failed by systems that gave him few pre-snap answers — Pete Carmichael's New Orleans offense and Josh McDaniels' in Las Vegas — versus offenses like Detroit's with [[Jared Goff]] or Miami's with [[Tua Tagovailoa]], where answers are abundant, and hopes play action and motion under Klint Kubiak change that for [[Chris Olave]]. The clearest case is [[Diontae Johnson]]: over 88% X alignment for four years under Matt Canada produced the per-target inefficiency people blame him for, and 2023's more balanced 76.3/21.1 split delivered career-best yards per target and per route run. Harmon likewise credits Dave Canales for rebuilding [[Mike Evans]]' route tree around [[Baker Mayfield]] and warns Liam Cohen not to touch it.
- 2024-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-06]]): Harmon uses the [[Brandon Aiyuk]] trade rumours to argue against the system-receiver label — an X receiver with Aiyuk's yards per route run and 60% success on the nine route cannot be called a Shanahan creation, and he calls the suggestion borderline stupid. But he and James Koh (not tracked) both hold that environment still governs opportunity: San Francisco threw the fewest passes in the NFL, Atlanta sent just 209 targets to receivers and Chicago 259, and Pittsburgh under Arthur Smith projects the same way, so a move from Kyle Shanahan to Smith would be the inverse of [[Stefon Diggs]] going to Buffalo. Their [[Davante Adams]] example — leaving an MVP quarterback for an unsettled situation — is offered as the warning that the grass is not always greener.
- 2024-09-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-09]]): receiver team fit is routinely misjudged by the fantasy community in both directions. He argues [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] is mismatched to become what his tape shows because Arizona wants to run, spreads the ball around and has a quarterback in [[Kyler Murray]] who won't climb the pocket to a second read — while [[A.J. Brown]] and [[Justin Jefferson]] were both widely called the worst landing spots among their draft's receivers and were great as rookies anyway. The corollary he draws from [[Quentin Johnston]] is that scheme can also rescue talent: usage that stops asking a receiver to win at the boundary on deep verticals and instead feeds him slants, hitches and digs converted a misused player into a probable WR2 in his own offense.
- 2024-09-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-11]]): Waldman pressed on how much of [[Cam Ward]]'s leap is scheme and surrounding talent versus development. He asked whether people underrate surrounding talent — that a player finally paired with good teammates gets to play to his full capability because he is no longer worrying about things he should not have to worry about down by down, leaving enough clean bandwidth for game-changing improvisation. Sharpe agreed and pointed to the Air Raid context: Eric Morris (a Mike Leach disciple who coached [[Patrick Mahomes]] at Texas Tech) ran a horizontal, east-west spread at Washington State that de-emphasized both downfield passing and the run, while Miami under Mario Cristobal gives Ward a 6-1, 240 back and the best receiver group he has had. Sharpe also warned that a college receiver's offense can make him unproductive regardless of ability.
- 2024-09-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-12]]): two Week 1 illustrations of scheme fit doing the work that talent alone will not. On the ground, duo puts two pairs of double teams up front and creates narrow creases that demand fast multiple-choice vision, while zone offers wider creases and an easier read — so Tampa Bay adding zone to its gap-heavy menu directly serves [[Rachaad White]] (as well as [[Sean Tucker]] and [[Bucky Irving]]) rather than exposing him. In the passing game, he criticizes the habit of importing a college usage template wholesale: the league assumed [[Quentin Johnston]] would be a TCU-style contested-catch vertical target, which his own tape did not support by NFL standards, and only worked when the Chargers moved him underneath and across the field. He also separates scheme-created production from earned separation — [[Gabriel Davis]] wins where the design does the work, while [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] manipulated defenders to create his own. Harstad (NOT tracked) praises New Orleans for defining receiver roles and asking players only to do what they are good at, citing [[Alvin Kamara]].
