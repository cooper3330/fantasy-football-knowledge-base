# Yards After Catch Receiver Archetype

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-09-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-26]]): the episode's organizing idea, which he calls taking a 'meta look' and has also written up on the site. The receivers today's NFL deprioritizes are smaller route-runners who are not fast, not explosive, and give you 'net nothing after the catch' — his examples are [[Jahan Dotson]], [[Elijah Moore]] and [[Skyy Moore]], all of whom posted decent Reception Perception separation scores without becoming high-end target earners. The reason separation alone does not convert: those windows are small, and most quarterbacks are not good enough to hit them. What is prized instead is the [[Rashee Rice]] / [[Khalil Shakir]] / [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] archetype — beats zone coverage, reliable hands, and turns a 'Mickey Mouse route' into five to seven extra yards. Harmon's illustration: Kansas City puts Rice inside and [[Xavier Worthy]] outside precisely because Rice creates after the catch and Worthy does not. Koh praises him for 'not being a slave to your own numbers.'
- 2024-11-20 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-20]]): Koh argued YAC production is hard to replicate season to season, and that the Shanahan system is uniquely maximized by receivers who create after the catch because its route concepts — digs, overs — keep breaking away from the defender rather than back toward the quarterback. [[Deebo Samuel]]'s YAC per reception falling 12.3 (2020) to 10.4 to 9.0 to 8.86 is his evidence the archetype ages badly under that usage. Harmon's counterpoint is that the value in San Francisco is mostly spacing, not tackle-breaking: in a well-spaced Shanahan scheme receivers get open even when they are not separators and gain after the catch even when they are not tacklebreakers, which creates a wide margin for error — versus Chicago under Shane Waldron, where three receivers working in isolation produced no explosives until Thomas Brown's play calling restored horizontal structure for [[D.J. Moore]]. Harmon expects the 49ers' 2025 trio of [[Brandon Aiyuk]], [[Jauan Jennings]] and [[Ricky Pearsall]] to be separators and route runners rather than 'YAC bros.'
- 2024-12-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-16]]): YAC production on a short-passing offense is not simply a function of scheme. Using [[Khalil Shakir]] — who led the league in yards after the catch for much of the season — he argues plenty of receivers get put in that same role and cannot do what Shakir does, and that Shakir's work makes [[Josh Allen]] look good on those short throws rather than the reverse. He extends the point to [[Rashad Bateman]], whose first-round grade came from being multidimensional rather than a YAC-only over-the-middle player, and to [[Malik Nabers]], where after-catch ability is one leg of a profile that makes the quarterback irrelevant.
- 2025-01-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-07]]): Harmon restated a thesis he has pushed all season — the outside route-runner types who do not create after the catch and are not real big ball winners are less important in today's NFL, because they are compilers who need heavy volume to matter. His reasoning is that explosive plays are harder than ever to generate, so offenses hunt them either through yards-after-catch opportunities or over-the-top shots, and this archetype provides neither. He put [[Jahan Dotson]] and [[Elijah Moore]] in that bucket explicitly. The corollary elsewhere in the episode is that the receivers he was most encouraged by — [[Quentin Johnston]] fed sub-10 air-yards-per-target touches in space, [[Jalen McMillan]] on runway routes with a head of steam — were being used in ways that manufacture explosives rather than compile.
- 2025-03-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-05]]): the YAC-manufactured-touch archetype has a shelf life that shows up in the box score as volume without yardage. On [[Deebo Samuel]] to Washington he notes Samuel has never been a traditional separator even at his peak — 50.8%, 64.3% and 56% success rate vs man from 2020-2022 — so his value was always zone-beating plus yards after catch, and both are declining, with YAC down each of the last four seasons. Washington ranked fourth in receptions on plays with a wide receiver or tight end screen but 21st in yards per reception on them, which Harmon frames as the profile of the usage Samuel will get under Cliff Kingsbury: his joke was a '90 reception, 500 yard season'. The broader point is that a manufactured-touch role inflates catch totals while the efficiency that made the archetype valuable erodes first.
- 2025-04-02 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-02]]): the archetype he is deliberately putting more priority on is the receiver who can beat man coverage AND be dangerous after the catch — 'souped-up [[Khalil Shakir]] / faster, better-vs-man [[Rashee Rice]]' — which he says is where the value is in today's NFL. [[Luther Burden III]] fits (in space on 70% of catches, down on first contact 37.5%, multiple broken tackles on 25%). Harmon also argued YAC ability is not purely athletic: he traced [[Travis Hunter]]'s open-field play to football intelligence and reading defenders' tackling tendencies, citing a conversation with [[Malik Nabers]] at the Super Bowl about studying who over-pursues, who under-pursues and who is genuinely willing to tackle — combined with explosiveness and pound-for-pound play strength.
- 2025-06-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-11]]): Harmon says he cares far more about whether a receiver breaks the first tackle than about yards-after-catch-per-reception, because the per-reception number is heavily influenced by role and scheme. He splits the archetype in two: the zero-to-60 explosion type, where the ball in the hands is the play — [[Ja'Marr Chase]] is the exemplar and Harmon thinks [[Malik Nabers]] can become one — and the consistent tackle-breaker, where [[Puka Nacua]] lives, going down on first contact on only 43.2% of in-space plays and breaking multiple tackles on 16%, turning a 15-yard dig into a 20-yard gain by shielding the ball and churning. Harmon ties that to field awareness at the catch point rather than athleticism, contrasting it with [[Diontae Johnson]] stopping to assess after the catch, and notes the rugged style may contribute to Nacua's nagging injuries.
- 2025-06-27 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-27]]): the closing would-you-rather asked which is more valuable — a receiver who struggles to catch but is dynamic with the ball in his hands ([[Quentin Johnston]]), or one who struggles to catch but is a promising route runner ([[Dontayvion Wicks]]). Waldman split it by roster context: with contested-catch route runners already in place he takes the run-after-catch player, because he can be role-catered on slants, crossers and quick inside routes where he catches better than downfield; but if he has to develop and lean on a player, he takes the route runner, because Wicks's catch issue reads as overthinking rather than an inability to track the ball. [[Matt Harmon]] took the other side outright — the run-after-catch player, because it is simply easier to get him the football, a quick five-yard slant versus hoping both the route and the throw connect. Both agreed price matters: at equal cost, take the easier one.
- 2025-07-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-16]]): Harmon argues the way to unlock a YAC-first receiver is alignment, not target volume — he wants [[Quentin Johnston]] moved off the X and used as a flanker so the yield per target rises, citing 65.6% in-space catch attempts, 33.3% down on first contact and 23.8% multiple broken tackles as the real skill. He invokes [[Rashee Rice]]'s Kansas City deployment as the archetype of getting the ball out quickly and letting the player run, while explicitly saying Rice is the more physical player and not a true comp. The same logic explains [[Marvin Mims Jr.]]'s screen and backfield usage in Denver.
