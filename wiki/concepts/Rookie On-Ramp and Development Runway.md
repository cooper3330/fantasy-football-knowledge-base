# Rookie On-Ramp and Development Runway

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-08-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-15]]): guest Dan Hatman (Scouting Academy, NOT a tracked expert) framed rookie deployment as a highway on-ramp. Philadelphia's Schuylkill had a 50-degree incline with a hundred feet to merge into cars doing 75; where he lives now there is a 15 percent grade with half a mile to come up to speed. Which do you want to hand your rookie? If you have a trusted veteran who extends the roster's versatility and redundancy while you onboard the kid, and the room knows the rookie is the eventual answer, there is no reason to rush. His model case is Andy Reid trading up to 10 for [[Patrick Mahomes]], keeping [[Alex Smith]] as a quarterback the room believed could win games, and assigning Matt Nagy a year on protections and coverages. The counter-cases are [[Anthony Richardson]], [[Cam Ward]] and [[Caleb Williams]], all of whom simply were better than the incumbent and got the steep ramp. Waldman's fantasy translation: still bet on the talented second-rounder even if weeks one to five are rough.
- 2025-08-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-18]]): drawing on a conversation with Dan Hatman of The Scouting Academy (not a tracked expert), Waldman described teams that carry an assignment-sound veteran as a deliberate mechanism for giving a more talented rookie a longer runway. Chicago is his live example: [[Olamide Zaccheaus]] can be trusted with the assignment while Ben Johnson enforces a 'no block, no rock' standard that makes [[Luther Burden III]] earn snaps, and Waldman expects Zaccheaus to start for part of the year without that changing Burden's talent or his above-100-target projection. He tied the same idea to [[Troy Franklin]] in Denver, whose blocking willingness converted into targets, and to [[Trey Lance]], for whom a stable organization with a quarterback-coach head coach and a good starter ahead of him provides the watch-and-learn runway his earlier situations never did.
- 2025-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-19]]): relaying a conversation with former Eagles and Giants scout Dan Hatman of The Scouting Academy, Waldman argues that a competent, versatile veteran ahead of a rookie on the depth chart is a feature rather than a problem. When you have a player like [[Olamide Zaccheaus]] who can be trusted, you can give a more talented young player a runway instead of force-feeding him the ball before his assignments are sound — which lowers the risk of the rookie drowning, and typically ends with him overtaking the veteran early in the season and playing strong football. He applies this directly to [[Luther Burden III]] in Chicago under Ben Johnson, and pairs it with the point that a coach pulling a rookie from practice for lining up wrong can be culture-setting for the whole unit rather than a verdict on the individual.
- 2025-08-27 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-27]]): Harmon restates his one-side-of-the-field rule — receivers who took most of their charted snaps at a single alignment usually need a year before they matter, if they ever do. He applies it to [[Jack Bech]] (over 70% of sampled snaps at right outside receiver) and [[Tory Horton]] (77.4% right outside). The exceptions prove the rule: [[JuJu Smith-Schuster]] and [[Rashee Rice]] moved straight into the slot (and Rice only came on in the back half of his rookie year), [[DJ Moore]] was a gadget/manufactured-touch rookie, and [[Drake London]] was simply an elite prospect. He pairs it with a college-system on-ramp: Josh Heupel's Tennessee offense, which he likens to Art Briles' Baylor, inflates collegiate production and leaves receivers unready — [[Josh Palmer]], [[Cedric Tillman]] and [[Jalen Hyatt]] all needed time or have not arrived, and [[Hendon Hooker]] was just cut by Detroit. Harmon says to read the Tennessee pipeline from 2021 on, when Heupel arrived, and expects [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]] to need a season or two.
- 2025-09-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-04 (Going Deep Rookie Receivers and RB Depth Charts)]]): a slow rookie start is often an intentional design rather than a signal of a coach souring. Waldman described Ben Johnson giving [[Luther Burden III]] a four-to-six-week on-ramp behind [[Olamide Zaccheaus]] so the rookie can be assignment-sound in narrow situations, build confidence and be coached in detail without being overwhelmed — the same make-him-earn-it method used on [[Jameson Williams]]. Angelo applied the same logic to [[Kaleb Johnson]] with [[Aaron Rodgers]], arguing you do not set a rookie up to fail alongside a master craftsman who audibles freely, since that is a confidence killer. Both cited [[Alvin Kamara]] with Drew Brees (three to four weeks before the staff trusted him) and [[Jahmyr Gibbs]]' light early usage under Ben Johnson as the rare and the normal cases respectively. Waldman noted the pattern fans get wrong: when a player looks good once, the demand is to dump everything on him immediately.
- 2025-09-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-15]]): Waldman publicly revised one of his own heuristics. [[Quinshon Judkins]] took the bulk of the workload after one full padded practice and a walkthrough — his first practices since the first week of June — and looked good doing it. Waldman said that 'throws my whole theory out that you need August to get ready to play football,' and credited Judkins directly. The practical implication for fantasy is that a talented back who misses camp entirely may not need the multi-week acclimation ramp the market usually prices in.
- 2025-09-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-23]]): the industry is 'so impatient' with young receivers, and Week 3 offered the counter-evidence. [[Quentin Johnston]] was set up to fail as a rookie X on go routes, made a measurable micro leap in year two, and in year three, after the staff spent a year learning that his best routes are in-breakers, is producing on a slant- and dig-heavy tree. [[Ricky Pearsall]] did nothing as a rookie and in year two is third in the NFL in receiving yards because he is locked in on what the offense wants. Harmon extended the logic to the Chargers stashing [[Tre Harris]] and [[KeAndre Lambert-Smith]]: an effective redshirt year can produce two system-fluent starters in 2026 with continuity at coach and quarterback. The counterexample is [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]] — a raw rookie forced into near-every-snap X work he is not ready for.
- 2025-10-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-10-28]]): citing a chart shared by Ron Stewart, Harmon notes rookie wide receiver production tends to increase from Week 9 onward, particularly for Day 2/3 picks -- naming [[Tetairoa McMillan]], [[Emeka Egbuka]], [[Chimere Dike]] and the Houston rookies as players whose season shouldn't be fully 'defined' yet based on the first half. He flags [[Travis Hunter]] as a likely exception who could see a 'post-bye bump' beyond the normal rookie-development curve, and separately blames Houston's underwhelming rookie-receiver integration partly on a poor offensive design plan and an offensive line GM Nick Caserio said pre-season he wasn't concerned about.
- 2026-01-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-01-05]]): cites [[Colston Loveland]]'s rookie year as a case study in why raw box-score output undersells a prospect -- Loveland played behind a bad offensive line, with a quarterback ([[Caleb Williams]]) effectively repeating a rookie year under new infrastructure, and a coaching staff (Ben Johnson) intentionally building assignment-soundness before increasing complexity. Waldman argues that context, not the counting stats, is what should drive the evaluation of Loveland's actual talent level -- which he still rates as the top tight end in his draft class.
