# Having Skills vs Being Skilled

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-03-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-24]]): there is a difference between having skills — tools in the toolbox — and being skilled, the master craftsman who can do more with a hammer than others do with the right tool. He reports RSP scores rising over the past three seasons with no change to his process: 38 prospects in the 2025 class scored at least 80 on the depth-of-talent scale (the rotational-starter threshold), versus 25 in 2024 and 22 in 2023. The growth is proportionate and concentrated in the contributor and rotational tiers, not the starter and franchise tiers — for every high-end grade there are three to five more receivers landing in the middle of the bell curve. He credits seven-on-seven, year-round flag football, private receiver coaches and camps for raising the technical baseline: prospects arrive knowing more releases, stem manipulations and break types than ever. But as the bar for competency rises so does the bar for excellence, and the number of NFL jobs is constant. His analogy is Instagram musicians with immaculate technique in solo clips who have never learned to play with a band: these receivers acquired skills in isolation, and are not yet skilled at sequencing techniques into a coherent story a defensive back believes, at picking the right technique at the right moment, or at working with a quarterback against disguises in the moment.
- 2025-04-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-21 (Film & Data with Dwain McFarland)]]): the RSP's edge over pure data is grading technique, conceptual understanding and craft separately. A big, strong, fast athlete can show a release technique — a double-up, a hip drop into a break — and beat most college corners with the tool alone; in the NFL the same maneuver requires patience, suddenness and pacing changes to set it up. Waldman illustrated it by showing Dave Kluge [[Tre Harris]] tape next to Chad Johnson and [[Davante Adams]] performing the same maneuver and looking completely different. His scoring tiers are gated by explicit checklists — for releases, two moves combining feet and hands to reach one tier, stacking defenders for the next, patience and suddenness on two or three moves for the one above. Asked by guest Dwain McFarland (Fantasy Life, NOT tracked) whether craft can be developed, Waldman said it depends which skill: releases can be learned (Adams did not enter the league with the releases he has now), but weight drop and acceleration into breaks are much harder, so he grades those closer to a hard ceiling. He contrasted [[Malik Nabers]], whose every move already looked like Adams at LSU, with prospects who get open in college but won't against a primary NFL defender.
- 2026-02-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-02-13]]): drew a hard line between measurable physical talent and actually being skilled at the position -- 'the best players can do the mundane things at the highest level,' while receivers who are all physical talent and no fundamentals (cited: Darius Heyward-Bay as a first-round-caliber athlete who never played out that talent) never pan out. Waldman said [[DeAndre Hopkins]]'s ability to make the 'amazing' plays is underpinned by fundamentals that are 'usually rock solid,' meaning the spectacular plays are built on the same mundane technique mastery, not a separate gift.
