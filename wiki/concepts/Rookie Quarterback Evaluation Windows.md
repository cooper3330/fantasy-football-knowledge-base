# Rookie Quarterback Evaluation Windows

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-09-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-20]]): the framework he uses for grading QBs once they start their NFL careers. The first two to six starts are a *continuation* of the rookie evaluation, not a re-evaluation, because — per scouts and QB coaches he has talked to, and against what media, owners and executives do — it takes roughly 18 to 30 games to know what a quarterback can and cannot do. Three overlapping windows: (1) games 6-8, ask whether the scheme permits a fair evaluation at all (can he maneuver a pressured pocket, is there skill talent getting open, is the scheme demanding too much or just enough) and whether he still looks like his college self, pluses *and* minuses; (2) games 6-20, defenses need four to six weeks of pro tape — they never scout college tape — before building a book and testing him, so early hype fades and you watch how far he regresses and whether he rebounds; (3) games 15-30, has he shown growth against his negatives, which is what separates second-contract long-term starters from franchise-tag stopgaps ([[Daniel Jones]]), bridge guys and backups. Throughout, he insists on process over results: good scouting is reading how a QB integrates physical, technical and conceptual skills to put himself and teammates in position to make positive plays, whether or not the play works. His examples: [[Caleb Williams]], [[Anthony Richardson]], [[Bryce Young]], [[Will Levis]], [[Jayden Daniels]], [[Bo Nix]], [[Skylar Thompson]], [[Baker Mayfield]], [[Geno Smith]].
- 2024-09-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-23]]): two connected arguments about judging young quarterbacks too early. First, early-season success by a previously written-off passer usually means defenses have not scouted him yet — his examples were [[Malik Willis]] in Green Bay, plus Josh Dobbs, [[Will Levis]], Marcus Mariota, Paxton Lynch and [[Drew Lock]], all of whom looked plausible for a stretch. Staffs limit the game plan to what the player does well, and it holds until a defense makes him overthink; he drew the parallel to 2-0 and 3-1 teams everyone is excited about in September and slapping their heads over by November. Second, the reverse error: expecting instant production. He cited the [[Bryce Young]] / [[Trevor Lawrence]] first-18-starts comparison (near-identical completion rates around 59 percent, both on their second scheme in two years) and previewed an [[Anthony Richardson]] versus Andrew Luck versus Peyton Manning same-window comparison he says looks remarkably similar. His prescription is intermittent starting — let a young quarterback play, then study in the background when things slip away, then return with the confidence that it is his team — and his complaint is that the 'get them on the field because of the money' logic ignores that early exposure can make them worse.
- 2024-09-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-25]]): Harmon argues the analysis community overreacts to rookie quarterback struggles and then gets excessively high on the rare rookie who does not struggle, citing [[Jayden Daniels]] as the current example of the latter. He also wants matchup context in the window — Week 1 against Todd Bowles or a Giants front with Dexter Lawrence is a bad sample for judging a rookie. With [[Anthony Richardson]] he goes further, saying he almost does not want to form a view until the end of the season when there is a full catalog, rather than hyper-focusing on individual throws. The corollary for [[Caleb Williams]] is that mistakes under pressure are the price of the rookie year and say nothing about arm talent; the situation — line play, scheme, front-office resource allocation — is the variable worth judging.
- 2024-09-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-26 (Win-Now Trades and Jayden Daniels)]]): Waldman defended the 'slow launch' model of rookie quarterback deployment, borrowing the business analogy of limiting hours or inventory so operations can steady before volume arrives. Given the common complaint that teams put rookies in bad spots, he argues the worse option is demanding high-level manipulation immediately — [[Caleb Williams]] without an offensive line, [[Anthony Richardson]] without refined receivers — versus Washington easing [[Jayden Daniels]] in with heavy screen and scheme usage and then opening the offense in week three. He adds a cynical layer: a slow launch also appeases media, owners and executives who listen to media, while buying competitive football. The caveat is evaluative, not developmental — he expects defenses to clamp schemed looks around weeks four to six, which is when you learn what the rookie can actually do. Co-host Adam Harstad (Football Guys, NOT tracked) is blunter still: playing well through three games beats not playing well, but the range of outcomes stays very wide and he will not crown anyone, and he jokes Washington's pass catchers have 'Dwayne Haskins goggles' rather than beer goggles.
- 2024-10-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-03]]): rookie quarterback results now say as much about the vehicle as the driver. Coaches, who hate starting rookies while owners love it, increasingly simplify schemes to meet the player where he is rather than stretch him — which works short term but not long term unless the player keeps growing. That makes [[Jayden Daniels]] (self-driving car, one-to-two read menu) and [[Caleb Williams]] (bad line, full progression reads and line-of-scrimmage checks) non-comparable on box score alone: Waldman thinks one is inflated and one deflated. He expects a three-to-five-year dip in passing yardage until teams give quarterbacks real development plans, noting the veterans who understand disguised coverages are still ripping up cover two, quarters and cover three — but they had to be given those experiences to grow into it, and Daniels may look like an exception rather than the norm within a year or two.
- 2024-10-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-29]]): using [[Trevor Lawrence]] as the case, Harmon warns against the default assumption that young players 'keep ticking up, up, up' in trajectory. His sequence: a rookie year suppressed by an unplayable environment (Urban Meyer), a year-two rebound, and then a year-three leap that simply never arrived — and he argues sometimes year three is just the same player. He extends the point to receivers and to draft position generally: being a top-10 pick at any position does not entitle a player to the step up. He pairs this with a plea to retire the 'generational' label, citing both Lawrence and [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]], and with a complaint that automatic record quarterback contracts pile expectations onto players who are merely viable starters.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31]]): the [[Anthony Richardson]] benching is a case study in how organizations wreck a developmental window. Waldman argues the right model is deliberate, *communicated* intermittent benching — Chuck Noll benched Terry Bradshaw repeatedly, and Marty Schottenheimer's alternating starts and benchings with Drew Brees in San Diego are credited by Brees himself for his development, because the message was 'you're our guy, you're just not seeing the field well.' What Indianapolis did instead was a sudden, unexplained switch that Angelo says teammates including [[Michael Pittman Jr.]], Josh Downs and Zaire Franklin learned about from social media or a walkthrough. Angelo contrasts Dave Canales telling the Panthers team before benching Bryce Young, and quotes Steichen's own draft-day line that development comes with playing time and Ballard's Bradshaw analogy — commitments the org then abandoned. Waldman's warning: the Sam Darnold 'I see ghosts' outcome is what happens when a young quarterback's play is allowed to deteriorate unaddressed.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31 (Tapping Out, Archetypes and Modeling)]]): co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) rejected the belief that a young quarterback can only develop while playing. We have mostly observed in-game development because the rookie wage suppression system makes starting rookies early maximally advantageous to teams — not because the bench stunts anyone; Philip Rivers, [[Aaron Rodgers]] and [[Patrick Mahomes]] (who sat almost his entire rookie year) were arguably better in their first starts because of that time. Applied to [[Anthony Richardson]], the benching is at most a weak second-order indicator and we already have the on-field evidence to judge him directly. Harstad's framing for both Richardson and [[Jayden Daniels]]: if either is the identical quarterback five years from now, neither has a long career — what matters is the development slope, and that is the same question whether the player is on the field or not.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-31]]): on the [[Anthony Richardson]] benching, Harmon argued a young quarterback's evaluation cannot be settled from outside the building — 'whatever we think we know on the outside, whatever we think we see on the film, these guys in the building know a lot more.' He said he was skeptical of the decision until Dominique Foxworth signalled on Mina Kimes's show that people close to the situation supported it, and he then read the visible tap-out as 'a symptom, not a disease.' He also rejected the framing as binary: a team can bench a quarterback to win games now, out of obligation to the other 52 players risking their bodies, and still be ramping him toward retaking the job. Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) made the sit-and-learn case from history — Steve Young, Patrick Mahomes behind Alex Smith, Aaron Rodgers — against David Carr, whose career he argued was damaged by playing behind a terrible line and building bad habits, and [[Bryce Young]], who needed a mental break in 2023 and never got one.
- 2024-11-07 — According to co-host Adam Harstad (not a tracked expert) and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-07]]): on the throw-him-in-the-fire vs. sit-him debate, Harstad said the answer is different for every quarterback and the reason why is the development band — you want him succeeding 80-90% and failing 10-20%, and whether that means in the game or off the field depends on the player, so intermittent development may be the best compromise: push him, watch whether the stretch is too far, pull him back or push further. Both noted quarterback is the position where the NFL actually does develop, because coaches' fates are tied to it — Harstad cited Hard Knocks footage of Matt Eberflus spending disproportionate time with [[Caleb Williams]], since if that quarterback is good the coach has another decade and if he is bad the coach has no job next year, whereas a running back who fails is simply replaced by the next one.
- 2024-11-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-11]]): early-season rookie mistakes should be treated as data collection, not verdict — 'let these guys make some mistakes and see if they learn from them.' [[Bo Nix]] drew concern from former-quarterback coaches for early game-management errors and has since learned from them, which is why Waldman is edging toward calling him the class's most productive rookie. He is skeptical that raw college experience transfers on its own (the argument made for [[Brock Purdy]]'s five years as a starter has failed for plenty of others); what matters is processing speed, pocket maneuvering, ball placement and intelligent game management. He also frames the second inflection point: once teams accumulate three weeks of tape, they add 'pages to the book,' which is what he thinks is behind [[Jayden Daniels]]' dip rather than any talent re-rating.
- 2024-11-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-12]]): most quarterbacks are ruined rather than simply failing, and Week 10 of 2024 offered a controlled comparison. Harmon's blueprint for ruining a good prospect — bad offensive line, a coordinator who cannot fix one, a head coach with no offensive handle — is what [[Caleb Williams]] is living through in Chicago, and he argues you would need to fix only one of the line or the coaching to survive, but not neither. Against that he sets [[Bryce Young]], whose benching produced a demonstrably more confident player, and [[Anthony Richardson]], whose situation is legible only if you know whether the benching was developmental or win-now. Koh (not a tracked expert) adds the sequencing risk: a second benching stops reading as a breather and starts reading as 'he stinks', which is the damaging version. Harmon also cautions against the offseason-hype framing that Chicago was the best situation a first overall pick could land in — ten weeks of film say otherwise — and against declaring any of these situations permanent this early.
- 2024-11-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-14]]): both hosts argue rookie-season data is a trap in both directions. Waldman's central example is [[Jayden Daniels]], whose nine-game rookie profile matches Robert Griffin III's down to an identical 8.59 adjusted yards per attempt, and Mike Shanahan's warning to ownership that the production would not survive defenses catching up to the scheme. Angelo supplies the punch-and-counterpunch version: [[Will Levis]]'s huge first start drew Marcus Mariota comparisons and a year later Tennessee was shopping for a quarterback, while [[C.J. Stroud]]'s spectacular rookie year did not survive losing his offensive line. The corollary is that a bad rookie year is equally uninformative — [[Caleb Williams]] behind a bottom-two line and [[Jared Goff]] labeled a colossal bust as a third-stringer are the same error in reverse.
- 2024-12-19 — According to Adam Harstad (NOT tracked) and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-19 (NFL QB Lunacy)]]): the objections to carrying two quarterbacks — you can't develop them, you won't know what you have, you'll damage their psyche — are all rejected here. Harstad cites Jordan Love getting no starter reps for two years then hitting the ground running, Aaron Rodgers looking atrocious in his first two preseasons while developing invisibly, and Patrick Mahomes sitting nearly all of his rookie year before an MVP first season; the pattern is rarer now for salary-cap reasons, not because it stopped working. On knowing what you have, Arizona knew about Josh Rosen inside a year, and Jimmy Johnson said he didn't know what a pro quarterback looked like until he saw Aikman in practice. On psyche, Harstad calls the concern 'infantilizing' — top-program quarterbacks fend off four- and five-star recruits every year. Waldman adds his own preference for intermittent starting and benching, with the coach reading whether a quarterback is lost (bench him before he overthinks it) or merely wrong-but-decisive like [[Bo Nix]] (let him play through it), and cites Steve McNair's famously long, graduated development curve under Jeff Fisher.
- 2025-01-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-20]]): scouts say 18 to 30 games is what you really want before you know what you have, because opponents catch up to a rookie quarterback's tape and start testing him in ways he was not tested in year one. He cites [[C.J. Stroud]] — where he judged both seasons outliers — and [[Baker Mayfield]] after a hot half-season as the precedents, and applies the lesson forward to every 2024 rookie: [[Jayden Daniels]], [[Caleb Williams]], [[Bo Nix]], [[Drake Maye]] and [[Michael Penix Jr.]]. He adds that most people over-weight year-one production and under-weight the context of how it was produced, and that a change in offensive line quality can dramatically change both the outcomes and the reaction to the player. His summary: he graded the whole class as capable of becoming quality starters, and 'we'll see if that holds up by the end of year two.'
- 2025-02-27 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-27]]): the pair argue that raw college statistics hide the thing that decides NFL jobs, and that evaluation has to run down-to-down and situation-to-situation — black zone, red zone, third down, high-leverage snaps. In college a quarterback can make five big mistakes and erase them with eight explosive plays and still win comfortably; Waldman uses Tevin Coleman at Indiana as the analogy, where two plays produced almost all of a 170-yard game and the rest were bad or neutral. In the NFL those high-leverage moments are less frequent but far higher magnitude — Waldman's rule is that one to three plays decide a game for every quarterback, and Angelo cites [[Will Levis]] making two or three crippling mistakes in a Bears game Chicago had no business winning. The evaluative question is therefore not whether a prospect makes those errors but whether he cleans them up over time. Both also warn against clipping bad throws without the game script: [[Cam Ward]]'s worst throws came down multiple scores behind a terrible Miami defense.
- 2025-03-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-03]]): Waldman stated his quarterback process explicitly — he likes to see 18 to 30 games from a quarterback and to watch the growth across them before committing to a verdict, which is why he holds [[Jayden Daniels]] at 'pretty good' rather than 'really good' despite a strong rookie year he partly reads as padded. The corollary he applied to the 2025 class is landing-spot dependence: whether a rookie starts immediately is dictated by owners rather than readiness (his example being Paxton Lynch), and the ideal is a year or two behind a veteran — [[Shedeur Sanders]] behind [[Aaron Rodgers]] in New York, or [[Cam Ward]] behind [[Russell Wilson]] in Las Vegas — rather than being thrust into a thin roster like New Orleans.
- 2025-04-03 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-03]]): the difference between a failed and a functional young quarterback is often only the development window. He used [[Malik Willis]] — thrown into the fire in the worst possible situation, judged a bust, then looking capable under LaFleur in Green Bay — as the template for what [[Jalen Milroe]] would be if allowed to sit, and named Pittsburgh behind [[Aaron Rodgers]] as the landing spot he wants, arguing Milroe is already a better processor than [[Justin Fields]] was last year. He stressed that maturity and preparation are what separate a project from a lost cause, which is where he splits Milroe from an [[Anthony Richardson]] profile. [[Matt Waldman]] agreed and said the fantasy expression of this is buying the processing at a discount: he would wait until the third-to-fifth round of a rookie draft rather than pay for [[Cam Ward]].
- 2025-04-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-24]]): the ideal quarterback investment for a contender is a second- or third-round passer who sits behind a secure veteran for two or three years — the [[Jordan Love]] model — on a team with a strong offensive line and run game, preferably an athlete who can be deployed the way Philadelphia used [[Jalen Hurts]] or Baltimore used [[Lamar Jackson]]. He named Detroit behind [[Jared Goff]] as the fit, since he thinks [[Hendon Hooker]] is a backup at best, and noted the option value: a good preseason showing lets you trade him at a profit if you have long-term doubts. [[Brandon Angelo]] made the same argument for the Rams behind [[Matthew Stafford]], saying any quarterback benefits from learning the ins and outs at this level before playing, and that teams trade up for archetypes like [[Jalen Milroe]] because those do not appear every class.
- 2025-06-02 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-02]]): applying the framework to [[C.J. Stroud]], Waldman restates the two windows explicitly. Year one measures acclimation — whether a rookie remembers the plays, can make changes against base defenses, and can execute with the accuracy and recall his college tape showed when defenses are not throwing significant wrinkles at him. The question is simply 'does he look like he did in college.' Year two measures whether he can raise his level once defenses start throwing adjustments designed to foil the game plan and the quarterback specifically. Stroud passed window one and only partially passed window two, which is why Waldman treats neither season as a mirage and says year three becomes the real read on where he is and what he still needs.
- 2025-10-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-13]]): applied to [[Jackson Dart]], the caution is that we tend to get over our skis on rookie quarterbacks in the first five or six weeks, before defenses figure out what to throw at them. Waldman separated 'getting good starting quarterback results right now' from 'being a good starting quarterback,' pointing out that [[Drew Lock]], [[Baker Mayfield]] and Mitchell Trubisky all had good early stretches too. The other half of the window problem is opponent circumstance: the Eagles' plan to force Dart high in the pocket failed largely because their best edge defender was unavailable, so the tape flattered him more than the plan would have.
- 2025-11-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-11-11]]): nothing is guaranteed from year one to year two at quarterback, against the common assumption that a good rookie season implies an upward trajectory. His 2025 examples: [[Bo Nix]] has taken a step back, [[Jayden Daniels]] a smaller one, [[Kyler Murray]] plateaued and never really got better, and [[Bryce Young]] went bad rookie year, good second season, terrible third year. He applies this to [[Jackson Dart]] and argues the original sin was ownership retaining a lame-duck coach and GM and letting them make the pick — the Bears' recurring mistake — which now constrains the next hire to coaches willing to build around one specific quarterback archetype. Harmon also notes Coach of the Year for a year-one overperformer is a cursed award, citing Matt Nagy and Brian Daboll.
- 2025-12-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-12-04]]): the hosts used Jared Goff's development arc -- air raid college offense into a 'shit show,' then years of Sean McVay 'training wheels' before hitting his stride under Ben Johnson in year five or six -- as the yardstick for judging [[Caleb Williams]], who is attempting the same air-raid-to-complex-offense jump with far less runway. They argued judging Williams by completion percentage or box-score stats in year one under Ben Johnson misses the real story: cutting pre-snap penalties, managing situational football, and letting the offense grow year over year so that by year three the whole group recognizes coverages fast enough to make once-impossible plays look 'easy.'
