## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-07-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-29]]): Waldman previews Footballguys work correlating RSP catch-technique grades with fantasy tiers, using six years of top-75 finishers. Players finishing top-three in a 12-team league graded strong on attacking the football 94% of the time — correct hand position, tight hands, approach matched to the trajectory — with [[CeeDee Lamb]] the only elite finisher showing any lapses and no elite or WR1 finisher showing serial flaws. That good-hands rate fell to 82% at WR1, 58% at WR2 and 58% at WR3, while serial flaws (the [[Quentin Johnston]] clap-and-attack profile) went 0% at WR1, 8% at WR2 and 17% at WR3. Contact and tight-coverage catching mattered less than pure hand position. Tracking is the hardest trait to fix later — [[Davante Adams]] a rare exception — and Waldman's working theory is that players with a history of lapses regress more readily than those who never had them.
- 2024-07-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-29 (Feel It or F-It)]]): summarizing RSP research written up in a recent Gut Check column, Waldman says how a receiver tracks and attacks the football are two of the most important traits correlating to fantasy production. [[Davante Adams]] is a rare exception — a receiver with serial ball-tracking issues who worked past them and improved. [[Garrett Wilson]], by contrast, has no serial flaw but has shown lapses tracking and attacking the ball, which is why Waldman caps him at WR2 or low-end WR1 rather than elite, especially with [[Aaron Rodgers]] challenging him on contested targets. He applies the same lens positively to [[Tre Tucker]], whose pass-catching and attack technique were the flaws he has since addressed.
- 2024-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-29]]): drop counts are close to meaningless because the definition is unsettled — 'overemphasizing drops is kind of stupid because what is a drop, what isn't a drop, is up for debate,' and Harmon says the term persists mainly because a word exists for it. Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) sharpens the distinction as could versus should: on the [[Javon Baker]] seam ball from [[Drake Maye]], the receiver was at full speed with a defender in his hip pocket and had to lay out at full extension, so charting it as a drop purely because it touched both hands is wrong — had he been a step slower it would have been recorded as an errant throw. Koh concedes Baker had a separate legitimate drop in the same game, and Harmon notes some receivers hold themselves to the standard that anything touching their hands should be caught.
- 2024-10-08 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-08]]): using [[Dontayvion Wicks]] as the case study, Harmon separates three distinct failure modes that all get lumped together as 'bad hands' — outright concentration drops (the [[Diontae Johnson]] slant-clank type, of which Johnson had 14 in 2020), contested-catch and ball-tracking failures, and quarterback-receiver timing. He argues Wicks' real problem is the second and third, not the first: a 0% contested catch rate across three sources, hands clapping together rather than framing the ball, eyes upfield early, plus [[Jordan Love]] throwing him into contested spots that don't suit an intermediate over-the-middle player. Harmon thinks the confidence/focus component can resolve in-season with more volume, while genuine technique repair takes an offseason. He notes drops run in spells even for established players — [[Amari Cooper]] is in one now and has gone years without the issue, and [[Davante Adams]] had a 15.6% drop rate in his second season before becoming an elite route-runner.
- 2024-10-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-14]]): Waldman notes Greg Olsen on the broadcast describing an 'epidemic' of receivers who want to catch the ball close to their chest without attacking it — a point Waldman says has been his own bailiwick for about five years, and which he likens to bad tackling. He applied it in both directions this week: [[Bub Means]] earned praise for a very clean, sound attack on the football, while [[DK Metcalf]] was the negative case — a receiver with elite size, length and speed who 'plays like he has T-rex arms,' failed to attack on three plays against San Francisco, and cost Seattle at least one score. Waldman's framing is that first reaction to the ball is a technical skill, and an unsound one caps a physically elite player.
- 2024-11-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-11]]): Waldman uses [[Courtland Sutton]] as the running benchmark for a 'clap-attack' catcher — hands that trap rather than pluck, which costs his quarterback tough contested catches a WR1 should make. He grades [[Marquez Valdes-Scantling]] below that bar ('worse hands than Courtland Sutton or maybe [[Gabriel Davis]]'), which is why he expects roughly one deep ball in three to connect regardless of athletic ability. He separates hands from tracking: [[Van Jefferson]] has a specific deep-ball tracking problem Waldman nearly clipped for Twitter, whereas [[Mike Williams]] is an excellent tracker who benefits disproportionately from a lofted throw. And the trait is why a player changes position at all — [[Tyrone Tracy Jr.]] was moved from receiver to running back at Purdue, which Waldman saw coming from his Iowa tape.
- 2024-12-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-16]]): the line between a startable NFL receiver and a top-tier starter is winning the football in the air against tight coverage — and specifically doing it chest-to-the-defender, not only with your back to him. He uses [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] as the case study: converting a third of the contested plays he has missed would make him a top-15-to-20 receiver, half would make him top 10. He also warns that this is a coached skill, not an inherited one — Marvin Harrison Sr. was a Hall of Famer with poor ball-attack technique, an anomaly, and 'his dad isn't his coach.' By contrast he praises Nick Nash's catch-point toughness and technique as exceeding many NFL players'.
- 2025-01-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-13]]): struggling at the catch point is not automatically a fatal flaw, but it is a fatal flaw to being elite — Harmon says there is no elite NFL receiver who has that particular problem unresolved. He frames the skill as more than turning around and showing your numbers: [[Ladd McConkey]] keeps the window open by coming back to the football and attacking it correctly, which is why he wins in zone. [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] had this listed as a minor concern on his pre-draft Reception Perception profile and it became his biggest rookie-year issue. [[DK Metcalf]] is Harmon's example of a far more impressive physical specimen still fighting it years into his career, and [[Garrett Wilson]] of a player who makes spectacular catches but whose catch-point wonkiness caps him in tier two — with Harmon adding that there is no shame in tier two, since those players still go top-10 to top-15 in fantasy. [[Justin Jefferson]] is his proof it can be fixed: drops and contested issues in year two, now one of the best ball winners in the league.
- 2025-03-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-18 (Feel It or F-It Free Agency Winners and Losers)]]): Waldman lays out his catch-point taxonomy while contrasting two rookies. The failure modes he names for [[Tai Felton]] are clap attacks, using the wrong hand attack for the trajectory of the ball, allowing the ball into the frame rather than attacking it at its earliest point, failing to earn ideal position, letting the ball through the hands, and focus drops — he says any two of those would stall a receiver's development. The positive case, using [[Luther Burden III]], is timing and positioning: winning boundary targets over the shoulder with late separation and late hands. His warning is that acrobatic-looking catches often disguise a technique failure — a big receiver who gets to the ball late surrenders position to the defender and gives up his body, the functional equivalent of breaking back badly to the quarterback and inviting an undercut, but observers see only the athleticism.
- 2025-03-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-24]]): the dominant technical flaw across the 2025 receiver class is the 'clap attack' — hands too wide at the point of attack, so the receiver fights the ball or it flies between his hands. Waldman separates this from a tracking problem, which he considers a deeper issue: [[Quentin Johnston]] has a tracking problem, while [[Arian Smith]] does not and therefore has a far more correctable flaw. He also distinguishes attack selection (overhand vs underhand for the ball's trajectory), attacking at the earliest window of arrival, pullback technique to shield the defender, and jump-up-and-through timing on back-shoulder and underthrown balls, where being one or two steps late leaves a receiver leaning away from the ball with his chest and hands opened to the defender. He adds that leaving your feet unnecessarily costs a slot receiver acceleration downfield and often signals unconfident tracking. Clap attacks were flagged on [[Tetairoa McMillan]], [[Jack Bech]], [[Ja'Corey Brooks]], [[Elic Ayomanor]], [[Xavier Restrepo]] and others.
- 2025-04-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-21 (Film & Data with Dwain McFarland)]]): the distinction that drives his pass-catching points is lapses versus bad habits. A bad habit is not knowing which attack to use for the height of the ball — [[Quentin Johnston]] going underhand on targets above his chest, fighting the ball because he can't react fast enough to apply the right technique — and it must be rebuilt at the next level. A lapse is correct hand technique that breaks down on a specific difficult rep: looking away to get upfield, or hands coming apart on a target you had to extend wider for than expected. Waldman graded [[Jack Bech]] and [[Savion Williams]] as lapse cases and awarded the borderline points; [[Kyle Williams]] and [[Tai Felton]] he graded as habit cases. He also noted the athletic-looking back-shoulder catch made parallel to the ground is often evidence of no craft at all, because the receiver tracked the ball too late and had to leave his feet. Correction is possible but demands an [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]]-level daily program before and after practice; he cited Quincy Enunwa as the last player he saw genuinely fix his hands, and noted [[Michael Gallup]] had similar tendencies.
- 2025-05-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-05]]): a taxonomy of drops used to separate correctable from disqualifying. Focus drops are 'not a big deal' — a concentration issue from being too busy trying to get downfield and run, and coachable. Wild 'clap attacks' at the target signal the receiver is not tracking the ball fast enough to get his hands into position, which is more concerning. A third, intermediate case: a receiver who extends his arms with hands together to begin the target but opens his hands slightly before clamping on the ball, creating 'a fight of the football' — correctable, precisely because the arm extension mechanics are already right. What Waldman treats as the real red flag is using the hands in a position that is wrong for the ball's trajectory with coverage on. Applied here to [[Kyle Williams]].
- 2025-07-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-11]]): Harmon restates that he generally de-emphasizes drops, but draws a line at extremes and separates two distinct failures in his charting — a drop is not also logged as a failed contested-catch opportunity. [[Dontayvion Wicks]] is the extreme case: a 15.4% RP drop rate (second in RP history behind [[Romeo Doubs]]' rookie year) alongside a contested catch rate falling from 71.4% to 25%. Harmon's distinction is that simple mental drops can be excused, but tracking and attack issues at the catch point are a real developmental problem. Koh argues the two compound psychologically for a day-three receiver with limited reps, and Harmon agrees the yips can bleed into other parts of a player's game. Harmon also flags Green Bay's team-wide drop problem (second highest rate in the NFL, behind Cleveland) across Wicks, Doubs, [[Jayden Reed]], [[Christian Watson]] and now [[Matthew Golden]].
- 2025-07-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-28]]): drops split into focus-related and technique-related, and only the second is a structural problem. His technique marker is the 'clap attack' — a receiver extending toward the ball with hands apart rather than meeting together, which creates a chance to fight the football. Waldman says most of the league's less reliable catch-percentage receivers are clap attackers, and that it is fundamental enough it should be coached out before the college game. His explanation for why it survives: the biggest, fastest, strongest players thrive in spite of it, get scholarships and starting jobs on athleticism, are told constantly how good they are, and only confront the fundamentals in the pros when the starting job comes under jeopardy. [[Diontae Johnson]] is his example of a player who eventually had to; [[Adonai Mitchell]] is the current case, and Waldman says Mitchell has not acknowledged it yet. He contrasts [[Keon Coleman]], whose drops he grades as focus, not technique.
