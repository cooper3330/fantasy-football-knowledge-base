## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-07-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-29]]): Waldman previews Footballguys work correlating RSP catch-technique grades with fantasy tiers, using six years of top-75 finishers. Players finishing top-three in a 12-team league graded strong on attacking the football 94% of the time — correct hand position, tight hands, approach matched to the trajectory — with [[CeeDee Lamb]] the only elite finisher showing any lapses and no elite or WR1 finisher showing serial flaws. That good-hands rate fell to 82% at WR1, 58% at WR2 and 58% at WR3, while serial flaws (the [[Quentin Johnston]] clap-and-attack profile) went 0% at WR1, 8% at WR2 and 17% at WR3. Contact and tight-coverage catching mattered less than pure hand position. Tracking is the hardest trait to fix later — [[Davante Adams]] a rare exception — and Waldman's working theory is that players with a history of lapses regress more readily than those who never had them.
- 2024-07-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-29 (Feel It or F-It)]]): summarizing RSP research written up in a recent Gut Check column, Waldman says how a receiver tracks and attacks the football are two of the most important traits correlating to fantasy production. [[Davante Adams]] is a rare exception — a receiver with serial ball-tracking issues who worked past them and improved. [[Garrett Wilson]], by contrast, has no serial flaw but has shown lapses tracking and attacking the ball, which is why Waldman caps him at WR2 or low-end WR1 rather than elite, especially with [[Aaron Rodgers]] challenging him on contested targets. He applies the same lens positively to [[Tre Tucker]], whose pass-catching and attack technique were the flaws he has since addressed.
- 2024-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-29]]): drop counts are close to meaningless because the definition is unsettled — 'overemphasizing drops is kind of stupid because what is a drop, what isn't a drop, is up for debate,' and Harmon says the term persists mainly because a word exists for it. Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) sharpens the distinction as could versus should: on the [[Javon Baker]] seam ball from [[Drake Maye]], the receiver was at full speed with a defender in his hip pocket and had to lay out at full extension, so charting it as a drop purely because it touched both hands is wrong — had he been a step slower it would have been recorded as an errant throw. Koh concedes Baker had a separate legitimate drop in the same game, and Harmon notes some receivers hold themselves to the standard that anything touching their hands should be caught.
