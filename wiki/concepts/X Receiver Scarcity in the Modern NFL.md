# X Receiver Scarcity in the Modern NFL

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-03-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-21]]): asked by co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) where all the X receivers have gone — Koh says ten years ago every team seemed to have one and now nearly every team they discuss needs one — Harmon offers a structural explanation. Many players who were X receivers a decade ago were never ideal target-funnel options; they did not separate enough. As pass volume has risen even with the run game revitalized, offenses need receivers who can handle more volume and create efficient passing windows rather than low-percentage contested plays, so those old-profile X types would flunk out today and instead get kicked inside or moved around the formation. The consequence is a league full of power slots and a shortage of true man-coverage-beating perimeter players, which Harmon cites on nearly every team in the episode: Patriots, Raiders, Chargers, Cowboys, Titans and Panthers. He notes the same tension in the draft class — [[Emeka Egbuka]] is a favorite but mostly a slot, and [[Jayden Higgins]] is liked by others as an X while Harmon would kick him inside.
- 2025-03-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-26]]): Harmon defines a family of 'better than you think at separating' X receivers, peaked by [[Michael Thomas]] with [[Drake London]] second, and including [[Michael Pittman Jr.]], [[Courtland Sutton]] and — as an outlier freak at the very top — [[Mike Evans]]. The point is that these boundary receivers are commonly mislabelled as pure contested-catch ball winners when their real value is quietly winning go routes and breaking routes. He places [[Tetairoa McMillan]] in the Pittman/Sutton band rather than the London band. Koh (not tracked) adds that the league has a dearth of X receivers generally, noting Detroit has been searching for one for years, which is why a slot-first player landing on such a team could end up functioning as a number one.
- 2025-04-04 — According to James Koh (co-host, not a tracked expert) on ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-04]]): the league is 'desperate for an X receiver', which is why prototypical measurables like [[Jayden Higgins]]' 6-4, 215 frame, 4.43 speed, 39-inch vertical and 6-7 wingspan will get him drafted and deployed as a boundary X regardless of what the charting says — Koh estimates only four or five NFL teams would share [[Matt Harmon]]'s preference to kick him inside. Harmon accepts the scarcity but argues it drives teams into predictable mistakes, citing [[Keon Coleman]]'s pure-X rookie usage in Buffalo. He also uses it to sort landing spots: Houston already has a true X in [[Nico Collins]], so Higgins would be moved closer to the line of scrimmage there rather than stranded on the boundary.
- 2025-04-08 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-08]]): Kyle Shanahan's system is an apex environment for an X receiver because it feeds him in-breaking routes rather than the static, straight-line boundary tree other offenses hand the X. [[Julio Jones]]'s 2016 chart is the proof — only 0.8% screens and 5.6% flats against 9.3% slants, 14.9% digs and 10.8% posts, with 82%, 78.4% and 74% success on those three. Harmon draws the same line to [[Nico Collins]] in Bobby Slowik's offense and [[Brandon Aiyuk]] when healthy under Shanahan, arguing this is why those situations should be obsessed over. Koh (co-host, not tracked) frames Jones as the bridge player between the 2010s big-bodied boundary X who won contested outside routes and the modern in-breaking X, and argues his athleticism, hands, press-beating and zone-reading would have played in any era.
- 2025-04-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-14]]): he attributes [[Jayden Higgins]]'s top-40 buzz almost entirely to scarcity rather than grade — Higgins is 'one of the few true X wide receivers in this class,' and the NFL likes the prototype enough that size plus adequate speed for that size plus contested-catch ability pulls him up boards. Waldman is explicit that this is a prototype premium: on his own board Higgins is WR6 and better valued as a late-second or third-round pick, since his release plan and footwork are unrefined. The general lesson he draws is that thin supply at a scheme-required archetype systematically inflates draft capital relative to evaluation.
- 2025-04-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-04-28]]): the episode repeatedly shows how few true X receivers exist and how much damage forcing one costs. Carolina had to shove [[Xavier Legette]] to the X in the back half of last season and his rookie year fell apart from that point; [[Tetairoa McMillan]] arriving fixes two spots at once. Houston already has [[Nico Collins]] at X, which is precisely why [[Jayden Higgins]] is a good landing-spot outcome rather than a bad one. In Green Bay, co-host James Koh (not tracked) argues the actual need is an X who wins against man and press and that [[Matthew Golden]] checks only the speed box, while Harmon replies that an alpha X is 'easier said than done' to find at pick 23 from a team that never trades up, so the better question is what the room can add — here, verticality and in-breakers.
- 2025-05-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-07]]): the episode's recurring thread is how few teams have a real X receiver and how that shapes signings. Harmon raised the same limiter twice — for [[Diontae Johnson]] and [[Elijah Moore]] — that a smaller receiver may get open on deep outside throws but few quarterbacks are willing and able to make the throw; the last time Moore played with one was [[Joe Flacco]] in 2021, and [[Josh Allen]] is one of the exceptions. Cleveland's answer is to shuffle alignments, with [[Cedric Tillman]] as the true backside X. Buffalo's problem is that nobody wins on the boundary, which Koh (not tracked) argued makes Moore's out and comeback routes valuable for moving the chains. Seattle's desperation at X is the stated reason the team floated a rookie tight end there, with [[Marquez Valdes-Scantling]] the incumbent and [[Tory Horton]] the hoped-for answer.
- 2025-05-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-09]]): the episode is effectively a survey of how many contenders have no true X receiver. Dallas came out of the draft with an unserious room behind [[CeeDee Lamb]] and had to trade a third-round pick for [[George Pickens]] to get a boundary winner; Tennessee's X job would otherwise fall to [[Van Jefferson]] or force [[Calvin Ridley]] out of the role that worked for him; Kansas City's top three of [[Xavier Worthy]], [[Marquise Brown]] and [[Rashee Rice]] contains no big ball-winning X, so Harmon argues UDFA [[Elijah Badger]] may be their best candidate; and Miami has nobody of that shape behind [[Tyreek Hill]] and [[Jaylen Waddle]], which is why [[Andrew Armstrong]] fits on paper. Harmon's point is that the archetype is scarce enough that teams overpay for it in trade or reach for projects, and that identifying an X-shaped body in a room without one is a repeatable way to find opportunity late.
- 2025-05-14 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-14]]): Harmon says an 80/80 player — roughly 80% of snaps outside and 80% on the line of scrimmage — is a rare and punishing profile, because that deployment is static, you never get hidden from the top corner and you are easy to bracket. [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] was used that way as a rookie (79.7% outside, just over 80% on the line) and was double-covered on 10.5% of routes, the most of any rookie charted. The only players who can carry a whole offense from that alignment, in his view, are unicorns like [[Nico Collins]] and [[Davante Adams]] — and neither was that as a rookie; [[Brandon Aiyuk]] and [[Rashad Bateman]] chart similarly without the offense running through them, while [[Mike Evans]] and [[Drake London]] are big X archetypes who still get moved around. Conversely, Harmon says a genuine X receiver lifts an offense's ceiling — Allen Robinson, [[Tee Higgins]], A.J. Green, [[Terry McLaurin]] — and that is the role he wants [[Rome Odunze]] to hold in Chicago, with [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] the rare rookie who could simply be an X immediately on traits.
- 2025-05-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-21]]): Harmon applies the scarcity argument to two rooms in the same episode. In Buffalo he says the post-[[Stefon Diggs]] build has produced a pile of useful pieces — [[Khalil Shakir]], [[Josh Palmer]], [[Elijah Moore]], [[Keon Coleman]] — but no perimeter player who can dominate targets, and that Coleman only justifies the boundary X role if he becomes a dominant contested-catch winner, which his 60% contested rate says he is not. In Kansas City he says the room would make far more sense with one real X: [[Rashee Rice]] does not separate against press man, [[Xavier Worthy]] cannot, Marquise Brown cannot at this stage, and [[Jalen Royals]] was not built for it either — so the position defaults to Hollywood Brown in a role Harmon would not choose for him. He explicitly says even a returning [[Tyreek Hill]] is not the missing piece; what he wants is a big-bodied outside receiver who consistently separates against press man.
- 2025-05-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-23]]): a pure X receiver profile is viable but narrow, and Harmon uses [[Adonai Mitchell]] as the extreme case — elite slants, outs and curls with a functional nine route, but a 14th-percentile zone grade that makes any slot or flanker usage a waste. He points out that the historical comps sitting in that zone-coverage neighborhood split hard: busts like Jalen Reagor, Kelvin Benjamin and [[Kadarius Toney]] on one side, 2015 [[Mike Evans]], 2018 A.J. Green, 2020 Mike Williams and 2024 [[DK Metcalf]] on the other — none of them career-best seasons, but all pure X's, and all except Mike Williams elite-to-very-good man beaters. Harmon's caution is that no single separation number defines a player; the route tree's balance and the alignment context have to be read together, which is why Reception Perception is lower on Mitchell than other separation-tracking services.
- 2025-06-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-04]]): Harmon reprises the show's earlier X-receiver-needs list — Buffalo, New England, Tennessee, Kansas City, Las Vegas and San Francisco (if [[Brandon Aiyuk]] is unavailable) — as the reason a surplus receiver in Green Bay has real trade value, naming [[Romeo Doubs]] and [[Dontayvion Wicks]] as candidates worth a late-round pick if [[Christian Watson]] returns healthy. He applies the same scarcity lens to Arizona, where moving [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] off the line only works if somebody else can win the downfield concepts, and to Las Vegas, where the absence of a size-speed outside receiver is what limits Chip Kelly's amorphous plan.
- 2025-07-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-25]]): both hosts frame the AFC West rooms around who can actually win as an X. James Koh (not tracked) argues that even if everyone on a roster breaks inside, someone eventually has to win along the boundary — the 15-to-20-yard corner route that beats cover two — and that this is the Chargers' roster-construction risk, since he sees only [[Ladd McConkey]] providing it and [[Tre Harris]]'s sideline work did not stand out. Harmon applies the same lens to Las Vegas, where [[Jack Bech]] and [[Jakobi Meyers]] are flanker-slot types and two tight ends dominate, leaving the raw [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]] as the only vertical X archetype on the roster. In Kansas City the same gap appears: [[Rashee Rice]] wins horizontally, so [[Xavier Worthy]] has to carry the vertical load.
- 2025-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-06]]): the AFC East preview is largely a study in missing X receivers. New England is running an open competition at the spot that [[Terry McLaurin]] would win on arrival, with [[Kayshon Boutte]] the likely default starter and [[Kyle Williams]] a longer-term bet. Buffalo, per Harmon, has five usable receivers and no superstar, and has simply not spent the last few years trying to find a man-beating X — [[Keon Coleman]] plays the role without the separation to hold it. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) frames the stakes in postseason terms: on third and long you need someone who wins a one-on-one, and he does not believe [[Josh Allen]] has that player on the roster. Harmon calls it a fair criticism, saying Buffalo lost that in [[Stefon Diggs]] and is now trying to 'create a wide receiver one in the aggregate', which he thinks works for a top-three regular-season offense but is unproven in nut-cutting time.
- 2025-10-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-10-09]]): Harmon offered a quick-and-dirty way to identify alignment roles — the X is outside on the line of scrimmage and therefore cannot move, so the receiver with the lowest slot percentage is usually the X; a 70/30 or 65/35 split marks the Z or flanker. He used it to argue teams keep miscasting players into a role few can play: [[Jerry Jeudy]] is Cleveland's only X candidate by default at 9.6% slot despite his lowest-in-class press number, [[Cedric Tillman]] had been doing that job even though Harmon thinks Tillman is more naturally an X and was moved to Z for blocking, and the Raiders insisted their X had to be a size-speed prototype rather than scheming around [[Jack Bech]]. He also sees Detroit trying to build [[Isaac TeSlaa]] into a big-bodied X.
- 2025-11-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-11-06]]): some receivers should be given exactly one job. He names [[Adonai Mitchell]] as one of the few players in the league he wants to see play nowhere except X — boundary, at the numbers or the sideline, on the line of scrimmage, no multi-alignment movement — because his zone route running is a mess (14th-percentile success rate vs zone as a rookie, 31st as a prospect, ~72% in his single charted 2025 game) while he wins above 70% vs man and 87% vs press. [[George Pickens]], Harmon's original prospect comp for him, validates the path: Pickens is having a fantastic season in Dallas while playing essentially nothing but X, with one game above a 15% slot rate all year. The corollary is that teams whose primary receiver now moves around the formation — [[Garrett Wilson]] with the Jets, [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] in Seattle — leave a specific boundary vacancy that a limited but combative press-beater can fill.
