# Prospect Pro-Readiness vs Ceiling

## Definition

The distinction [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] draw between how good a
player will eventually be (**ceiling**) and how much of his game already
transfers on day one (**pro-readiness**). The two come apart routinely, and
they are priced as if they don't — which is where rookie-draft and
[[Best Ball]] rookie-ADP value appears.

Angelo's compressed version of the same idea: a player can be "not as good of a
player as he is a prospect."

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]: the framing case, [[Malik Nabers]] vs [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]]. "Nabers is NFL-ready on all three levels of the field. Marvin Harrison Jr. is NFL-ready." He grants Harrison the higher ceiling and expects both to be top-10 NFL receivers within five years, but expects Nabers to produce first — 1,100+ yards and 10+ touchdowns as a rookie — while Harrison has "more of a learning curve."

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the mechanism: pro-readiness is mostly about how much of the route tree and alignment chart a rookie can be handed immediately. Nabers can be moved "in the backfield, in the slot, out wide, at Z" from day one; Harrison is "not the mover that Nabers is, which is going to limit his route tree initially," and should be given slot reps early to acclimate rather than being asked to beat NFL No. 1 corners one-on-one from the jump.

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode, the corollary that makes this a *buying* rule rather than a ranking: he separates "concern" from "concern relative to the buzz." His stated objection to the [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] discourse is not the player — "he's a very good prospect" — but that people talk about him "as if he is already there." Both hosts land on Nabers over Harrison "by a healthy margin," and Waldman notes NFL.com's **Chad Reuter** (not a tracked expert) independently gave him the same ordering.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, his pro-readiness checklist for receivers, given as the evidence behind the Harrison call. Three technique items he treats as gating day-one production: **(1) release work** — opening the chest to a press defender instead of using a footwork release, which gets a receiver pinned to the boundary; **(2) catch-window selection** — the best receivers choose *when* in the ball's flight to attack it (Chris Olave as his positive example: sometimes the earliest window, sometimes deliberately the latest so the defender passes under the ball), where a rookie tends to have one default; **(3) attack position** — overhand vs underhand. A receiver whose reflexive default is to catch underhand lets the ball into his frame, invites break-ups in tight coverage, and drops passes he shouldn't. Waldman's stated exception is Terry McLaurin, who catches underhand "rampantly" and is fine; the historical exception is Marvin Harrison Sr., who Waldman says trapped nearly everything to his chest and famously did not drop it. His rule: "you afford yourself a little leeway if you do anything at 99th percentile" — most players need optimal technique, and these items are correctable rather than disqualifying.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman and Angelo**, same episode, on what pro-readiness looks like from the other side: [[Malik Nabers]]'s year-over-year improvement was "more so just a consistency standpoint — going from inconsistent to consistent." Angelo calls him the most consistent receiver in college football in 2023 and says he should have won the Biletnikoff. Waldman's version: "a massive vocabulary of footwork maneuvers and handwork maneuvers that he applies at a very savvy level for this stage" — i.e. the accumulated technique *is* the pro-readiness.

- 2024-02-01 — [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-01]]: a full episode of worked examples at the extreme ends of the gap. [[Joe Milton III]] is the clearest ceiling-without-readiness case — top-five-pick arm talent paired with a floor of "not draftable at all," where Waldman's explicit prescription is the "Jordan Love treatment": don't play him until the readiness catches up, because rushing an unready high-ceiling arm (his Malik Willis comp) stalls the readiness gap from ever closing. [[Brevyn Spann-Ford]] is the mirror case — modest ceiling, but a readiness gap visibly closing in real time across a single season (2022 to late 2023) as blocking technique and pass-pro comfort caught up to his physical tools, the clearest single-player "watch the light bulb turn on" example in this wiki.

- 2024-02-22 — [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-22]] [Dynasty]: the running-back version of the readiness checklist, given as a direct counterpart to what's hard to develop (logged on [[Pace Control and Movement Intellect]]). What both hosts consider genuinely learnable on the job: **processing "the speed of the game"** — an experience-based trait that several athletes describe as "the game slowed down" between year one and year two, not purely physical; **situational/game management** — understanding down-and-distance well enough to take the two-yard gain a play design actually offers rather than pressing for a home run and putting the offense in second-and-15; and **pass protection fundamentals** — Waldman's explicit take is that most NFL backs already have the physical tools to pass block, so what's missing early is positioning and assignment knowledge, which is coachable. The one carve-out inside pass pro that Waldman does *not* consider easily learnable: **throwing a punch without dropping your head** while staying upright against a much bigger rusher — realistic outcomes for most backs top out at simply holding off a rusher as long as possible, with true elite pass-protecting backs like Edgerrin James or what Alvin Kamara (not tracked pages) has developed into at times treated as the rare exception rather than a coachable baseline.

- 2024-03-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], solo cast on the 2024 WR class A-Z ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-16]]): restates why he grades in tiers rather than strict 1-to-N rankings, using two hypothetical grades as the worked example — an 88 (an "instant starter," a couple points from Pro Bowl caliber) vs. a 90 (a projected "production leader" and immediate starter). If the 90-grade player lands on a team that already has [[Ja'Marr Chase]] and [[Tee Higgins]], he may not start at all as a rookie, while the 88-grade player who lands in a scheme built to minimize his weaknesses can outproduce his grade. Names [[Puka Nacua]] landing in [[Cooper Kupp]]'s vacated role with the Rams as the example of scheme/fit accelerating a player's on-field growth beyond his pre-draft grade. "Rankings suck" as a strict ordinal tool for this reason — "you don't have to try and be a surgeon with these rankings" — and tiers are meant to let readers weight fit and scheme match within a tier rather than treat one number as gospel. Also restates the [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] case (his #1 2023 receiver grade would rank #8 in the much deeper 2024 class) as proof this isn't the same as saying a player "sucks" — JSN still graded well on film despite limited fantasy production behind [[Tyler Lockett]] and DK Metcalf.

- 2024-03-28 — [[Matt Waldman]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-28 (Going Deep - If We Were NFL GMs)]]): applies the framework as the reason [[Drake Maye]] lands on his "do not draft" list for a team needing an immediate starter — coins "robo quarterback" for a prospect who checks every pedigree/physical box (program, yardage, completion percentage, frame, arm) that GMs use to justify optimism, while missing the harder pro-readiness items: pinpoint accuracy, confident coverage processing, pre/post-snap diagnosis, sensing edge pressure, and efficient pocket management. Ceiling comp is still Jordan Love, but only with a real bench runway to develop those items — the resume bullet points are not the same thing as being ready.

- 2024-03-30 — [[Matt Waldman]], solo cast on the 2024 TE class A-Z ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-30]]): restates the position-specific version of the framework — "opportunity precedes talent" for rookie tight ends more than any other position, because most NFL teams require in-line blocking that highly drafted "move" tight ends often can't do right away. Names [[Brock Bowers]] and [[Cade Stover]] the class's most pro-ready if their landing scheme uses them as receivers/H-backs rather than in-line blockers, [[Ja'Tavion Sanders]] and [[Ben Sinnott]] close behind, and [[Theo Johnson]] and [[Tip Reiman]] as prospects who could handle extended in-line work but likely at a cost to receiving upside. Historical pattern cited as evidence: rookie-year TE production tracks scheme fit more than draft capital — mid-tier-talent rookies with the right opportunity (Foster Moreau, Cade Otton, Dawson Knox) have out-produced higher-drafted, more physically gifted classmates (T.J. Hockenson, Noah Fant, Irv Smith Jr.) as rookies, while true burst cases like [[Sam LaPorta]], [[Dalton Kincaid]] and [[Kyle Pitts]] required both talent and a scheme willing to deploy them as receivers first.

- 2024-04-11 — [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-11 (Going Deep - Fave Evals, Coach-Killer Candidates, and High-End Career Outcomes)]]): a related framing they call "separating evaluation from valuation" — grading a prospect's full **range of outcomes** rather than defending a single right/wrong verdict. Worked example: [[Braelon Allen]] — Waldman can say "I don't like a lot about his game, but I can understand how he could be one of the three most productive backs in his class," and Angelo contrasts [[Caleb Williams]]'s narrow range of outcomes with Allen's wide one (out of the league in two years, or Pro Bowl-caliber in two years, both plausible). Angelo frames this class in particular as unusually deep in the middle tier — many prospects graded within 2% of each other on talent — which makes scenario/opportunity/scheme fit, not talent gap, the deciding factor in who actually hits.

**How to use this:** when a rookie's ADP is built on ceiling language ("best
prospect in years"), check pro-readiness separately — release work, catch-window
variety, attack position, and how many alignments he can be trusted in. The two
prospects here are the worked example, and the call is checkable against their
actual rookie seasons.
- 2024-10-07 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-07]]): Waldman publicly logged a miss on his own principle — he preaches all year round not to let a less-than-ideal landing spot scare you away from really good receivers, because they tend to rise above it, and he cited a thousand recent examples. Then he let Las Vegas' circumstances cloud his view of [[Brock Bowers]]' talent and ended up underexposed. He treats the episode as a lesson worth naming out loud. The paired observation, from the same show, is the recency-bias version of the same error: [[Ja'Marr Chase]] and [[Khalil Shakir]] both went through public stretches where people decided they could not catch, and talent won out in the end.
- 2024-12-03 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-03]]): a distinction between upside and theoretical upside. 'I think we get caught up in the industry on the theoretical upside of a player who, like, look at the size and look at the measurables, imagine if he puts it all together — well, I've never seen him put it all together, so I don't even know what that looks like. You're just talking about the idea of an idea.' Harmon's preference is for the upside you have actually observed: he ranks [[Ricky Pearsall]] first on upside among the second-tier 2024 rookies because he has seen the fully maximised version of Pearsall's skill set, and ranks [[Xavier Legette]] last because projecting him as a plus-plus route runner who separates downfield asks you to imagine something never shown in college or the NFL. He applies the same lens to [[Keon Coleman]], where the ball-winning highs are real but the instinct question is unresolved.
- 2024-12-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-19 (NFL QB Lunacy)]]): quarterbacks arrive more pro-ready than they used to because offenses have been dumbed down, and Waldman relays Kurt Warner's recurring argument that pure progression reads are ruining development — simplified offenses speed a quarterback onto the field and make him look good optically, but he never builds the full mental game. Waldman's own gloss: it keeps the head coach's and coordinator's jobs longer, and it only delays the dip that comes when defenses stop letting a quarterback play simple. He contrasts that with Aaron Rodgers, who learned probability through Strat-O-Matic as a kid and spent an hour a day with Jeff Tedford at Cal doing a labeled checkers game — lining up formations and defenses and explaining motion, shifts and blocking — development that happened away from the facility, not from an NFL coach. Harstad (not tracked) partly pushes back: there is an 'old man yells at clouds' dimension to Warner and Tom Brady's version of this, and if receivers are better protected that is good regardless of whether it makes the quarterback's job easier — but he agrees on the principal-agent problem between a coach's short-term job security and a quarterback's long-term growth.
- 2025-01-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-23]]): Waldman puts numbers on how much thinner the 2025 receiver class is than 2024. Last year he scored 20 receivers at 85 or above — the highest-scoring class he has ever graded and, he says, unheard of even across all positions. This year he has about six players within striking range of 85, with roughly 20 scored in the 80-84 band: players who in today's league end up on benches, can contribute situationally, but have a clear hole, need a clearly defined role, play only one or two spots, and may need a year or two before they become occasional starters ([[Jauan Jennings]] and [[Noah Brown]] as the low end, Robert Woods as the upside version of that developmental path). He resists the lazy 'this class sucked' framing: plenty of these players become contributors in three to four years, and one to three always surprise. [[Brandon Angelo]] agrees the only day-one primary starter is [[Travis Hunter]], and uses [[Ladd McConkey]] — a 1,000-yard rookie — as the production bar nobody in this class obviously clears.
- 2025-02-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-28]]): a receiver who takes over 70 percent of his college snaps on one side of the field, on a stripped-down route tree, is a long-standing red flag that he is developmental rather than pro-ready — regardless of his athletic wins. Harmon ran the Reception Perception prospect database list of that profile: Kevin White, Corey Coleman, Josh Doctson, Laquon Treadwell, Dede Westbrook, James Washington, [[Cedric Tillman]] (possible hit), [[Quentin Johnston]] — against a short list of successes in [[JuJu Smith-Schuster]] and [[Rashee Rice]] (both of whom kicked into the slot), [[Drake London]] at 80.5 percent left wide receiver, and [[D.J. Moore]] at 79.3 percent, who still took time as a gadget player first. He applied it to [[Javon Baker]] (70.7 percent left outside, 35.6 percent nine routes, 22.4 percent curls) as proof Baker was never an instant-impact prospect, and paired it with [[Ja'Lynn Polk]] as a case of a staff misusing a player against his college role.
- 2025-03-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-03]]) [Dynasty]: Waldman laid out the safety-versus-upside trade at tight end directly — [[Mason Taylor]] is the safest and best option because he wins against man and zone, catches the ball reliably and offers in-line blocking promise, while [[Elijah Arroyo]] carries vertical speed and more upside, and Waldman conceded a fair argument exists to simply wait and roll the dice on Arroyo since the gap is not large. His caveat is that upside guys have to actually hit the upside mark. He also stated a positional rule of thumb: the more you want a tight end to threaten deep, the more likely you are really looking at a bulked-up wide receiver, and what you actually want is crispness in the short and intermediate ranges with run-after-catch ability.
- 2025-04-03 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-03]]) [Dynasty]: with the gap between RB2 and RB12 in the 2025 class thin, the floors of those backs are similar, so paying a first-round cost for ceiling is the expensive half of the decision — drafting [[Omarion Hampton]] in the first round forces him to be serviceable from day one and creates bust risk, while [[Damien Martinez]] two-plus rounds later is arguably the more pro-ready starter today. Angelo quantified the fantasy side of falling draft capital: first-round backs have averaged roughly 15.5-16.5 points per game versus 6.5-7.5 for fifth and sixth rounders. [[Matt Waldman]] added the counterweight that NFL teams shy away from very-good-everywhere prospects with no elite, surface-level skill ([[Tahj Brooks]], Hampton), which is exactly what pushes third-round talents into the sixth and makes them fourth-round rookie-draft bargains.
- 2025-04-07 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-07]]): Waldman separates the 2025 quarterback class explicitly along the readiness/ceiling axis — [[Jalen Milroe]] has the highest upside, [[Cam Ward]] the lowest floor, and [[Shedeur Sanders]] sits in the middle as the safest bet because his techniques, footwork, drops and timing routes are already professional. The underlying warning is that college-proven off-script ability does not automatically transfer: a quarterback consumed with repairing foundational technique in the NFL may lose the improvisation that made him attractive, and may fall out of sync with what timing routes demand. He applies a milder version to [[Will Howard]] and [[Hunter Dekkers]], both filed as developmental projects with journeyman-starter outcomes rather than ready contributors.
- 2025-09-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-04 (Going Deep Rookie Receivers and RB Depth Charts)]]): the hosts separated a rookie's eventual ceiling from what he can do in year one against the league's best 32 starting corners. Angelo's evaluation of [[Tetairoa McMillan]] was that he is best used as a No. 2 to start, building brick by brick — Carolina's situation forcing him into the alpha role does not accelerate the skill development. Waldman added that projecting success against college corners is different from beating NFL ones, and that [[Davante Adams]] by his own estimation needed two to three years to solve release technique, so it is unlikely McMillan or [[Tre Harris]] solved it in a few months of camp. The inverse case is [[Travis Hunter]]: because Jacksonville is deploying him in a defined Chris Godwin role, Waldman called that a huge endorsement of him playing near his ceiling in year one.
