## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-02-27 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-27]]): the limiting factor after a ligament or tendon repair is not the structure but the neurology. Surgeons in 2025 reliably reconstruct an ACL or Achilles, but what cannot be replicated is the pathway of millions of neurons carrying information to those tendons, ligaments and supporting muscles — a player severs 20-plus years of accumulated movement inventory. Angelo has seen explosive athletes unable to walk upstairs without a hitch for eight months post-Achilles rupture despite a physiologically sound repair, and notes the deficit shows up at speed: not walking down a hallway at two miles an hour but escaping 'human bears' at fifteen and delivering an accurate boundary throw. He extends it positively as well — mastery is the same mechanism, the pathway widening from a one-lane road to a five-lane highway, which is why running backs come into tune around 26-27 ([[Saquon Barkley]]'s age) and why the 10,000-hours idea is really about neurologic inventory rather than hours logged. [[Matt Waldman]] analogizes it to moving a motherboard. Applied to [[Kurtis Rourke]] and cited against [[Jonathan Brooks]]'s re-tear.
- 2026-05-07 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-05-07 (Going Deep with Brandon Angelo)]]): drawing on his athletic-performance/rehab background, Angelo explained that a full Achilles rupture is fundamentally different from tendinitis or tendinopathy because it severs the neurologic connection the body uses to code force and rate of ground contact -- the athlete effectively has to relearn how to hit the ground at speed. Rupture location matters: injuries higher up near the gastrocnemius insertion heal cleaner with less debris than those closer to the ankle/joint, but the neurologic relearning process is complex regardless of location. He said some high-level athletes don't feel fully comfortable again until the third year post-rupture, and specifically that a 33-year-old, 250-pound NFL athlete (referencing [[George Kittle]]) faces a much tougher recovery than a lighter athlete; he cited [[J.K. Dobbins]] as a cautionary example of a player who returned productive but without his prior explosiveness. Angelo also noted, half-jokingly, that high processed-sugar intake actively slows tendon and tissue repair by diverting the body's resources away from healing.
- 2026-05-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-05-28 (Going Deep)]]): the hosts laid out why ACL/Achilles recovery is a motor-relearning problem, not just a healing-tissue one -- return-to-play (as early as roughly week 8 for a comparable injury) and return-to-prior-performance (commonly another full calendar year, per players' own accounts of not feeling right until year two or three) are very different thresholds. Waldman compared rebuilt neurological sequencing to a broken paper chain that has to be relinked one loop at a time rather than in bulk, and flagged a specific trap for high-processing players like [[Jonathan Brooks]] and [[Malik Nabers]]: because they notice detail faster than most, they also notice more acutely when their body doesn't feel right, which can tip into an overthinking, near-neurotic hurdle separate from the physical recovery itself. Case study cited: [[J.K. Dobbins]], called the 'gold standard' for accepting a redefined, less-explosive archetype rather than chasing his old one.
- 2026-08-06 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-08-06]]), discussing [[Malik Nabers]]' multiligament knee recovery: surgical trauma is a massive neurological event, not just a physical one -- affecting the mind-body connection, nervous system timing, and coordinative elements -- and the body doesn't simply return to full contact-recovery capacity once symptoms clear. Angelo uses a mono-recovery analogy (weeks of profound fatigue from minor exertion even after symptoms resolve) to describe why 'looking good cutting in shorts' in camp clips doesn't reflect stability under real contact. Both cite Adrian Peterson's recovery as the gold-standard outlier that other severe-ligament comebacks are measured against and typically fall short of, and project Nabers needs into 2027 before he's fully back to himself.
