# Separating the Player from the Person

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-10 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and co-host Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-10 (Regression Candidates and Exuberance-Pessimism)]]): prompted by Brett Favre's birthday, both argue you can evaluate on-field contribution separately from the person, while refusing to flatten the ethics. Harstad's central case is Jim Tyrer, the most All-Pros of any player not in the Hall of Fame, excluded because he killed his wife and himself — while OJ Simpson, Marvin Harrison Sr. and Ray Lewis, all with murder allegations of varying credibility, are enshrined; he notes Tyrer's act is the one most plausibly linked to CTE, which makes football washing its hands of it hypocritical. Harstad also argues that in an environment with selective pressures as intense as the NFL, the traits people call flaws are usually adaptive: Odell Beckham Jr.'s diva reputation, Barry Sanders' understatement, Amon-Ra St. Brown's grudge list of names read before games. Both reject asking the NFL to serve as the punitive body in place of the legal system, since outrage is unevenly distributed. Waldman's summary: people are complicated.
- 2025-01-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-13]]): character intel can override film almost instantly. Harmon charted [[Jermaine Burton]]'s first few games, asked a trusted contact why the player was not more highly regarded, and got a two-second answer — character reject, can't touch him — which he treats as a huge concern precisely because it arrived that fast. His rule is that when someone shows you who they are, believe them the first time. [[Matt Waldman]] adds that NFL teams employ investigators for exactly this, and that what you learn about a person can matter as much as or more than anything on tape; he also offers a self-aware bias note that when he falls hard for a receiver there is roughly a 45 percent chance of major off-field trouble, citing Josh Gordon and his first such love, Michael Westbrook. Waldman separately declines to extend the same judgment to [[Javon Baker]], arguing the concerns there trace more to his father than to the player.
- 2025-01-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-23]]): Waldman's operating rule for off-field write-offs is that once a talented player has been given several deliberate second chances — including the tell of a team leaking things to the media to put him on notice — he would rather be pleasantly surprised later than keep holding the roster spot. He applies it to [[Jermaine Burton]], noting the pre-draft concerns he did not hear at the time have been confirmed in hindsight. He is careful to separate cases: he declines to put [[Javon Baker]] in the same category because Baker's issues may be 'more daddy than it is son', i.e. external family influence. [[Brandon Angelo]] frames the whole class of cases as less about talent and more about whether the player can be a professional athlete, and notes Burton's situation is trending toward legal action in multiple cases.
- 2025-04-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-03]]): he deliberately excludes off-field information from his grades because it is hard for him to assess where the report is coming from and what weight it deserves — while [[Brandon Angelo]] does incorporate it, and does grade visible on-field effort and demeanor as an evaluation 'panic button' ([[Isaiah Bond]], [[Adonai Mitchell]]). Both agreed the league itself is inconsistent here, citing Brandon Marshall and Terrell Owens as talents teams debated being around, and last year's [[Jermaine Burton]] and [[Javon Baker]] as starting-caliber players dragged down by off-field noise. Waldman's practical point: an off-field fall can be the value, since a day-three developmental pick is not expected to start and the drop may be what forces the player to zoom out and correct.
- 2025-04-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-16]]): character can be the dominant variable in a prospect's range of outcomes. He says [[Isaiah Bond]] carries [[Jermaine Burton]]-level red flags — citing Steve Palazzolo of the 33rd Team and Mike Renner (neither tracked here), the latter joking that the pair of them in the 2023 Alabama offense is why Nick Saban retired — and confirms he has heard the same independently. Harmon then adds that Bond was arrested on sexual assault charges after all of that was already public. His conclusion is that the on-field profile supports late day two but the off-field file probably pushes him to day three, with the caveat that he made the same fall prediction about Burton, who went in round three.
- 2025-05-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-05]]): on the 'doesn't love football' label, Waldman is openly skeptical of the evaluators making it — he questions whether 25-to-35-year-olds assessing another person's love of the game have enough life outside football to put it in perspective, and calls some of those assessments really bad. His historical examples: the same was said about Arian Foster because he studied philosophy and Robert Smith because he wanted to be a med student. His process on [[Damien Martinez]] was to have trusted, well-connected people check the off-field reports rather than accept or dismiss the chatter, and what came back was a drunk-driving offense from a couple of years earlier for which Martinez was cleared before a bowl game at Oregon State.
- 2025-06-02 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-02]]): discussing the [[Stefon Diggs]] boat-video story and [[Aaron Rodgers]]' public persona, Waldman argues fantasy managers systematically overweight off-field noise. Most NFL teams, he says, have players doing as much or worse than what was splashed across the tabloids and those players still perform; coaches are aware, would prefer better decisions, but ultimately treat them as grown men with lives to sort out. He notes the same player can draw opposite reputations — Diggs criticized by Minnesota teammates yet a leader on and off the field in Houston — and that being someone you would not want dating your sister is orthogonal to being someone you want leading your football team, a line he applies directly to Rodgers, whose teammates love working with him regardless of what he says publicly. Bob Harris (co-host, not tracked) drove the framing with the reputation contrast.
- 2025-06-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-23]]): discussing Marshall edge Mike Green, whose pre-draft history included two separate sexual assault investigations that produced no charges, Waldman declines to prescribe a stance and lays out both defensible positions instead. If a manager feels they do not want a player suspected of that on their roster, that is fine — while noting that drafting someone in fantasy is not actually rewarding them. If a manager reasons that no charges were brought and that an NFL contract implies the justice system had nothing to act on, that is fine too. He keeps the football evaluation separate and explicit: tools, a good motor, and a Baltimore defensive end room that needs youth.
- 2025-07-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-21]]): working through the [[Quinshon Judkins]] arrest, Waldman is explicit that declining to draft a player charged with that conduct is a legitimate personal choice — 'good for you, that's your decision' — and separately that as a fantasy analyst he is not a legal analyst and defers on the law to non-tracked writer Drew Davenport, who he credits with delivering nuanced explanations of how such charges actually resolve. His own framing is that this is a hobby, that we rarely know the full circumstances, and that the market has historically re-admitted players ([[Tyreek Hill]], Peyton Manning) after giving them room to grow; at some point it reduces to pricing. Bob Harris (not tracked) treats it purely as a risk-tolerance question suited to casual leagues with deep waiver wires.
