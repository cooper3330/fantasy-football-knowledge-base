## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-06-27 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-27]]): studying receivers for the RSP over the past three years, he believes seven-on-seven, flag football and private position-specialist training have raised the baseline fluency of receiver technique to its highest ever, so where a class once produced maybe half a dozen 'could get there in three years' prospects, it now produces something like 18. That compresses the middle of the bell curve and forces him to be more granular — separating players by whether they are at stasis or still moving, not just by grade. [[Matt Harmon]] frames the same problem descriptively rather than methodologically: he has tightened his use of words like 'elite' and talks in tiers and buckets instead, pointing out that best-ball ADP now runs down to [[Jakobi Meyers]] around WR40 and [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] at WR49 — genuinely good receivers — so the hard question is 'good to what degree.'
- 2026-01-29 — According to JJ Zachariason (not tracked) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-01-29]]: his second 'pet theory' is that there are simply too many good WRs now relative to roster spots, so target share and route share get spread thinner even among talented players - he cites Luther Burden III currently running behind Olamide Zaccheaus in Chicago as an example of a good rookie who, in a weaker era, would already have taken the job outright. Both hosts extend the 'targets are earned' framework here: Zachariason argues the concept has been correctly applied in the positive (a heavily targeted player earned it) but overextended in the negative - a low target share doesn't prove a player is bad, only that his teammates or scheme ceiling capped him. They use [[Michael Wilson]] as the case study and note that a team fielding Justin Jefferson, Ja'Marr Chase, [[Puka Nacua]] and [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] together could never let all four sustain a 30% target share regardless of talent.
- 2026-05-07 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-05-07 (Going Deep with Brandon Angelo)]]): Waldman argues that over the past five-to-seven years, the minimum technical competency of college receivers has risen (comparing it to rising average SAT scores) while actual in-game execution -- reading coverages, adjusting routes, playing with nuance -- has declined, because prospects change schools constantly and get less repetition with the same staff and quarterback. He compares the resulting glut of good-looking, athletically gifted but shallow receivers to Chris Conley: big combine numbers, productive college tape, but a career reserve who never becomes a real fantasy contributor. His practical rule for this year's class: once you're past Denzel Boston/Chris Brazell in the third round, be wary of drafting size/speed specimens who 'clog a roster for three or four years,' name-checking [[Chris Bell]] and comparing that tier to Josh Palmer-type outcomes; instead he points to the era's outlier technicians -- [[Puka Nacua]], [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]], [[Cooper Kupp]] -- as the model for who actually breaks out. [[Brandon Angelo]] agreed, adding that better offensive coaching resources have raised players' knowledge without raising their ability to execute it at NFL speed against equally talented cornerbacks.
