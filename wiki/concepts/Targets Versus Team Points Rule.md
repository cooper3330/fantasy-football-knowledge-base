# Targets Versus Team Points Rule

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-17]]): one of Harmon's stated rules is that your number one receiver should not have as many or more targets than the points your team scores — if he does, the offense has no other threats and the volume is a symptom, not a gift. Examples given: the Giants scored 18 vs Washington with 18 [[Malik Nabers]] targets, scored 15 vs Dallas with 15 Nabers targets, and scored six in Week 1 with seven; the Rams were the worst offender, with 21 [[Cooper Kupp]] targets in a 20-point Week 1. Harmon is explicit that he is 'team throw it to your good players' — the fix is not fewer good players getting the ball but adding threats and using the alpha to move coverage, as [[Davante Adams]] did in Las Vegas. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) liked the rule on hearing it.
- 2024-12-31 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-31]]): James Koh (not a tracked expert) invokes Harmon's heuristic explicitly — if a receiver gets more targets than his team scores points, that is not good — and Harmon confirms it. The Week 17 example is extreme: [[Jerry Jeudy]] drew 18 targets while Cleveland scored three points, clearing the threshold six times over, and converted them into only 12 catches for 94 yards with several drops. Koh's framing is that a million targets mean nothing if the offense is broken; Harmon uses the same game to argue Jeudy's raw target volume is inflated by Dorian Thompson-Robinson's play rather than being evidence of a true No. 1 receiver.
- 2025-09-12 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-12]]): analysts who have done this a long time carry an outdated belief that a receiver needs 10 targets a game, and Harris argues that is no longer the NFL — essentially nobody gets 10 targets. His stated list from Week 1: [[Ja'Marr Chase]] 10, [[Justin Jefferson]] nine, [[CeeDee Lamb]] 10, [[Puka Nacua]] 10, and that is it. He uses this both as a reason not to panic about [[DeVonta Smith]] on six or seven targets and as the biggest reason not to take Chase, or any receiver, first overall. He pairs it with a related point on volatility: everybody outside those five or six guys is subject to whims — a defense's look, three targets drifting elsewhere — so the target-security premium buys less than people think.
