## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-07-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-29]]): guest Dwain McFarland of Fantasy Life (NOT a tracked expert) lays out the play-design levers that convert directly into fantasy points. Per route run for a wide receiver, a play-action route is worth 26% more PPR, motion at the snap 55% more, and two-receiver personnel 29% more than three-receiver sets (fewer mouths to feed). By those measures Arthur Smith in Atlanta ranked second in the NFL in play action, fourth in motion at the snap at 57%, and first in two-receiver personnel — grouping him with Kyle Shanahan and Mike McDaniel — which McFarland argues means the bad Atlanta results were quarterback play, not scheme, since 70% of NFL plays are first-read plays and [[Desmond Ridder]] couldn't make the first read. His foil is Zach Taylor, who does none of these things and has been able to rest on [[Joe Burrow]], [[Ja'Marr Chase]] and [[Tee Higgins]]. Waldman adds Smith's real sin was player utilization — [[Drake London]] in the low 80s of pass-play snaps instead of 92-94%, [[Kyle Pitts]] the same, [[Bijan Robinson]] under 50% of rushing attempts.
- 2024-08-27 — According to guest Derek Klassen (not a tracked expert) and host James Koh (not tracked) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-27]]): an extended discussion of which play callers actually add value. Klassen is of two minds on Kliff Kingsbury — a poor NFL passing-game caller running simple RPOs, four-wide quick game, slot fades and go balls, but with one of the better gun and quarterback run games in football, which happens to match [[Jayden Daniels]]'s strengths; the problem is Washington's personnel. On college-to-NFL translation, Koh warns against assuming Seattle inherits Washington's offense with Ryan Grubb, who has worked almost entirely under Kalen DeBoer and never spent a day in the NFL — the same pattern as Pete Carmichael away from Sean Payton, and Joe Brady, who Klassen believes was not really calling LSU's offense and struggled in Carolina before improving at Buffalo. Klassen rates Grubb's scheme as NFL concepts retrofitted to college, well above Kingsbury's high-school air raid, but notes Washington's elite line let them win with five-man protection. Both also question Kellen Moore's run game and refusal to design running-back targets.
- 2024-09-10 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-10]]): Week 1 gave a clean A/B on deploying motion and quick game. Ben Johnson used full-speed pre-snap motion to get [[Jameson Williams]] off the line and away from press man, producing a 42.9% first-read target share and 68 of his 121 yards after the catch, while simultaneously pushing [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] onto the line as a power slot — Harmon calls the two evolving in conjunction 'really cool to see,' though he expects a learning curve for Johnson in redistributing touches. Against that, Cliff Kingsbury put [[Terry McLaurin]] in pre-snap motion zero times (46 snaps left, three right, per Grant Paulson's charting). Harmon's principle: motion and rhythm throws are good tools, but 'do that shit with your best players' — Kingsbury has instead motioned the Mickey Mouse players since Arizona, where [[DeAndre Hopkins]] did the same static iso work McLaurin is doing now.
- 2024-09-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-16]]): motion, play action and rollouts are 'what the cool kids in the coaching community do' — he names Mike McDaniel and now Klint Kubiak. Waldman argues Kubiak's ability to create scenarios that use players to the best of their ability and disguise intent is what turned New Orleans's personnel into production, killing defenses multiple ways: [[Chris Olave]] as the most versatile receiver, [[Rashid Shaheed]] as a game-breaker who can also work the middle, Foster Moreau when healthy, [[Taysom Hill]] everywhere, and two-tight-end/fullback sets that hand [[Alvin Kamara]] an extra gap. He also credits Kevin O'Connell's play-calling for [[Sam Darnold]]'s start and a 97-yard [[Justin Jefferson]] touchdown, while noting he had been a year early on the Saints because Trevor Penning undercut the offensive line.
- 2024-09-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-17]]): Week 2 gave a controlled experiment in play caller value. New Orleans changed almost no offensive personnel and became the league's most efficient offense under Clint Kubiak — 75% under center, motion on 74% of plays for 9.5 yards per play and a 61.5% success rate (NextGenStats), and [[Derek Carr]] going from dead last in play-action rate to 51.2% of dropbacks. Harmon's rule of thumb: a scheme that goes from far out of line with modern design to pushing all the right buttons elevates the quarterback, the offensive line and the receivers simultaneously, so the whole room rises together. The inverse is Chicago, where Harmon says Shane Waldron let the 2023 Seahawks offense go to rot with no answers to pressure and is repeating it, and asks why the Bears did not simply hire Kubiak. Koh (not tracked) adds that a good coordinator can substantially devalue a bad offensive line, citing New Orleans; Harmon agrees, referencing his podcast with Brandon Thorn on helping a poor line.
- 2024-12-10 — According to co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) with [[Matt Harmon]] agreeing ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-10]]): Koh argues [[Puka Nacua]] on a different staff is not producing this, and that Sean McVay's motion is never needless — it moves him off top coverage, gets his motor running before the catch, and forces defenses out of autopilot base looks when they also have to account for [[Cooper Kupp]]. Next Gen Stats supports it: 383 scrimmage yards after motion since Week 8, more than double the next player ([[Jayden Reed]], 168). Harmon extends the point to Kupp, saying 30 or 31 other teams would have wasted the original big-slot receiver, and both cast Green Bay's handling of Reed as the exact inverse — a coaching staff failing to maximize its best separator.
- 2024-12-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-23]]): the fantasy-relevant evidence for coaching effects keeps stacking. Dave Canales now has [[Geno Smith]]'s comeback, [[Baker Mayfield]]'s turnaround and [[Bryce Young]]'s emergence — 'at some point I have to believe what my eyes are telling me' — and Waldman credits fortifying the interior offensive line plus a commitment to running the ball ([[Chuba Hubbard]]), since an effective ground game is a quarterback's best friend. In Chicago, Thomas Brown replacing Shane Waldron as play caller moved [[Caleb Williams]] to a projected 18-20 points per game and lifted both [[D.J. Moore]] and [[Keenan Allen]] into top-15 receiver production. In Minnesota he credits Kevin O'Connell for building to [[Sam Darnold]]'s strengths. Waldman contrasts all of it with the anti-example of a coach players would refuse to play for.
- 2025-01-14 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-14]]): the play caller Harmon most wants to hire is Joe Brady, because of personnel flexibility rather than any one scheme — Buffalo can get heavy, overpower a front and run the ball, or spread out and put four or five men in the route for [[Josh Allen]], and Harmon sees the same shape in the best Cowboys offenses under Kellen Moore. He is explicit about the limit: you cannot lift the Josh Allen offense and drop it elsewhere. The counterexample is Mike McCarthy, whose system Harmon calls typical West Coast stuff tied to quarterback footwork — it produced the [[Dak Prescott]]-to-[[CeeDee Lamb]] slant connection in 2023 but, Harmon says, does not fit [[Caleb Williams]]' game at all. Koh's separate negative case is Todd Bowles, who he says provides neither a schematic nor a game-management advantage.
- 2025-01-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-21]]): asked what actually makes Ben Johnson a good offensive coordinator beyond the trick plays, Harmon names three things. First, a willingness to hit the same concept over and over rather than hunting a counterpunch — the repeated [[Jameson Williams]] dig, [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] racking up receptions on the same five routes; if there is a weakness, pick at it repeatedly. Second, spacing in the pass game, so receivers are not running into each other. Third, clarity of roles for hyper-specific talents — Williams, St. Brown and [[Sam LaPorta]] each know their one job. Harmon's caveat is that this may not be a portable system: much of it was built around [[Jared Goff]] keeping the offense on schedule, and it could look different with [[Caleb Williams]]. He points to the 2023 Eagles promoting Brian Johnson after Shane Steichen left as the warning for Detroit's internal-promotion path.
- 2025-01-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-23]]): the coaching carousel segment produced a general theory of why offensive play callers get to wait for the right job while defensive coaches must strike while the iron is hot. Harmon argues offense is more stable year to year than defense, the market is thirsty for the play-calling head coach archetype off the McVay and Shanahan trees, and many of those hires never actually called plays. His two cautionary examples are the workload problem: Thomas Brown going passing game coordinator to OC to head coach in Chicago, where Harmon says you can map [[Caleb Williams]]'s season improving when Brown took play calling and falling apart again once he had too much on his shoulders; and Jeff Ulbricht in New York taking on the whole building plus [[Aaron Rodgers]]. He adds that McVay, Shanahan and Andy Reid are so coveted precisely because they can run the offense and manage the building at once. Applied to players, Harmon's [[Jameson Williams]] take is the same idea in reverse — the right coordinator expresses a skill set, the wrong one wastes it.
- 2025-03-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-18 (Feel It or F-It Free Agency Winners and Losers)]]): the episode treats coordinator quality as a first-order fantasy input in both directions. Bob Harris (untracked) says the Ben Johnson hire alone is enough to make him a [[Caleb Williams]] believer, and notes new Jets OC Tanner Engstrand was groomed under Johnson in Detroit as a reason to expect designed usage for [[Justin Fields]]. Waldman's inverse case is why Cincinnati had to keep paying its receivers: if a team sacrifices its offensive identity to fund another unit, it can end up changing coordinators, installing 'some sort of doof' who doesn't understand the scheme — the pattern he says Chicago has run through several iterations and Green Bay has hit in the past — and derailing the players it already paid.
- 2025-04-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-16]]): Andy Reid converting [[Rashee Rice]] from an SMU vertical, one-side boundary receiver into an immediate interior player is the archetype of a coach seeing a role the evaluation community could not — Harmon calls it 'the genius of Andy Reid' and notes Rice confirmed the plan existed at the moment he was drafted, not after camp. He applies the lesson to [[Jalen Royals]] as an argument for humility, while explicitly refusing to let it become a blanket excuse: 'not everyone can go and attach themselves to Patrick Mahomes and Andy Reid'. The practical framing is that some coach has to have a better vision for a limited-deployment prospect than the evaluator does.
- 2025-07-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-11]]): Harmon warns against the reflex of assuming an elite offense survives losing its play caller. Detroit in 2024 was third in EPA per play, first in success rate, second in explosive play rate and first in points per game — he compares it to the 2016 Falcons under Kyle Shanahan (first in EPA per play, third in success rate, first in explosive plays and points per game), who in 2017 under Steve Sarkisian stayed sixth/third/third in those rate stats but fell to 13th in points per game. Successor John Morton's only prior NFL coordinator job was the 2017 Jets, dead last in offensive success rate. Harmon's point is that you cannot argue Ben Johnson is pixie dust in Chicago and also that Detroit will be unaffected — the offense can still be good and someone still suffers, which for him is [[Jameson Williams]] rather than the metronomic [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]]. Koh adds that nobody was better than Johnson at scheming Williams open via motion and a 65/35 wide-slot split.
- 2025-10-02 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-02]]): play callers and offensive designers sit in tiers exactly like quarterbacks do — you have the Mahomes/Stafford/Herbert/Allen tier and you have fringe starters, and the same distribution exists on the headset. He placed Kevin Patullo at the bottom of that list, calling his promotion a nepotism hire without the nepotism (he had never called plays at any level), and put Kellen Moore among the best offensive minds in the league; the gap between them, he said, is the gap between [[Spencer Rattler]] and [[Matthew Stafford]] in how you design, get everyone on the same page, build checks and give the quarterback creativity. [[Matt Waldman]] added the corollary that whoever inherits a bad coordinator's offense inherits the scheme too, so firing the play caller midseason does not reset anything.
- 2025-11-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-11-06]]): Waldman describes a recurring pattern of schemed 'free parking' production for [[Tyler Warren]] and [[Tucker Kraft]] — delayed releases into the flat, running a back out of the backfield so the linebacker chases downfield, or crossing a receiver in front of a linebacker or safety who has no coverage responsibility there but reacts anyway. The result is a tight end floating into 10-15 yards of open runway with nobody near him, at least two such plays per week, and in one game over 80% of Warren's yardage. His point is diagnostic, not dismissive: this is credit to the play caller and does not prove the player is bad or incapable of more, but it means the box score is measuring the scheme. It also creates a takeaway risk, since a defense that decides to punish the player on and off the ball — as Pittsburgh did — can remove that yardage in a single week.
- 2025-11-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-11-11]]): Dan Campbell taking back play calling from John Morton is evidence that Detroit's offensive identity was always more Campbell's than the market credits — he did the same thing with Anthony Lynn in year one before collaborating with Ben Johnson. Campbell framed it as needing a change-up and said he has a good feel for his players; Harmon reads the proof as putting [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] in the slot on 78% of snaps and reducing [[Jameson Williams]]' air yards per target to about 10 so he could run after the catch. Harmon argues the real merit of a play caller is not feeding the easy alpha but activating the specific, sensitive archetypes, and says Campbell has now earned the same untouchable status fans grant Shanahan and McVay — brain drain on the staff no longer matters. Koh agrees it is time to stop discounting him.
