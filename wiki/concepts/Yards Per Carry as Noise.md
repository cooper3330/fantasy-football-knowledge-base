## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-10 — According to Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert), co-host with [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-10 (Regression Candidates and Exuberance-Pessimism)]]): yards per carry is close to a pseudo-random number generator and is his favourite regression stat precisely because it is so noisy. It is not fully random — there is a repeatable component — but that component is not general running back talent, it is straight-line speed. The mechanism: once a back clears the second level, a 4.3 turns a 20-yard gain into a 70-yard gain, and that one extra 50 yards moves a season average by 0.3 to 0.4. Most backs who finish above league average need only one or two runs removed to fall below it. He cites Le'Veon Bell, Matt Forte and post-injury Frank Gore as excellent backs with below-average marks purely for lack of long speed, versus burners like Raheem Mostert. Evidence this season: [[Gus Edwards]] (top-ten all-time to last in the league), [[DeAndre Swift]], [[Breece Hall]] at 3.0, and [[Saquon Barkley]] and [[Derrick Henry]] at unsustainable sixes.
- 2024-11-14 — According to Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-14 (Age Cliffs and Dented Cans)]]: he will 'always do air quotes' around running back efficiency, citing [[Tony Pollard]] as the classic case — useful behind [[Ezekiel Elliott]], declared a star on efficiency, then underwhelmed as a lead back. He extended the point to broken tackles: it is the easiest element of back play to quantify superficially and it is genuinely sticky year to year, so the industry over-weighted it and 'valued what we measure' — which is how [[Javonte Williams]], a hallmark tackle-breaker, got priced as a top-three dynasty back. Breaking tackles is good all else equal, but it is 'surprisingly not the be-all end-all' of the position. He also dismissed career-carry mileage as 'pseudoscience,' arguing a high carry count is if anything a positive signal. [[Matt Waldman]] agreed, and countered that scouting should isolate what a back does within his control to create explosives rather than counting the explosives themselves.
- 2025-08-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-11]]): Waldman says 'f**k' the idea that preseason box-score stats or runs through open creases should dictate how we grade running backs. His example is Twitter analysis of [[RJ Harvey]] against the 49ers built on still photographs with arrows pointing at open space — usually a sign the analyst doesn't understand the scheme design or the decision the back is actually making. His analogy: take a still of your least favorite highway at rush hour, point at the shortcut three lanes over and across the median, and declare the driver should have taken it. Guest Dave Kluge (Footballguys, not tracked) applies the same logic to [[Tahj Brooks]]' 2.5 yards per carry behind a second-team line, versus the excitement over [[Braelon Allen]] going 11 untouched yards — the box score is damning and the tape is encouraging.
- 2025-09-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-18 (Film & Theory)]]) relaying Adam Harstad (Football Guys, not a tracked expert): Harstad's older study of young running backs handed a huge workload while posting a terrible yards-per-carry found roughly 98 percent went on to be stars and about 2 percent became Trent Richardson. He names Le'Veon Bell, [[Jonathan Taylor]], LaDainian Tomlinson, Matt Forte, Walter Payton, Emmitt Smith and Marshawn Lynch as backs whose rookie workload-plus-inefficiency profile looked identical to the one now being used to indict [[Ashton Jeanty]]. The point is that a heavy rookie workload is the signal — the team's revealed belief — while early efficiency and a lack of explosive plays are close to noise. Waldman's film-side agreement: he told people to expect a Tomlinson-style rookie year from Jeanty, where the box score annoys people but the tape shows a talented back who needs help and acclimation.
- 2025-12-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-12-22]]): Waldman generalizes that both quarterback completion percentage and running back yards-per-carry are largely team-performance stats mistakenly thrust onto individuals, and 'it makes no sense whatsoever' to grade a player on them in isolation. For quarterback accuracy he wants charted catchable-pass rate instead of raw completion percentage, citing [[Lamar Jackson]] (whose completion percentage looked light despite a high catchable-pass rate) and [[Caleb Williams]] (whose Bears-media completion-percentage target ignored that it took [[Jared Goff]] three years to reach similar marks). For running backs he draws the same parallel with [[TreVeyon Henderson]], whose plain YPC looks poor next to [[Rhamondre Stevenson]]'s once his explosive plays are stripped out, arguing his real merit is in the big plays themselves, not the per-touch average.
- 2026-02-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-02-13]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the single most important non-physical running back trait is understanding down and distance and living to fight another down rather than chasing a low-probability explosive on 2nd or 3rd and short -- a habit that gets applauded in college (where it produces highlight runs) but fails in the NFL because defenders close the athletic gap that let backs get away with it in college. Cited [[David Montgomery]] and early-career [[Saquon Barkley]] as backs who had to unlearn bouncing runs outside, and praised [[Christian McCaffrey]] as a back whose disciplined, on-schedule running matters far more than his often-criticized YPC.
