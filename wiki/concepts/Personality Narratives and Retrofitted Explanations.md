## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-09-26 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-26]]): Harris devotes the opening five minutes to what he calls insidious analysis — Kirk Herbstreit saying on air that what you'd love to see from [[Kyler Murray]] is to get his guys to believe, and that you could look at [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] and tell he had lost confidence. His objection is not that the claims are impossible but that nothing licenses them except the scoreboard: Herbstreit isn't reporting, he is doing connect-the-dots pop psychology, and the man in the box saying it makes fans treat it as true. The same move produced sympathy for [[Sam Darnold]] having been through a lot, when the team changes had everything to do with Darnold being bad at his job. The structural danger: if your primary lens on sport is personality, you inevitably chalk performance up to personality and end up implying good guys win and bad guys lose. Harris says this is what pushes data people to strip the human out entirely, and argues for a middle path — ignore what kind of people or leaders they are, and judge what they are physically and mentally capable of by what they show when they play.
- 2025-12-02 — According to Matt Harmon ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-12-02]]): discussing fan reaction to George Pickens' development, Harmon described 'results-based' commentary as retrofitting -- if a player is playing well now, fans assume he was 'always this guy,' blaming scheme/QB/coaching for any prior struggles and crediting the player nothing for growth. He tied this to highlight bias (a few spectacular catches make you assume every other play was great) and its inverse negativity bias around drops, arguing failure and success both 'have many fathers,' and that evaluators need to separate a player's own development from the environment around him.
