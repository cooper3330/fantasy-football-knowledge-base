# Zone vs Man Route Running

## Definition

Beating zone coverage and beating man coverage are separate skills. Zone route
running is about finding and settling into space and being where the
quarterback expects; man route running is about winning a one-on-one release
and separation battle. [[Matt Waldman]] treats "can he do both?" as a primary
sorting question for receivers — and as the reason speed is a poor proxy for
receiver quality.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]): "You don't always have to have blazing speed to be a top wide receiver — it's about how they fit in their offense" and about route-running skill, "especially zone route running, which isn't always easy to do." His tiering: some players are great zone route runners but not good man route runners (he names Deebo Samuel); some are better at one than the other; a few do both. [[Keenan Allen]] and [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] do both, and are used inside and outside — "their versatility reigns." That combination, not speed, is why he calls them top-5 receivers.

- 2023-12-18 — **Matt Waldman**, same episode: [[Jayden Reed]] "may wind up one of those players," with the difference that "Reed actually has speed" on top of the route-running profile — his qualifier is the offense, not the player.

- 2023-12-18 — **Matt Waldman**, same episode, applying the same lens elsewhere: [[Brandon Aiyuk]] is "the best route runner on their team" and has developed into one of the top route runners in the league, which is the whole basis for taking him as a WR1; [[Justin Jefferson]] is "one of the best three route runners in the NFL"; [[T.J. Hockenson]] is "great in zone coverage" and positions himself well in man. Reliable route running is also what makes receivers quarterback-proof — see [[Weak Quarterback Play and Receiver Value]].

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]): adds a third axis alongside man/zone — **pacing variation within the route**. [[Malik Nabers]] "is excellent at using pacing, not only with his footwork but with his routes... the best receivers tell stories well like that," and he "understands how to set people up." Michael Crabtree is his historical example of a receiver who was a terrific route runner "for his size" specifically because of pace. Cross-framework: [[Pace Control and Movement Intellect]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, the pre-route and post-route bookends he uses to grade pro-readiness — **release work** (opening the chest to press instead of using a footwork release gets a receiver pinned to the boundary) and **catch-window selection** (Chris Olave as the model, varying whether he attacks the earliest or latest window based on coverage) — plus overhand vs underhand attack position. [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] is the negative worked example on all three; Terry McLaurin is the standing exception on underhand catching. Full detail: [[Prospect Pro-Readiness vs Ceiling]].

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]], same episode, on the practical consequence: route-tree breadth is what makes a receiver deployable everywhere. [[Malik Nabers]] can be aligned "in the backfield, in the slot, out wide, at Z" from day one; [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] is "not the mover that Nabers is, which is going to limit his route tree initially."
- 2024-02-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host — not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-13]]): a live game-level confirmation of Waldman's December 21 [[Deebo Samuel]] read, independently arrived at by Harmon from the Super Bowl LVIII tape rather than cited from Waldman's episode. Kansas City pressed Samuel at the line all game specifically to take away his zone-beating/YAC strength (11 targets, 3 catches, 33 yards) — Harmon calls the plan "quietly the most brilliant part" of the Chiefs' defensive game plan. Same episode, applied to a hypothetical Kansas City free-agent target: Harmon rejects [[Marquise Brown]] as a fit for the Chiefs' outside-receiver need on identical grounds — "another zone-beating type," not someone who can beat man coverage or win contested-catch situations, which is explicitly what Harmon says Kansas City actually needs at that spot.

- 2024-03-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (co-host — not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-21]]): reaffirms [[Marquise Brown]] as "one of the most solid zone beaters out there" (80%+ success rate vs. zone in every season but his injury-shortened 2023, when it dipped to a career-low 51st percentile), which Harmon frames as a specific fit for Kansas City given opposing defenses play man coverage against them only 25.7% of the time. Contrasts that with his man-coverage numbers, which have declined by year (73.6% rookie season, down to 62.6% in 2022 and an all-time-low 59% in 2023). Same episode, on [[Jerry Jeudy]]: reaffirms the man/zone split from the 2024-03-14 charting (70.6-70.7% vs. man, 68.4%/70.3% vs. zone, 8th percentile) as the reason he should play flanker rather than slot in Cleveland — man coverage skill (63rd percentile) is "pretty good," but the zone weakness makes him a bad fit for the high-zone-rate slot alignment.
- 2024-04-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-18]]): Both 2024 prospects Xavier Legette (81.4% success vs. zone, 19th percentile vs. man) and Troy Franklin (82.4% vs. zone, 26th percentile vs. press) posted strong zone-beating marks alongside coverage-specific weaknesses, illustrating how RP's man/zone/press splits can separate 'wins vs. soft coverage' upside from 'true X receiver' readiness better than aggregate box-score production.
- 2024-04-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-25]]): used man/press vs. zone success-rate splits as the throughline for a full 2024 WR-class superlatives exercise -- [[Rome Odunze]] and [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] graded best vs. man/press, [[Ricky Pearsall]] and [[Ja'Lynn Polk]] close behind, while [[Ladd McConkey]]'s class-low 44% success rate vs. press was treated as a hard cap on his ability to play outside, and [[Troy Franklin]] graded notably better vs. zone than vs. man. Reinforces that press/man success rate is Harmon's primary determinant of X-receiver viability at the next level.
- 2024-04-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-30]]): Harmon evaluates every first-round 2024 receiver's landing spot through RSP-charted success rates vs. man, vs. zone, and vs. press, treating ~80% as the benchmark for a strong zone score and using press-coverage success as the key differentiator for X-receiver readiness. He applies the framework to argue San Francisco specifically needed a man-beater (Ricky Pearsall, 87th percentile vs. man) because Deebo Samuel is a zone-beater who struggles in man coverage on money downs, and to downgrade Xavier Worthy (59.5% vs. press) as a poor scheme fit for Kansas City despite plus man/zone marks, while praising Brian Thomas Jr.'s 80.4% zone success on a heavily vertical route tree as unusually strong.
- 2024-05-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-05-21]]): both [[Tank Dell]] (72.8% success vs. man, 74th percentile; 77.6% vs. zone, 40th percentile) and [[Dontayvion Wicks]] (72.2% vs. man; 78% vs. zone) as rookies showed the same pattern -- strong man/press separation on outbreaking routes (corner, out, comeback for Dell; out, corner for Wicks) paired with comparatively weaker zone-coverage success. Harmon frames this as a recurring profile for undersized, craft-based outside receivers who win at the catch point and downfield rather than settling into zone windows, and notes it tracks with each player's college charting data as well.
- 2024-06-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-06-03]]): using San Francisco's receiver room as the case study, Waldman drew a hard line between [[Brandon Aiyuk]], whom he called a genuinely good man-to-man route runner who fits Brock Purdy's anticipation-and-timing passing style, and [[Deebo Samuel]], who wins almost exclusively in zone coverage and struggles to separate man-to-man — which is why Waldman labels Samuel 'a premium gadget player' rather than a true X receiver a defense can't scheme around. He noted this distinction is exactly why San Francisco wouldn't trade Aiyuk (defenses can dedicate a lesser corner to Samuel in man coverage and still win) and why the team drafted [[Ricky Pearsall]] as a lighter Deebo-archetype who nonetheless offers better man-coverage route skills.
- 2024-07-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-30]]): coverage-specific success rates diagnose route trees, not just quality. Koh challenges that a high zone success rate cannot come from nine routes, and Harmon agrees the zone-beating work shows up on posts, digs and deep overs — while a curl is fundamentally a man-beater route that demands deception, technique and timing, which is why [[Jameson Williams]] posting 94.1% on curls in his final five games was the most impressive thing in the sample given he could not work back to the quarterback as a college prospect. He applies the same lens to [[Christian Watson]] (good on digs and posts, poor on slants, disastrous on comebacks — anything requiring him to stop and come back), and to [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] and [[Cooper Kupp]], where the path to tier one runs through the coverage type each was historically weakest against: press and man for Kupp, downfield zone beating for St. Brown.
- 2024-08-01 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-01]]): Harmon argues a poor press-coverage score is not disqualifying if the offense can keep the receiver away from press — moving him pre-snap, aligning him as a flanker off the line, or simply playing in a scheme like Kansas City's where [[Xavier Worthy]] will rarely see it. He cites the small press sample sizes of established outside winners as evidence the league already solves this for its best players. Conversely he treats press and contested-catch weakness as a genuine ceiling cap when it is paired with a thin frame and no YAC ability, contrasting [[Ladd McConkey]] — who breaks tackles in space, so schemed touches pay off — with [[Troy Franklin]], for whom scheming space does not compensate. He also frames a 70/80 man-zone split as the functional-starter benchmark.
- 2024-09-03 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-03]]): beating zone matters more than ever because the NFL plays more zone than ever, and it matters most with a scrambling quarterback — defenses will not turn their backs on [[Lamar Jackson]] and give up rushing lanes, so [[Zay Flowers]] saw zone on 60.5% of his sampled routes. That, Harmon argues, is why Flowers can approach elite production on an 85% zone success rate even though his press success rate is far the weakest among the receivers charting near him and his 71.7% vs man is only 68th percentile. He contrasts this with [[Deebo Samuel]], a great zone beater who is not a man beater, and with [[Ricky Pearsall]], who clears the bar against both.
- 2024-10-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-17]]): Harmon restated the Reception Perception benchmarks — you want 70% success rate vs man and 80% vs zone — and used the gap to sort deployment. [[Jerry Jeudy]] hit 70.7% vs man in 2023 but only 70.3% vs zone, an 8th-percentile mark, and Harmon's conclusion is categorical: 'you cannot play in the slot with those kind of zone numbers.' The inverse trap is smaller receivers who post great zone success rates but are not ball winners — open, but nobody is completing the contested throw to them. He also tied coverage diet to team reputation: nobody fears [[Deshaun Watson]] downfield, so Cleveland saw the fifth-most man-coverage snaps in the NFL, which incidentally suits a man-beater like Jeudy even if the quarterback cannot exploit it.
- 2024-10-22 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-22]]): this episode is a sustained case for the zone/man split as the key charting distinction. [[Xavier Worthy]] posted a 45.5% success rate versus man and 25% versus press while still ripping posts against zone on free releases — Harmon's point being that zone production and man production are close to different skills. [[Ricky Pearsall]] showed the same shape in reverse-scale form: a very good success rate versus zone, just over 50% versus man. Harmon also argues the split has team-level consequences: San Francisco faced more man coverage on third down than anyone in 2024, lost its designated man-beater in [[Brandon Aiyuk]], and [[Brock Purdy]] went 5-of-14 for 80 yards with three picks against man versus Kansas City. James Koh (not tracked) adds that a 45% man success rate would rank just outside the bottom five in Reception Perception history.
- 2024-10-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-29]]): the framework for slot receivers shifted this season. Koh (not tracked) proposed it and Harmon agreed 100% — the zone-coverage success rate is the baseline requirement for playing slot in the NFL (they want roughly 80% or better versus zone), but beating man coverage from the slot is what 'completely and utterly unlocks' the route tree and defines the ceiling. Zone-beating slot work produces what they call 'scammy' underneath volume; man-beating slot work is how you hunt explosives on out-breakers, posts and slot fades. [[Ladd McConkey]] (77.3% vs man) and [[Josh Downs]] are their case studies, with [[Tyler Lockett]] as the archetype — always good versus zone, but the over-the-top plays came from man-beating ability. They distinguish this from the power-slot archetype ([[Rashee Rice]]), where underneath touches and broken tackles are the explosive source.
- 2024-11-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-29]]): Harmon states his working thresholds for zone-coverage success rate in Reception Perception — he wants a slot receiver around 80%, and for an outside receiver he does not 'raise the alarm bells' until the number drops below 77%. He applies them to [[Adonai Mitchell]], whose 72% vs zone is well under the line and tied with [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] for the lowest among rookies sampled in the in-season rookie report, and treats it as a consistency and processing issue rather than a separation one, given Mitchell's 84.2% vs press and 71.4% vs man. His stated rule of thumb: there are few examples in RP history of a receiver being labelled a true number one while sitting at 72% vs zone, so that number rising is the precondition for the projection.
- 2024-12-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-05]]): [[Jerry Jeudy]] is his working example of the man/zone split mattering more than the aggregate. Harmon is interested in any receiver above a 70 percent success rate versus man; Jeudy has charted around the 63rd percentile versus man but the 8th percentile versus zone, because he adds wasted motion and does not know when to settle into a soft spot. His rule: that profile is survivable outside on iso and runway routes, but disqualifying in the slot, where beating zone is the job — which is why Denver's vertical-slot usage failed and Cleveland's flanker role with downfield play-action digs, posts and slants works.
- 2024-12-10 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-10]]): Harmon uses coverage-split yards per route run as the organizing lens for the whole episode, arguing an offense is best understood by which receiver is its man-coverage answer and which eats against zone. Minnesota is his cleanest example — [[Justin Jefferson]] draws bracket attention, [[T.J. Hockenson]] answers man at 6.67 yards per route run over four weeks, and [[Jordan Addison]] feasts against zone and off man at 3.37 while remaining suspect against press. [[Puka Nacua]] is exceptional precisely because he is second in the league against both man (4.31) and zone, so there is no coverage answer for him. [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] is tenth against man for the season despite being a slot player, which Harmon says makes him more than a rinky-dink zone beater, and [[Jauan Jennings]] rates well against zone but lacks the downfield threat to be a number one.
- 2024-12-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-12]]): man coverage remains the most player-isolating environment for evaluating a receiver, and man-heavy, press-heavy defenses are the games he most wants to chart — he traced the origin of Reception Perception on the old Backyard Banter articles to exactly that receiver-cornerback iso matchup. His problem in recent years has been supply and selection: those looks are increasingly rare, and the most man-heavy defense was often a bad one, so charting against it risked giving receivers an artificial boost. Detroit in 2024 solves that — most man-coverage-heavy defense in the league and now with good corners, making those games a genuine test of player isolation. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) observed man press now shows up mainly on third-and-medium (third-and-five or six), with zone still likely on third-and-long.
- 2025-02-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-11]]): Super Bowl LIX is a clean case study in cascading effects. Philadelphia blitzed 0% of the time yet pressured Patrick Mahomes on 50% of his dropbacks midway through the third quarter, which let Vic Fangio play 95% zone coverage — a big departure from his 71% zone rate over the season. Harmon's point: once four rushers get home, you dedicate the extra defender to coverage, and there is no reason to line up in man against a receiver group whose best weapon ([[Xavier Worthy]]) is a man-coverage beater on quick-hitters rather than an in-rhythm route runner who threatens zone as a first read. Squeezing underneath with zone also erased Mahomes' scramble first downs, with Zack Baun sitting in the flat. Kansas City by contrast played 70% zone and 30% man, and Philadelphia went 12-of-16 for 147 and two touchdowns against zone; the blitz was the one thing that worked for Kansas City (2-of-5, 36 yards, an interception and a sack).
