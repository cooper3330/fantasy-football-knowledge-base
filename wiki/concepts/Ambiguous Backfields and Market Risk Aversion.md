# Ambiguous Backfields and Market Risk Aversion

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-05-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-20]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: guest JJ Zachariason (Late Round Draft Guide, not a tracked expert) laid out a finding he called counterintuitive — previous-season points per game is predictive at the top of the position but is not predictive at all for middle-round running backs (RB3/RB4 range), where the opposite tends to hold: the back who did nothing last year, or is a rookie, beats the back who was an RB2 last year. His explanation is that the market is risk averse and hates ambiguity, so a talented day-two rookie in the seventh or eighth round is discounted for an unclear role, while a demoted former RB2 is discounted for a reason — age cliff, added competition, scheme change, or a fluke season the market has already recognized. His conclusion: boring middle-round running backs are generally just boring, and you should attack first- and second-year backs with ceiling. He cited [[Chase Brown]] and [[Tony Pollard]] as ambiguous-backfield winners.
- 2025-06-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-16]]): a June episode built almost entirely on split backfields produced a consistent rule — the market discounts the veteran in a committee for reasons that are about narrative rather than role. On [[J.K. Dobbins]] he argues analysts will refuse to see him as the legitimate lead back because he signed late in the summer, because he has an injury history, and because his old team let him go, so he will stay priced as an RB3 even as Denver establishes him as the 1A over [[RJ Harvey]]. The mirror case is [[DeAndre Swift]], where the market priced in a Chicago running back signing that never happened. Waldman's practical response is to name the sweet spot rather than the projection — Harvey as an RB3/RB4, not an RB2 — and to accept that ambiguity is a permanent feature: he can see two- or three-headed outcomes in Las Vegas and an even split in Houston between [[Nick Chubb]] and [[Joe Mixon]].
- 2025-07-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and Bob Harris (untracked co-host) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-28]]) [Best Ball]: ambiguous backfields are a best ball asset and a redraft liability. Their framing on Jacksonville — where three organization sources name [[Tank Bigsby]] as the Week 1 starter, another observer says [[Travis Etienne Jr.]], and a third doubts Etienne even opens the season on the roster — is that the entire range of outcomes is realistic, so the correct response is format-dependent rather than a prediction. Harris: if you hit in best ball you make out like a bandito, but in a single redraft league you are taking your chances until the pecking order clarifies. Waldman extends it to Dallas with [[Jaydon Blue]], and Harris applies the same logic to buying [[Nick Chubb]] cheaply as insurance on [[Joe Mixon]] shares. The shared rule: address ambiguity by format, and let the price rather than the projection do the work.
- 2025-08-25 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-25]]): the episode ran the same test across three backfields and reached the same conclusion — the market prices the rookie as if the split will not happen. On [[Omarion Hampton]] (up from about RB16 to RB14 during camp), Waldman expects an even split because [[Najee Harris]] is better than his Pittsburgh-media reputation, with Greg Roman on record saying Hampton will be paired with someone regardless. In Denver he prefers [[J.K. Dobbins]] to [[RJ Harvey]] on role while conceding Harvey's receiving upside, and Bob Harris (Footballguys, NOT tracked) buys Dobbins purely because he costs up to four rounds less. In Washington, Waldman expects [[Jacory Croskey-Merritt]] to lead with [[Austin Ekeler]] situational and [[Chris Rodriguez Jr.]] limited. Harris's counter-strategy is to accept concentration risk instead — he drafts [[Kenneth Walker III]] as an RB1 based on beat-writer reporting of no split, knowing he has no fallback.
- 2025-08-25 — According to guest JJ Zachariason (NOT a tracked expert) on [[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-25]]: all of his research points to ambiguous backfields being a place you actively want to target in drafts. The mechanism is the same one he describes for second-year quarterbacks — question marks about who gets the most valuable touches push cost down, and the market's risk aversion overshoots. He accepts that many of these committees stay obnoxious and never resolve, but points to the Tampa Bay backfield of [[Leonard Fournette]] and Ronald Jones, where both were middle-round picks nobody wanted and Fournette emerged as a league winner partly on dump-offs. He applied the framework to [[Bhayshul Tuten]] in Jacksonville and [[Jaydon Blue]] in Dallas. [[Chris Harris]] applies the same instinct differently, saying the Cowboys backfield is one nobody has a handle on, so the fast guy could turn out to be the most valuable guy.
- 2025-09-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-04 (Film & Theory Sequences, Signal, Steelers)]]): the general lesson not to overreact early has one clean exception — an injury with an obvious successor. Waldman contrasted two hypotheticals: a Kenneth Walker III season-ending injury makes [[Zach Charbonnet]] a clear-cut beneficiary worth going super strong on early, whereas a Kyren Williams injury would leave Blake Corum and Jarquez Hunter in a much cloudier situation where managers go all in on one or the other and probably waste FAAB. Harstad agreed, adding that we know what we know and it is easy to pivot on new information, and that the warning against overreacting has real value but should not stop a decisive move when the situation is genuinely clear.
- 2025-09-08 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-08]]): when people cite Week 1 volume in a genuinely contested backfield, 'fuck that, because it has no context.' They may end up right, but the snap counts frequently encode conditioning and availability rather than a coaching decision — his example is [[Omarion Hampton]]'s Week 1 workload existing only because [[Najee Harris]] had no time to get in shape after the eye injury. He applies the same discipline to Seattle ([[Kenneth Walker III]] / [[Zach Charbonnet]]), where Bob Harris (Footballguys, not tracked) notes owning both backs yields a weekly RB1 but neither being the clear workhorse is precisely the problem, and to Cleveland, where [[Quinshon Judkins]]'s pending return reshuffles [[Dylan Sampson]] and [[Jerome Ford]]. September variance is wild; clarity may not arrive until October.
- 2025-09-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-15]]): the Seattle backfield illustrates how box counts and run direction, not talent, drive week-to-week committee outcomes — [[Kenneth Walker III]] produced on downhill designs run into plus-one and plus-two box advantages while [[Zach Charbonnet]] was stuffed running to the perimeter into even and minus-one boxes, and the pattern was reversed in Week 1. Waldman's inference is that if the beat writer (Bob Condotta of the Seattle Times, relayed by co-host Bob Harris, not a tracked expert) says the team may not know its plan, the team may genuinely not know. Practical guidance: expect it to stay week to week until it normalizes, and do not read a single week's split as a settled depth chart.
- 2025-09-15 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-15]]): several Week 2 backfields showed how badly snap counts alone mislead. Seattle's split flipped entirely from Week 1 — [[Zach Charbonnet]] out-snapped [[Kenneth Walker III]] 35 to 23 yet went 15 carries for 10 yards against Walker's 13 for 105 — which Harris reads as evidence that the same job produces opposite outputs depending on which back gets the well-blocked plays, so you rate the more explosive back slightly ahead and accept variance. In Los Angeles the overall split was lopsided ([[Kyren Williams]] 41 snaps and 19 touches to [[Blake Corum]]'s 18 and five) but the red-zone snap count was only 10-8, and Corum was the only back with a carry inside the five. In Denver, draft capital did not translate: [[RJ Harvey]] at 17 snaps and six touches to [[J.K. Dobbins]]'s 25 and 15.
- 2025-10-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-06]]): three Week 5 backfields illustrated the same failure mode — the market projecting a full takeover onto a back who is actually in a split. Waldman argued [[Woody Marks]] will not overtake [[Nick Chubb]] because Marks's good runs came untouched behind convoys while Chubb was creating yardage with cutbacks and contact; that [[Rico Dowdle]] will not take the job from a healthy [[Chuba Hubbard]] even if he might be the marginally better runner; and that [[Michael Carter]] is the lead back in Arizona but [[Emari Demercado]] keeps enough touches to matter. Bob Harris (not tracked) added the market-expectation framing directly: fantasy players imported the [[Rachaad White]] and [[Rico Dowdle]] full-takeover template onto Marks, and that was the wrong prior. The practical rule Waldman applies is to grade the tape for who created the run rather than who produced the box score.
- 2025-10-07 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-10-07]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: 'I can't legislate usage because, in my opinion, nobody can.' Harris uses the Arizona backfield as the week's proof — pre-game drips and drabs had [[Emari Demercado]] keeping his role and [[Michael Carter]] noncommittal, and the actual split 'turned out not really even to be much of a contest', with no hot hand at all. Guest Brandon Funston of The Athletic (not tracked) extends it to the Chargers: 'we can talk until we're blue in the face about how, oh, this is probably a timeshare, and then the Cardinals absolutely wasn't this past week... you're going to be flying by the seat of your pants.' Neither host hangs pride on the call; both prefer sizing bids to the uncertainty rather than pretending to resolve it.
- 2025-10-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-13]]): relaying a Greg Olsen (not tracked) broadcast point that some running backs actually get stronger as a game goes along, and that the downfall of committee backfields is that a team never finds out who that back is — who you want carrying it late to wear a defense down. Waldman endorsed the point in general but declined to apply it to [[Rico Dowdle]], noting Dowdle has a full game's worth of touches by halftime, is cramping by late in games, and at South Carolina could not stay healthy enough to earn late-game volume often enough to know.
