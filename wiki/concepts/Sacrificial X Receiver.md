# Sacrificial X Receiver

## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-09-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-05]]): Harmon says he talked about sacrificial X receivers all offseason — the outside player who runs deep and backside routes to clear space and block, almost never getting the ball himself, so the offense can do the fun things with its primary weapons. His named examples: [[Alec Pierce]] as maybe the most sacrificial X in the league last year, clearing out for [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] and [[Josh Downs]]; Mack Hollins in Buffalo, where blocking matters because the Bills were number one in success rate on man-gap power runs and neither [[Khalil Shakir]] nor [[Curtis Samuel]] blocks; [[Romeo Doubs]] as something a little more than a sacrificial X while still handling the backside work in Green Bay; and [[Stefon Diggs]] projected into a backside X role in Houston. Harmon notes nobody likes when he talks about receiver blocking, but it drives snap distribution.
- 2024-09-10 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-10]]): Harmon applies the archetype to [[Alec Pierce]], calling him 'definitely a sacrificial X receiver' — the field-stretching, space-eating body whose routes exist mostly to clear coverage. The refinement he offers here is that the archetype's fantasy value is not fixed by the role but by the quarterback attached to it: Pierce plays for [[Anthony Richardson]], who will attempt throws [[Gardner Minshew]] physically could not, so what would be a pure clear-out route for another team occasionally gets ripped for a touchdown. On that basis Harmon says he is 'a little skeptical' of the blanket analysis that a sacrificial X will simply be a zero. He also applies the same lens to the Rams, where [[DeMarcus Robinson]] is the pure vertical option but not the rugged press-beating outside receiver McVay's scheme actually needs.
- 2024-09-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-25]]): Harmon applies the idea to Carolina, saying you can run sacrificial X work with [[Jonathan Mingo]] to free [[Diontae Johnson]] and [[Xavier Legette]] into combination routes, and separately that the worst outcome for a project receiver is being run out as a solo X. His cautionary example is [[Treylon Burks]], whom Tennessee deployed as a boundary X for no clear reason, and he sees the same failure mode waiting for Legette if Carolina uses him there rather than off the ball on horizontal, after-catch routes. The corollary is that a smaller X like Johnson can absorb that alignment burden and let the bigger, less refined receiver work from a spot that suits him.
- 2024-09-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-26]]): applied to Buffalo, where Harmon says Mack Hollins (not a tracked player page) 'is doing the sacrificial X receiver thing' — absorbing the on-the-line boundary snaps that suppress a better slot receiver's volume. He admits two competing interests: as a fantasy and evaluation matter he does not think Buffalo will or should play Hollins forever and hopes those snaps go elsewhere, but 'for the sacrificial X receiver movement, maybe we do want Mack Hollins out there.' The practical consequence is that [[Khalil Shakir]] may never see a [[Rashee Rice]]-sized target load. He makes a related point on Kansas City, where the absence of a true on-the-line intermediate X — the role [[Marquise Brown]] would have filled — is what he says caps the offense's ceiling.
- 2024-10-01 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-01]]): the X receiver is the hardest position in the receiver room and the least schemable — there is no way to manufacture opportunity for an X the way a coach can for a power slot like [[Chris Godwin]] or a schemed-open weapon like [[Malik Nabers]]. Harmon contrasted the sacrificial version of the job (clear out space so underneath 'Mickey Mouse' routes generate YAC) with the real version: win against press man coverage from top corners, run dig routes into the middle of the field between zones, then win after the catch. His argument for [[Nico Collins]] rests entirely on that distinction, and he noted Collins is doing it in a league saturated with two-high coverage explicitly designed to take away that archetype — Houston has faced two-high on 64% of dropbacks, the most in the NFL.
- 2024-10-03 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-03]]): Buffalo is a case study in assigning the sacrificial on-the-line X role to the wrong player. Harmon argues [[Mack Hollins]] — a blocker with no separation or speed value — should be the one anchored on the line so that [[Keon Coleman]] can play off the ball and be schemed open; instead Coleman runs a mostly vertical X tree with only 3.8% slot rate and 81% on-line snaps in the Ravens game, and Hollins is the one used more off the line. His diagnostic for when a young X escapes the role: multi-position alignment, which signals the staff trusts him with more of the playbook. He also floats [[Tre Tucker]] as potentially doing 'sacrificial' field-stretching work in Las Vegas, and separately notes that a genuine coverage dictator on a vertical tree ([[Davante Adams]] in 2022) can keep boxes light enough to hand [[Josh Jacobs]] a rushing title.
- 2024-10-08 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-08]]): Harmon applies the sacrificial X framework across three offenses in one episode and notes a twist — the label doesn't always land on who you'd think. In Buffalo, [[Mack Hollins]] runs ~27% of his routes from the slot while [[Keon Coleman]] is at 3.9%, so the rookie is doing more pure clear-out work than the veteran who is supposed to be absorbing it. In Jacksonville the division is clean: [[Gabe Davis]] takes the sacrificial X reps, which frees [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] to work flanker and the middle of the field on digs, curls and slants. Harmon's related point is that a receiver who is not a separator turns every target into a coin flip, and he claims there is no above-average NFL starter who is an actively bad separator — [[Tee Higgins]] charts slightly above average and [[Mike Williams]] average in RP, whereas the Buffalo perimeter is not. Koh (not tracked) riffs that [[Romeo Doubs]] and [[Alec Pierce]] could join the union.
- 2024-10-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-17]]): Harmon named [[Romeo Doubs]] the best sacrificial X receiver in the league — a title co-host James Koh (not tracked) mocked but Harmon defended as an important, valuable role. The profile: a boundary player who runs the most routes per drop-back on his team and is never off the field, yet is targeted on only 18% of routes, well below the slot and tight end options around him, because his job is clearing coverage for them. Harmon ranks Doubs above [[Gabe Davis]] in the archetype and concedes the praise is faint. The fantasy escape hatch is quarterback temperament: a passer like [[Jordan Love]] who 'will do the inadvisable' turns clear-out routes into targets, as [[Kirk Cousins]] did chucking a designed clear-out to [[Darnell Mooney]] for a touchdown through three defenders.
- 2024-10-22 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-22]]): Harmon names [[Romeo Doubs]] the best sacrificial X in the league — the guy who takes the hard boundary snaps, absorbs the tough coverage and doesn't post friendly yards-per-route-run or targets-per-route-run numbers as a result, which is why the efficiency-stat community pans him. Doubs ran 86.1% of Green Bay's WR routes with 87% of snaps out wide. Harmon then partially retires his own label: 'maybe we need to stop calling him a sacrificial X, maybe he's just a good X.' Co-host James Koh (not tracked) pushes back on the naming itself, arguing that calling anyone 'sacrificial' sounds disrespectful even when it's only describing a role; Harmon holds that it is just a role.
- 2024-10-24 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-24]]): Harmon offered Justin Watson as the purest current example of the sacrificial X — third-most routes on the Chiefs at 114, targeted on just 7% of them, on the field mainly to clear space. The [[DeAndre Hopkins]] trade replaces that with an X who can actually be thrown to on slants and contested balls, which in turn lets [[Xavier Worthy]] and [[Travis Kelce]] work off the line. Harmon extended the idea in a second direction with Jacksonville: [[Calvin Ridley]]'s bad 2023 X usage (go routes, curls, comebacks) was the sacrifice that taught the staff not to anchor [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] to the boundary, and co-host James Koh (not tracked) added that [[Gabe Davis]]'s contract now forces someone else into that role. Harmon also named Arizona as a team currently doing this to its clear best receiver.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-31]]): Harmon opened the show arguing there is a league-wide 'dearth of X receiver play' — so many teams need an outside X that anyone who can actually play the position becomes a transformative addition. He applied it three ways in one episode: Baltimore likely keeps [[Rashad Bateman]] at X 'doing some sacrificial X receiver stuff' while still trusting him in one-on-ones, with [[Diontae Johnson]] and [[Zay Flowers]] rotating inside in 11 personnel; Carolina has no target-earning X at all after trading Johnson, leaving journeyman David Moore as the stopgap and risking [[Xavier Legette]] being force-fed a role Harmon says he cannot win at yet; and Houston loses Stefon Diggs and slides [[Tank Dell]] to X with [[Xavier Hutchinson]] as flanker. The Indianapolis segment reinforced it from the other side — Harmon said there is 'not a lot of positive play from the outside wide receivers' there.
- 2024-11-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-05]]): [[Quentin Johnston]]'s two-year arc is Harmon's illustration that X-receiver deployment can make or break a player who is not a natural at the job. The prior Chargers staff asked Johnston to run a full vertical route tree from X and track the ball over his shoulder — something nothing in his college profile supported — and he charted as one of the worst separators in Reception Perception history. Harbaugh's staff kept him at X but changed the diet: more alignment off the line of scrimmage, runway routes over the middle where he can build speed and escape press, and a run-heavy identity that invites single-high cover-one. Harmon still expects drops to be part of the experience and calls the raw production a mirage, but the role change converted a player Koh thought would wash out into a useful NFL starter.
- 2024-11-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-12]]): the X spot is defined by isolation reps, which is why Reception Perception weights a candidate's man-coverage numbers more heavily than his zone numbers when projecting into the role. He applies it to San Francisco after [[Brandon Aiyuk]]'s injury — nobody in the room had X experience, since [[Jauan Jennings]] was a power slot, [[Ricky Pearsall]] beats man but not press and profiles as flanker/slot, and [[Deebo Samuel]] is a gadget flanker whose role does not change. Jennings's fit comes from third-down usage (10 of 19 catches last regular season on third down, when man rates spike) and an out-breaker-heavy route chart, both man-coverage signals — but Harmon is clear he will not take the top off or win in isolation like Aiyuk. He applies the same lens to Carolina, where the room needs a true X dropped into the spot [[Diontae Johnson]] vacated before the supporting cast is complete.
- 2024-11-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-21]]): coaches routinely default to putting their best receiver at X, and Harmon argues this can actively suppress production when the player's skill set does not match the role. Chicago imported Shane Waldron's Seattle template where [[DK Metcalf]] was the X and installed [[DJ Moore]] there despite Moore being a very different winner — not an elite press-man beater and not a go/out-route runner — leading to Moore's worst season. Miami's version is structural rather than schematic: [[Jaylen Waddle]] is the boundary X on the line and is never the designed target, so screens and layups flow to [[Tyreek Hill]] and manufactured touches to [[Jonnu Smith]]. Harmon's proposed fixes follow the same logic — move Moore around and put [[Rome Odunze]] at X — while noting [[Courtland Sutton]] and [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] are examples of X roles that do fit the player.
- 2024-12-10 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-10]]): Harmon names [[Romeo Doubs]] as the player who made him start talking about sacrificial X receivers in the first place — a limited player who nonetheless has to take the iso routes on the perimeter so the offense's better separators can work elsewhere. He argues Doubs is currently Green Bay's best option for that job because he has been the most consistent of the room, while stressing the archetype should not command a prominent target share; his complaint about the Packers is that the sacrificial role and the featured role have been blurred, with [[Jayden Reed]] paying for it.
- 2024-12-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-17]]): Carolina's staff is chasing an X receiver — the vertical, traditional man-coverage beater who runs gos, comebacks and curls and takes the top off — and Harmon believes it is a genuine offseason priority for them because of what Dave Canales' Tampa Bay offense got out of [[Mike Evans]] in that alignment. His argument is that the role is real and valuable but must be filled by a player who *plays* the part, not one merely built for it: [[Xavier Legette]] has the frame but not the game, and forcing him there wastes his best traits, while [[Diontae Johnson]] had the skill set and failed on fit. Harmon's preferred structure pairs a true vertical X with [[Jalen Coker]] as the power slot and Legette as the flanker.
- 2024-12-24 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-24]]): two live 2024 examples of the archetype. In Buffalo, Harmon says [[Amari Cooper]] is at least 'doing some sacrificial X receiver stuff' — he is nominally the X but sixth on the team in routes over the last two weeks behind [[Khalil Shakir]], [[Mack Hollins]], Dawson Knox, [[Keon Coleman]] and [[Dalton Kincaid]], with the blockers on the outside playing over him. In Kansas City he says Justin Watson is 'doing truly the most sacrificial X receiver stuff in the league.' The structural point Harmon repeats is that Buffalo spent training camp cycling through castoff X receivers, drafted [[Keon Coleman]] to play there, and still settled on Hollins — evidence of how hard the position is to fill and how little fantasy value the role itself confers.
- 2024-12-31 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-31]]): two teams in one episode illustrate the cost of miscasting an X. In Green Bay, [[Romeo Doubs]] runs 100% of first-half dropbacks purely because he is the most consistent player at running ISO routes, not because he is a difference maker — and with no receiver defenses must double, Minnesota and Detroit could play man across the board without allocating extra resources. In Carolina, Harmon says moving rookie [[Xavier Legette]] to a traditional X has gone poorly and was predictable, because the role demands either a separator or an 80/20 contested-catch Alshon Jeffery type, and he does not want to rely on a young player to be that. His conclusion: Carolina needs a real lid-lifting X regardless, because even if Legette develops, that is not the job he should hold. Harmon makes the same point about [[Troy Franklin]] in Denver.
- 2025-01-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-09]]): Harmon publicly recants his application of this label, saying that in the 2024 offseason he called [[Alec Pierce]] 'something worse than a sacrificial X receiver' and apologizes for it. His clarification is that he had originally liked Pierce as a prospect in exactly the vertical X role he ended up playing — rookie Reception Perception data was disgusting against zone but actually solid against press man, which is the right shape for a vertical X — and that he turned on him without cause. Pierce then led all receivers with 517 yards on 20-plus air yard throws on 12 catches. The lesson Harmon draws is that a narrowly deployed X can be genuinely valuable inside that narrow role even when his overall efficiency profile looks poor.
- 2025-01-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-13]]): two 2024 rookies were damaged by being asked to play the vertical boundary X. [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] was at times stuck in the sacrificial X role in an Arizona offense Harmon describes as fun in spots but built on strict rules and roles. [[Ja'Lynn Polk]] is the more extreme case — a flanker-slot hybrid and dirty-work player deployed for long stretches as New England's vertical X with zero college evidence he could win go routes on the boundary, producing the worst rookie season in Harstad's model. [[Kayshon Boutte]] was mostly tossed out in the same role and outperformed expectations there, though Harmon hopes he does not open next season as the starting X. Harmon's broader point is that when a receiver is out of position and out of confidence, skills that were real as a prospect — Polk's hands and blocking tenacity — can simply stop showing up.
- 2025-01-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-20]]): endorsing [[Matt Harmon]]'s framing from the prior week, Waldman said Reception Perception's value here is showing that [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] was used in the sacrificial X role — a very limited route-running assignment — so the raw target and reception totals need that context before they mean anything. He pairs it with Harmon's drops revision as a general lesson: charting gives you a deeper look than receptions, yards and touchdowns, but its real job is telling you which metrics matter in a specific context rather than on average.
- 2025-01-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-30]]): the X role in a Shanahan-branch offense is defined less by go routes and more by in-breakers — slants and digs on the move — which is how he says [[Chris Olave]] was used under Klint Kubiak in New Orleans in 2024, though it did not translate into production. He argues that makes the role a better fit than most systems for a receiver with [[DK Metcalf]]'s profile: still a good press-coverage player, but one who does not come back to the ball or play physically enough at the catch point to win as a pure boundary isolation receiver. Harmon also notes these offenses need a dirty-work receiver who blocks and mixes it up, naming Jake Bobo and [[Jauan Jennings]] as the archetype.

In Chicago, Harmon's ideal Ben Johnson alignment puts a boundary receiver on the outside as a sacrificial X, with [[Rome Odunze]] on the line of scrimmage in the slot and [[D.J. - 2025-02-26 — According to [[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-26]]: the hosts applied the sacrificial-X idea in two directions in one episode. Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) used it as an indictment of Arizona, saying they were giving [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] a route tree 'similar to a sacrificial X' — endless go routes — and asking what the point of that is with a receiver of his quality; [[Matt Harmon]] agreed the exclusive-X deployment is a misallocation. But both hosts also argued the role has genuine value when filled by the right body: the Rams, with [[Puka Nacua]] not a burner and [[Cooper Kupp]] leaving, specifically need 'somebody who's a sacrificial guy that can take the top off and make some of these safeties work', with Koh naming speed options like [[Marquise Brown]] and [[Dyami Brown]]. - 2025-02-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-28]]): the AFC East episode gave two working examples of the archetype. [[Keon Coleman]] is Harmon's model sacrificial X in Buffalo — a big-bodied outside receiver targeted on just 17.6 percent of routes who does not separate, but who 'dunks on you every now and again' and whose presence occupies the perimeter; Harmon compares him to a more well-rounded [[Gabe Davis]]. Conversely, Miami's problem is the absence of one: [[Jaylen Waddle]] is the on-the-ball X who gets the fewest schemed layups, so Harmon explicitly says the Dolphins 'should maybe add a sacrificial X' to free him. Jacksonville signed [[Gabe Davis]] as 'the number one sacrificial X receiver on the market' to free [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] to work off the line of scrimmage — the deployment worked even though the contract did not. Harmon proposes [[Nick Westbrook-Ikhine]] as an [[Alec Pierce]]-type X for Tennessee, 'a little sacrificial but can also make big plays'. And he revisits his own past line that Pierce was 'something worse than a sacrificial X receiver', now arguing the more important effect is downstream: a good-enough sacrificial X is one you cannot remove from the field, which stunts the development of the player behind him — [[Adonai Mitchell]] behind Pierce, [[Dontayvion Wicks]] behind [[Romeo Doubs]].
- 2025-03-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-13]]): the 2025 free agency middle class was, in Koh's phrase, 'sacrificial X receiver alley'. Harmon's cleanest example is [[Justin Watson]], who ran a route on 58.4% of Kansas City dropbacks but was targeted on only 8% of them — versus roughly 10% for [[JuJu Smith-Schuster]] and near 24% for [[DeAndre Hopkins]] — an enormous cardio load for almost no footballs, yet valuable because it clears space. The function explains a run of signings: [[Marquez Valdes-Scantling]] to Seattle purely to stretch the field for [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]], [[Dyami Brown]] to Jacksonville to hold the perimeter so [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] can take slot reps, and [[Mack Hollins]] to New England as a pure perimeter body. - 2025-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-19]]): Seattle's signing of [[Marquez Valdes-Scantling]] is a textbook application — in three-receiver sets MVS is the sacrificial X, a lid lifter whose function is clearing space underneath for [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] and [[Cooper Kupp]] rather than generating targets himself. Harmon adds a structural point about the Kubiak/Shanahan tree: the farthest-outside receiver in these offenses is frequently condensed to the line of scrimmage, so a converted slot player moving outside is not simply exposed to press the way a traditional X would be. - 2025-04-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-11]]): many sacrificial X receivers come out of the straight-line X archetype, and NFL offenses are moving away from using that body type as a true number-one read in their concepts. Harmon sketches the archetype as a spectrum — [[DK Metcalf]] at the peak, [[Mike Williams]] in the upper band, [[Alec Pierce]] in the low-middle, and busts like Terrace Marshall at the bottom — and argues the range of outcomes is what makes these prospects volatile: no floor, but a big hit when it lands. He applies it to [[Tre Harris]], whose 56% go/curl/screen route diet and sub-8% out-breaking rate are exactly what the archetype looks like coming out of college.

- 2025-05-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-16]]): [[Xavier Legette]]'s rookie season is a clean case study in a team being forced to burn a non-X receiver at X. - 2025-05-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-28]]): the episode is a league-wide survey of the role. Harmon's clearest example is [[Tre Tucker]], who at 5-foot-9, 185 ran 639 routes — the most of any third-year receiver — with only 23 slot snaps, functioning purely to clear space for [[Jakobi Meyers]], [[Jack Bech]] and [[Brock Bowers]]. He argues the sacrificial X does not need to be a high-target player, which is why a blocker with catch-point issues like [[Elic Ayomanor]] can hold the job in Tennessee, why [[Mack Hollins]] can absorb the snaps in New England, and why a depth body like [[Gabe Davis]] is a reasonable Vegas signing. The counterweight is size: Harmon says small receivers can separate as an X — [[Elijah Moore]] proved it as a rookie — but quarterbacks will not throw those windows, so you effectively have to be Antonio Brown for it to work.
- 2025-06-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-13]]): the Colts and Packers are the two cleanest live examples of why the sacrificial X theory exists, a framework Harmon says he has been pushing for two years. Indianapolis needs [[Alec Pierce]] to stretch the field so [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] as flanker and [[Josh Downs]] in the slot can operate underneath — Harmon points to the 2023 Gardner Minshew season, when Pierce barely saw the football but cleared enormous space for Pittman's production. The cost is that the genuinely better separator ends up rationed: Downs ran just 16 routes from one- or two-receiver sets in two seasons, [[Jayden Reed]] seven. Harmon's own preference would be to make Pittman the X in two-receiver sets and slide Downs to flanker, but notes teams are generally hesitant to make the X the player who rotates out in 11 personnel.
- 2025-06-27 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-27]]): 'developmental' should not be read as 'cannot play for three years' — some developmental players can play early precisely because you put very little on their plate, sending them out as a sacrificial X to run a small handful of routes very well and stretch the defense for the real target earner, with route-tree depth, break timing and release refinement added later. He applied this to [[Tre Harris]] behind [[Ladd McConkey]], hoping for [[Alec Pierce]]-style output if [[Mike Williams]] misses time. [[Matt Waldman]] made the complementary point that the X role itself suppresses fantasy value — [[Rome Odunze]] gets the lower-percentage downfield targets in Chicago, which is why he projects him at only ~100 targets while giving the flanker/slot pieces more.
- 2025-07-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-11]]): Harmon applies the sacrificial X idea to Detroit, arguing [[Jameson Williams]] would be maximized with fewer X snaps (he was on the line of scrimmage on 66.5% of snaps) and more flanker work, but that [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] must hold the flanker-slot role, so the team needs a dedicated big-bodied boundary receiver to absorb the hardest press-man assignments. Detroit has repeatedly chased that archetype — Allen Robinson, [[Tim Patrick]], and now possibly third-rounder [[Isaac TeSlaa]] on size and athleticism. Harmon ties this to the league-wide condensed-formation trend, noting Detroit ran one of the most condensed offenses in the NFL last year and expects that to persist under John Morton.
- 2025-07-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-07-23]]): the sacrificial X — a vertical outside receiver who occupies the safety so the real number one can be moved around — is the missing piece he keeps identifying across the NFC West. Seattle bought one in [[Marquez Valdes-Scantling]], whom he calls a diet-Coke [[Alec Pierce]], to stretch the field while [[Cooper Kupp]] works underneath and [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] takes the intermediate. Arizona did not: Harmon says it would have been nice if the Cardinals had added one sacrificial vertical X to let [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] come off the line of scrimmage, and the absence is the core structural criticism of that room. In Los Angeles, James Koh (not a tracked expert) applies the label to [[Tutu Atwell]] but refines it to 'sacrificial slot', since Atwell moves around rather than lining up at X.
- 2025-07-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-30]]): big-bodied secondary receivers can absorb the outside X snaps so the true number one gets moved around the formation. He names [[Xavier Legette]] as the candidate to do it in Carolina for [[Tetairoa McMillan]], and [[Jayden Higgins]] for Houston, and suggests Atlanta could use [[D.J. Chark]] — a non-separation X for most of the last few years of his career — for that role, though he laments the Falcons never added a genuine speed vertical X. The value is not in the sacrificial player's own production but in freeing the alpha to work from the slot; Harmon frames Atlanta's inability to do this once [[Darnell Mooney]] got hurt as the real cost of the injury.
- 2025-08-01 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-01]]): Harmon applies the sacrificial X role across three AFC South teams as the enabling piece for a better receiver's mobility. [[Alec Pierce]] is the archetype — a deep-threat X who does one thing repeatedly and does it well, whose ~23 yards per catch is partly a function of [[Anthony Richardson]] happily throwing the clear-out route. [[Dyami Brown]] fills the same slot in Jacksonville, in Harmon's words in the Pierce mold, so [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] and [[Travis Hunter]] can take inside reps. Tennessee's version was [[Nick Westbrook-Ikhine]], who Harmon notes drew nine touchdowns on 60 targets in that role — a number both hosts call absurd — with [[Van Jefferson]] or rookie [[Elic Ayomanor]] likely inheriting it so [[Calvin Ridley]] can lean flanker. The general principle Harmon states is that you do not want your best receiver stuck as the static on-line X.
- 2025-08-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-04]]): applied to Chicago, the X receiver in a short-passing offense absorbs the low-percentage vertical work that clears space but does not convert efficiently into fantasy points. Waldman uses this to argue [[Rome Odunze]] is overvalued — he is the X, and Ben Johnson's offense does not throw downfield the way Joe Burrow does to [[Ja'Marr Chase]] — while [[D.J. Moore]] (back at flanker rather than miscast at X, as he was in 2024) and [[Luther Burden III]] should benefit from the volume of short throws. He also uses the same lens on [[Michael Pittman Jr.]], whose damage comes mostly on over and crossing routes rather than as a singular primary ball winner.
- 2025-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-06]]): Miami is Harmon's live example of the archetype — because [[Tyreek Hill]] has not been an on-line player in three seasons as a Dolphin (over 60 percent on the line just once in his career, in Kansas City, and even then a 44 percent slot player), somebody else has to absorb the on-line X reps in three-receiver sets. Harmon nominates [[Nick Westbrook-Ikhine]] for that job, arguing the value of the role is not the target volume but what it frees up: it lets [[Jaylen Waddle]] get the role catering Harmon wants for him without disturbing Hill's alignment.
- 2025-08-07 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-07]]): the sacrificial-decoy idea extends to running backs used as receivers. He described [[Brashard Smith]] in Kansas City as a player whose value will come largely from putting stress on backers and safeties to get [[Rashee Rice]] and [[Travis Kelce]] open and to buy [[Patrick Mahomes]] time, rather than from his own catches — valuable to the Chiefs offense, much less so to fantasy GMs. He applied the same framing to [[De'Von Achane]], calling him likely to be 'the sacrificial lamb more than we'd like' with [[Jaylen Waddle]] the biggest beneficiary. [[Matt Waldman]] separately applied the label to [[Matthew Golden]] in Green Bay, suggesting Golden may be more of a sacrificial X while [[Jayden Reed]] does the real damage. The upside for the decoy is that a Cover 0 look can still be punished by a wheel route with good ball placement — Angelo said that is 'probably six points.'
- 2025-08-08 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-08]]): Harmon applies the sacrificial X idea to Dallas's third receiver spot — if you want [[George Pickens]] and [[CeeDee Lamb]] aligned together in stacks and bunches so neither gets pressed, somebody has to clear space on the back side. He names [[Romeo Doubs]] and [[Kayshon Boutte]] as the archetype and thinks [[Jalen Tolbert]] can do a passable version of it, since the job mostly demands running the slants and curls the offense asks for rather than explosiveness. He also frames the reverse benefit: giving a zone defense two dangerous receivers to account for on one side can lift a poor zone beater's success rate.
- 2025-08-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-13]]): among receivers with 40-plus receptions in 2024, the players clustered at the bottom of slot usage were all true or sacrificial Xs — [[Rashad Bateman]] dead last at 7.7%, with [[Quentin Johnston]], [[Romeo Doubs]], [[Kayshon Boutte]], [[DK Metcalf]] and [[Courtland Sutton]] the nearest names, none of the rest under 10%. Harmon's framing is that the role suppresses efficiency inputs by denying easy targets: Bateman still led the group with a first down or touchdown on 77.8% of receptions and a 40% explosive catch rate despite it, which he reads as evidence the role, not the player, is the limiter. He applied the same logic to [[Cedric Tillman]] settling in as an outside X (about 70% versus man is acceptable there) and to [[DeAndre Hopkins]], stuck on the line at 77.7% in Kansas City only because the roster had no other perimeter option.
- 2025-08-15 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-15]]): Harmon walks back the industry's use of his own sacrificial-X framing, saying the fantasy community has become down on X receivers and that it might be his fault and he may need to take accountability. His correction: the original point was to give overdue credit to slots and power slots — the [[Khalil Shakir]]s, [[Chris Godwin]]s, [[Rashee Rice]]s and an [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] who was dismissed as a fourth-round slot merchant — but the field has over-corrected into discounting X receivers wholesale. His line is that if the X receiver is good, they are not sacrificial, they are balling, and he applies it directly to [[Rome Odunze]] playing a critical downfield X role in Chicago. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) needles him for having fought Harmon on [[Alec Pierce]] using this same logic.
- 2025-08-25 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-25]]): applied to [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]] in Las Vegas. With [[Brock Bowers]] commanding the targets, Waldman expects Thornton's job to be stretching the field and run-blocking on the outside — clearing space rather than earning volume — which he distinguishes sharply from being a fantasy asset. He paired it with a separation critique: contested catches in preseason are not evidence of winning, and Vic Tafur of The Athletic noted Thornton struggled to create separation even against backups. Waldman's format split follows directly from the archetype: a sacrificial X with size and speed but no separation is a strong [[Best Ball]] play, where a handful of deep scores pay, and a poor weekly redraft starter. Bob Harris (Footballguys, NOT tracked) agreed with the best-ball framing while still taking Thornton as an 18th-19th round redraft flyer.
- 2025-08-27 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-27]]): discussing Las Vegas, Koh (not tracked) uses Harmon's own term back at him — [[Tre Tucker]] is the sacrificial X, a speed player whose job is to get long and force safeties out of the box rather than to win routes, which is why Harmon calls him 'not a needle mover whatsoever'. Harmon separates that from what he wants from [[Jack Bech]], a route runner who gets open in the short-to-intermediate area, and from [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]], the only true size-speed X body on the roster. The takeaway is that alignment and role, not raw camp buzz, determine which of the three actually accumulates targets — and that a sacrificial X can hold a starting job while offering nothing to fantasy.
- 2025-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-29]]): the sacrificial X is the roster slot teams fill to keep their best receivers off the line of scrimmage and out of press punishment. He expected [[Nick Westbrook-Ikhine]]'s size and vertical resume to make him Miami's sacrificial X, freeing [[Jaylen Waddle]] to move around; [[Malik Washington]] winning the job instead keeps Waddle a perimeter on-the-line player. Elsewhere he named [[Romeo Doubs]] as Green Bay's sacrificial-plus X ahead of [[Matthew Golden]], argued the Chargers should not make [[Ladd McConkey]] absorb X punishment, and — with James Koh (not tracked) — preferred [[Quentin Johnston]] over [[Zay Jones]] as Arizona's sacrificial X to free [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] to move.
- 2025-09-03 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-03]]): Harmon applied the sacrificial X idea twice in one episode. In Buffalo, [[Gabe Davis]] can play that role better than [[Elijah Moore]] or [[Curtis Samuel]], which is the only argument for him having value — it could free [[Keon Coleman]] to move around the formation. In Arizona, he argued the effect is measurable: in 2023 Jacksonville, [[Calvin Ridley]] produced markedly better week-to-week when Zay Jones was on the field, because one other credible body on the line of scrimmage running clear-outs stops the top receiver from being 'hung out to dry' as the extra guy. He expects Jones's arrival in Arizona to unlock formation movement for [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]], noting Arizona seems unwilling to use [[Michael Wilson]] that way.
