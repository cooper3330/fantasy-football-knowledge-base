## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-05-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-20]]): guest JJ Zachariason (Late Round Draft Guide, not a tracked expert) described his central lesson from years of building the guide — you become humbled by results, and fantasy football is not about precision but about being directionally right within a range of outcomes. He said he dislikes analytics work presented as black and white; data points you in a direction, it does not give the answer, and more than half the detailed stats that get clicks on Twitter are not predictive at all. His worked examples: he and Waldman both liked [[Isiah Pacheco]] and [[Puka Nacua]] pre-draft, but neither claimed a first-round rookie grade or predicted a record rookie season, and analysts do the audience a disservice by retroactively claiming they got it completely right. Fantasy edge is simply liking a player a little more than consensus. Waldman added the inverse case — a player he does not rate as a scout, such as [[Ollie Gordon II]], can still be a good directional bet if the scheme fit matches what he does well, ridden as a sell-high while it lasts.
- 2025-09-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-18 (Film & Theory)]]) relaying Adam Harstad (Football Guys, not a tracked expert) and his Dynasty and Theory article: fantasy is weird because it is dominated by randomness over tiny samples, and the danger peaks in September when one week is all the data there is. Harstad's examples: a 2011 dynasty team with the second-fewest points in the league backed into the playoffs on schedule luck and won the title on fluke weeks from Darrius Heyward-Bey, Brent Celek and a slow-starting rookie Julio Jones; Cleveland's Hail Mary specialist Frisman Jackson exploded in week one and meant nothing, while Miles Austin's 250-yard breakout was genuine signal. His prescription is to go re-read a week 11 from a past season, see how much oddity everyone has already forgotten, and use that as the baseline rate of weirdness that week one and week two results will also decay to. Harstad qualifies that 'random' means functionally random for prediction, not causeless — [[Ja'Marr Chase]] breaks a tackle because of athleticism and craft, not quantum mechanics. Waldman's addition: some apparent weirdness is film knowledge the industry has abandoned for box-score derivatives, e.g. 3-4 fronts are generally easier to run up the middle against and 4-3 fronts easier to run outside, which may be all that resolves an ambiguous backfield like Seattle's.
