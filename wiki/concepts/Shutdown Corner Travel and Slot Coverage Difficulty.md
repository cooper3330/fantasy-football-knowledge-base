# Shutdown Corner Travel and Slot Coverage Difficulty

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-31 — According to guest Patrick Peterson, the retired All-Pro cornerback (not a tracked expert), speaking on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-31]]: travelling shutdown corners have largely disappeared because offenses changed to hide from them. In his era (roughly 2010-2015) receivers averaged around 6-4 and 215 pounds, formations were generic, and splits were predictable — so he and Darrelle Revis could win with press. As he and Revis 'started to take the game over,' offenses compacted, shrank and moved receivers inside, into motion, into bunches and to the two, three or backside-solo spots specifically to deny press coverage and marquee matchups. Peterson's core insight is a route-tree asymmetry: from outside, splits tell you everything — at a plus-three split there are only three routes available, so coverage is a process of elimination. From the slot it is 'free game' — the whole tree plus picks, rubs, jerks and wheels — and the slot receiver plays off the nickel's leverage, which is itself dictated by inside help. That is why even elite corners like Patrick Surtain II do not follow power slots such as [[Cooper Kupp]] or [[Chris Godwin]] inside. [[Matt Harmon]] connected this to his own charting: the iso receiver-corner matchup he built [[Reception Perception Methodology]] around is rarer now, with softer zone and match coverage replacing true man.
- 2024-12-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-05]]): the [[Jerry Jeudy]] Denver game is the cleanest case yet for why WR/CB matchup analysis is so hard — most teams play more zone and do not move their top corner into the slot, so Jeudy took only 3 targets against Patrick Surtain II and 10 against everyone else. His practical framework across the week 14 slate: check whether the corner actually travels (A.J. Terrell does and has taken essentially no slot snaps; Denzel Ward stays on the left on 82 percent of snaps; Jalen Ramsey splits 24 percent slot and 34/34 boundary; Devon Witherspoon lives inside), then find the alignment that avoids him — push [[Justin Jefferson]] into the slot against Atlanta, motion [[Jerry Jeudy]] inside, move [[George Pickens]] around to face Martin Emerson instead of Ward. He also used Next Gen Stats tight window percentage as a corner-quality screen: Tariq Woolen first, Darrell Baker second, Witherspoon third, Ward fifth.
- 2024-12-12 — According to guest Patrick Peterson (former NFL cornerback, not a tracked expert) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-12]]: travelling with the opponent's number one is far harder than it is described. Once offenses knew he followed the number one to the line of scrimmage, they countered by motioning receivers inside and running more screens to pull him off, forcing him to play off — and playing off widens the job from narrow press vision to reading the lineman's hat, the quarterback's drop and the receiver's stem simultaneously. They also ran fourth and fifth receivers deep on him purely to drain his conditioning before bringing a Julio Jones or Calvin Johnson back on, and he could not come off the field. Peterson's test: not can you do it, but can you do it 65 plays a game, week in and week out — most corners do not even want to play both sides. [[Matt Harmon]] noted Jalen Ramsey is one of the few who does travel and also plays slot, though he is only about 24% slot in Miami's defense.
- 2024-12-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-19]]): Kansas City's use of Trent McDuffie is the current template for travel — a former slot corner now played all over the formation, who they stuck on [[Chris Olave]] and effectively dared the rest of New Orleans to beat them, and who Harmon expects to shadow [[Nico Collins]]. Harmon notes the tradeoff is visible in the splits: the Chiefs allow the fewest receptions and second-fewest yards per game to outside receivers and a 53.6% completion rate out wide, second best in the NFL, but the most yards per game to slot receivers — so travel buys perimeter suppression at the cost of the interior, and only helps if the opponent has someone to attack it. Harmon adds that a receiver who lives at X, like Collins, is easier to travel with than one who moves. Koh's counterpoint elsewhere: name recognition can misdirect assignments, as with Jalen Ramsey likely shadowing [[Deebo Samuel]] over the better-playing [[Jauan Jennings]].
- 2025-01-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-21]]): Kansas City traveled Trent McDuffie with [[Nico Collins]] for 22 of 32 routes (just under 69%) and gave up two catches for 42 yards in coverage. Harmon's point is that a shadow assignment is a statement about the rest of the roster as much as the target — Houston's supporting cast gave a defense no reason to fear conceding elsewhere, so putting your best corner on an island was close to free. He frames the same logic in reverse for Kansas City's approach to Houston's two perimeter corners: they simply threw at everybody else.
- 2025-02-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-05]]): Kansas City's use of Trent McDuffie as a travelling cover man on opposing No. 1 receivers is the single most exploitable structural feature of their defense, because it leaves the slot vulnerable — they surrendered the second-most receptions and sixth-most receiving yards to inside receivers in 2024. Harmon credits co-host James Koh with first putting his attention on the Chiefs' slot problem. The applied read for Super Bowl LIX: McDuffie travels with [[A.J. Brown]], so [[DeVonta Smith]], who takes roughly 55% of his snaps inside, draws defenders he can beat. Harmon pairs it with a coverage-shell caveat — the Chiefs had the eighth-highest man rate in the league but ran two-high on 66.7% of postseason opponent drop-backs, so travelling a corner does not necessarily mean a single-high man game.
- 2025-09-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-30]]): Harmon says too many people still believe the opposing number one corner simply travels with the receiver listed first on the depth chart, and that is not how it works — coverage follows alignment. The X receiver on the line of scrimmage draws the hard press-man assignment while the movable inside receiver gets easier looks, which is why [[Davante Adams]] absorbs the tougher matchups so [[Puka Nacua]] can be moved around, and why in Week 1 [[George Pickens]] took the harder outside coverage while [[CeeDee Lamb]] moved around and drew softer assignments. Harmon frames this as good coaching: you can play a versatile star at perimeter X, but you maximize him by moving him.
