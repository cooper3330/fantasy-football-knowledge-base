# Ball Security and Fumble Rate Grading

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-08-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-12]]): he had spent the past year or so considering whether the Rookie Scouting Portfolio penalizes running backs too heavily for ball security, and ran a study to test it — six years of top-60 fantasy running backs, 2018 through 2023, checked against their RSP ball-security grades. Result: he is not loosening the penalty. Roughly 90% of backs who made the top 60 over those six years had a college fumble rate better than one fumble per 60-61 touches. Only 10 backs graded below committee level for ball security became fantasy assets at all (RB2 through RB5), and only 3% of those below-committee backs ever made the top three. He concedes the correlation is imperfect and cites counterexamples — Tiki Barber corrected his fumbling, and Sonny Michel is often raised, though Waldman questions whether Michel ever became a real fantasy asset and notes injuries muddy it. Applied to [[Marshawn Lloyd]], whom Green Bay's staff said had an easily fixable issue.
- 2024-09-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-05 (Going Deep)]]): reviewing his own past RSP grades this summer, only about 3% of running backs who reached elite fantasy production over roughly the past six years had graded below an adequate tier of college ball security, and only about 10% of RB1/RB2 finishers did — grades taken in college, before any pro improvement. His second point is that fixing it late is costly: a back who has to focus on carrying the ball stops running fluidly, loses second-effort plays, and plays scared of being cut. He recounts a Russ Lande discussion of the flip side of the veteran-mentor archetype — the veteran chirping at a hot young back that they will cut him if he fumbles — and cites a running back who broke a long touchdown, fumbled, and came out after halftime carrying it tight and looking slow while a veteran took over the job. [[Kyle Monangai]]'s 410 carries without a fumble is the positive case.
- 2024-09-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-11]]): Waldman shared results of a study he ran over the summer across his RSP data on backs who produce fantasy value in the NFL. Of college backs who fell below an acceptable rate of ball security, only 3% ever earned elite fantasy production over the six-year window he studied, and only 10% earned even RB1 or RB2 production — meaning roughly 90% of backs below that ball-security tier never became top-24 fantasy starters. His explanation for why correcting a fumbling problem does not fully fix it: concentrating on holding the ball tends to alter how a back runs, so he reads the field less clearly or finishes less strongly. He allows exceptions — he named Tiki Barber changing how he carried the ball — but treats an acceptable college fumble rate as a threshold. He applied it to [[Tahj Brooks]], whose one fumble per 223 touches he called elite.
- 2025-04-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-03]]): his RSP study of six years of running backs and ball security found that backs falling below a certain fumble-rate threshold delivered even one top-24 fantasy season only about 10% of the time, and reached top-10-to-12 only about 3% of the time. He applied it to [[Bhayshul Tuten]], who is well below that threshold, and flagged the obvious follow-up he still wants to run: the 10% who did make it were probably highly rated players able to shore the problem up, so the threshold may function differently by talent tier. [[Brandon Angelo]] framed the same profile as physical measurables being present while the perceptual-cognitive layer — fumbles, stuff rate, key recognition — is not.
- 2025-04-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-21 (Film & Data with Dwain McFarland)]]): his baseline for an acceptable college fumble rate is one per 60 touches, and he deducts points below it — [[Jaydon Blue]] at one per 45.5 falls out of his top ten backs on that alone. Waldman cited a preliminary six-year study he ran for Football Guys on RSP evaluations: of the backs who fell below the one-per-60 mark, only 10% ever posted a top-24 fantasy season and only 3% a top-10 season. He conceded the study needs normalizing for draft capital, since a bad college fumbler taken late may simply never have been given an opportunity, and that guys who keep fumbling in practice never get on the field at all ([[Marshawn Lloyd]], David Wilson, Stevan Ridley as examples). Guest Dwain McFarland (Fantasy Life, NOT tracked) pushed that ball security ought to be coachable; Waldman agreed the technique is simple — high and tight, points of pressure — but said it degrades under dynamic movement when arm carriage becomes subconscious, and that over-correction is its own trap: he described Ty'Son Williams in Baltimore fumbling late in a first half and returning in the second half running 'like Tiki Barber', his style completely changed, because a runner has to operate at the speed of instinct.
- 2025-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-19]]): fumbling is the risk that most often makes a talented rookie back's outcome binary rather than a range. He jokes that [[Bhayshul Tuten]] and [[Jaydon Blue]] should race while carrying a greased watermelon to see who holds on and how many teammates they wipe out — adding that you would not even need the grease, just a football and maybe some talcum powder. For Tuten he states the two outcomes explicitly: a 1,200-yard rusher out of the gate if he holds the ball once an injury opens the door, or 30 carries with four fumbles and the team saying 'next year'. He raises the same concern about [[Cam Skattebo]], and notes that his own miss on [[Alvin Kamara]] came from exactly this — Kamara fumbled a lot and bounced everything outside playing hero ball before something switched, which is why Waldman treats fumbling as coachable rather than terminal.
