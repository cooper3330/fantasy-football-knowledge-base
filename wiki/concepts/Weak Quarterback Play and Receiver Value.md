# Weak Quarterback Play and Receiver Value

## Definition

How much a bad or backup quarterback should discount the receivers around him.
The claim tracked here is that managers systematically over-discount: modern
receiving talent is good enough at route running and catching that it raises
the level of quarterback play, rather than the quarterback capping the
receivers.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-18 — **Bob Harris** (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]: states it as a rule he set for himself before the 2023 season — "I was going to not let less-than-ideal quarterback situations scare me off of wide receivers." Credits the framing to Jeff Mans on SiriusXM's fantasy pre-game show: receiving talent "has come to a point where it can raise the level of quarterback play," making players who would formerly have been journeymen serviceable not just for NFL purposes but for fantasy purposes. Cites his own 2022 lessons with DK Metcalf and Tyler Lockett, and says the industry likely repeated the mistake in 2023 with Michael Pittman Jr. — "we really overplay that in our mind."

- 2023-12-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: supplies the mechanism from film. With Nick Mullens starting in Minnesota, he is comfortable starting all of [[Justin Jefferson]], [[T.J. Hockenson]] and [[Jordan Addison]] because "when you see that these players are reliable as route runners, it inspires confidence from even just NFL-caliber, not starting-caliber, quarterbacks to fit the ball in the tight spots — because the receivers are where they need to be."

- 2023-12-18 — **Matt Waldman**, same episode, on the size of the discount: it is real but small — bump each affected pass catcher **one tier** below his absolute ceiling and keep starting him, rather than benching him. The exception is the specific throw the backup can't make: [[Tee Higgins]] becomes boom/bust with [[Jake Browning]] because Browning doesn't throw the moving 25–40-yard ball that Joe Burrow does, while [[Ja'Marr Chase]] "is still going to be Ja'Marr Chase."

**Practical read:** discount for the *throws the quarterback cannot make*, not
for his name. Alpha receivers and reliable route runners survive a downgrade;
receivers whose value is concentrated in one throw type do not.

- 2024-03-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-12]]: groups [[Drake London]] and [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] with [[Terry McLaurin]], [[D.J. Moore]] and Allen Robinson (not tracked) as talented X-receivers whose true quality has been masked by a "carousel" of bad/rotating starting quarterbacks — arguing all are legitimate star-caliber No. 1s whose depressed box-score production is a quarterback problem, not a talent problem, and predicting that [[Kirk Cousins]] specifically should unlock London the way durable, drop-back-competent QB play would for any of them.

- 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-19]]: applies the framework to [[Curtis Samuel]]'s career-long charting profile (a consistent ~75%+ success rate vs. man coverage across four seasons, roughly 80th percentile) despite him never posting a 900-yard season — attributes the box-score/talent gap directly to the quality of his surrounding offenses and quarterbacks (Kyle Allen in 2019, a crowded/disorganized Washington passing game under Eric Bieniemi in 2023), contrasted with his one clearly good situation (Joe Brady's 2020 Carolina offense, his best statistical year) as proof the talent was constant and the QB/scheme was the variable.
- 2024-05-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-05-23]]): using [[Josh Downs]]' Gardner Minshew-quarterbacked rookie season as the example, Harmon argues weak QB play (which he calls a 'hard ceiling' on offense) suppresses receiver box-score production but should not be used to discount the underlying skill shown in charting data like separation success rate -- Downs still posted elite man/zone numbers despite the QB situation, and Harmon dismisses anyone adjusting his receiver evaluation downward for it as 'not watching the games.'
- 2024-06-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-06-03]]): Waldman restated a running thesis that fantasy managers overrate the downside risk of a shaky quarterback situation for an elite receiver, calling it 'more a mental thing than an actual thing.' He cited Amari Cooper's 100+ catch, WR18 finish on the NFL's worst-scoring offense (with Bryce Young) and Davante Adams' WR10 finish past age 30 as direct evidence, and extended the point to D.K. Metcalf and Tyler Lockett both posting top-15 seasons in 2022 under a Geno Smith many had written off beforehand. Co-host Bob Harris (not tracked) pushed back with a counter-example on [[Justin Jefferson]], noting Jefferson's per-game scoring actually dropped to WR3-level (down from elite WR1 pace) over the final month of last season while playing behind backup quarterbacks — arguing the effect is real, just usually overstated in degree rather than imaginary.
- 2024-06-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-06-06]]): using [[Nico Collins]]' first two seasons with Davis Mills and Kyle Allen at QB as the case study, Harmon argues most receivers 'cannot magic themselves the ball' through bad quarterback play and poor scheme -- low counting stats in that context shouldn't be read as a talent indictment. He explicitly carves out Justin Jefferson as the outlier who has produced through bad QB play, calling him 'the best receiver on the planet,' but says most good-not-elite receivers like Collins simply can't overcome the role/context they're given, and that role usage (e.g., Collins running 26% deep routes as a rookie with no QB who could connect deep) materially caps a receiver's statistical output independent of his separation ability.
- 2024-06-10 — According to Bob Harris (untracked co-host) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-06-10]]), arguing for [[Malik Nabers]] over [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] on opportunity cost: don't shy away from receivers paired with shaky quarterbacks, citing recent examples -- DJ Moore finished WR6 with Justin Fields/Tyson Bagent, Mike Evans finished WR7 with Baker Mayfield's unexpected 2022 season, Amari Cooper finished WR11 mostly with Jacoby Brissett, Tyler Lockett and DK Metcalf finished WR13/14 with Geno Smith outperforming expectations, and most notably Adam Thielen caught 103 balls and finished WR18 playing with Bryce Young's historically poor rookie season (11 TD/10 INT) behind the NFL's lowest-scoring offense. Harris's conclusion: he doesn't expect Daniel Jones or Drew Lock to be any worse than Young was, so WR15 is a realistic range for Nabers despite the quarterback situation. Waldman agreed and referenced a similar 1980s-era case in Josh Gordon's 87-catch, 1,646-yard, nine-touchdown 1980 season with Jason Campbell throwing to him despite a low catch rate on his targets.
- 2024-07-12 — According to JJ Zachariason (not a tracked expert) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-12]]): he quantified the rookie-QB drag on pass-catchers -- since 2014, only 4 of 159 WRs drafted in the top 72 at the position who scored 15+ PPR points/game came from a team with a rookie QB (300+ pass attempts) that season. Running backs on rookie-QB offenses have also underperformed ADP expectation at a higher rate, though less dramatically than WRs. His practical advice: it's fine to draft such players at or below ADP, but don't pay a premium expecting a big ceiling -- the effect (lower team-wide yards/play and touchdown rate) is real even though the market already prices some of it in.
- 2024-07-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-23]]): the AFC North preview repeatedly leaned on the rule that a bad passing offense supports only one productive receiver — 'you're lucky that you're getting one productive receiver out of these offenses, much less two.' He applies it to [[Elijah Moore]] twice over: behind [[Zach Wilson]]'s Jets, where [[Garrett Wilson]] absorbed what production there was, and now in a Cleveland offense he says has produced maybe six good quarters total under [[Deshaun Watson]]. He also warns Cleveland's four interesting pass catchers could all miss their potential in 2024 for the same reason, and that he can see the Browns finishing in either the top ten or bottom ten of offenses. The corollary he draws for scheme is that quarterback-friendly design — under-center play action, motion, manufactured touches — inflates receiver numbers, which is why he wants Cincinnati to keep the [[Jake Browning]]-era concepts for [[Ja'Marr Chase]] even though [[Joe Burrow]] prefers shotgun point-and-shoot.
- 2024-09-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-17]]): quarterback archetype sets which receivers in a room can be productive, independent of talent. In Indianapolis, [[Anthony Richardson]]'s inconsistent accuracy means Harmon lowers expectations for [[Michael Pittman Jr.]], [[Josh Downs]] and [[Adonai Mitchell]] alike, and he says outright that with [[Derek Carr]] under center instead he would have drafted all of these Colts receivers in fantasy — a lower-ceiling quarterback producing a higher fantasy floor. Conversely, Richardson's live arm is precisely what unlocks [[Alec Pierce]], whose vertical skill set was wasted on Indianapolis's previous dead-arm quarterbacks. The mirror case is [[Chris Olave]], excellent downfield but paired with a quarterback in Carr who does not throw downfield aggressively — Harmon says the charting backs this up and calls the pairing oil and water, adding that the industry overlooked the mismatch because the pre-Carr Saints quarterback situation was so untenable.
- 2024-09-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-19]]): the fixing-struggling-offenses segment is a study in quarterback-driven caps on receiver value. Harmon frames New England as an oil-and-water problem — [[Jacoby Brissett]] is a statuesque deep-shot thrower under pressure on 44.3 percent of dropbacks with no downfield separators. He says none of the Cleveland receivers matter until [[Deshaun Watson]] is functional, with the Browns 31st in dropback success rate and the scheme requiring under-center play he cannot provide. Conversely he shows the effect running positive: [[Anthony Richardson]]'s 12.8 average depth of target vs zone is what makes [[Alec Pierce]]'s limited nine-and-post profile productive, where any other quarterback would take the checkdown. He also invokes the touchdown-or-checkdown pattern for Tennessee under [[Will Levis]].
- 2024-09-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-25]]): Harmon's sharpest framing yet of this idea is that not all bad quarterbacks are created equal, and analysts wrongly lump them together when projecting receivers. [[Daniel Jones]] is inaccurate and overpaid but functional, with the arm to reach any part of the field, and Koh adds that a quarterback who cannot threaten the full field lets the defense defend a truncated area. That is a different tier from [[Zach Wilson]] or Desmond Ridder, whom [[Garrett Wilson]] and [[Drake London]] have had to play with and with whom no functional offense is possible. The second half of the framework is receiver fit: Harmon said at the draft that [[Malik Nabers]] was uniquely skilled to survive, not thrive, with the Giants, because a receiver who can be schemed off the line, win immediately against zone and produce after the catch — the [[Khalil Shakir]] archetype he wrote about — extracts far more from a marginal quarterback than a classic boundary X like [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] or [[Rome Odunze]] would.
- 2024-11-14 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2024-11-14]]): the Giants are the case study for how quarterback and personnel infrastructure caps a receiver's charted output. Harmon argues [[Malik Nabers]]'s numbers should be read against an offense that has been reduced to spamming him the ball because the surrounding cast — [[Darius Slayton]] as a solid role player and [[Wan'Dale Robinson]], whom Harmon dismisses as a fake receiver — cannot support high-level route concepts and [[Daniel Jones]] cannot execute them. His broader point is that drop rate and contested-catch rate are contaminated by quarterback behaviour, so the honest evaluation requires showing the open routes where the throw never came. He also frames the possible upside case: a quarterback change may improve the vibes, the aura in the room and therefore the RP numbers, even if he does not rate [[Drew Lock]] as an upgrade on talent.
- 2024-12-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-17]]): quarterback change can move a receiver dozens of spots in the yardage rankings independent of the receiver's own play — his case study is [[Jerry Jeudy]], 44th in receiving yards in Weeks 1-8 and fourth before last week once [[Jameis Winston]] took over, which Harmon calls a Ponzi scheme for receiver production. The corollary is that the gains are fragile: Winston wears out his welcome on turnovers, so the volume that inflates a receiver's line can vanish with the starter. Harmon also worked the inverse case in this episode — Seattle's protection problems capping [[DK Metcalf]]'s downfield work, and [[Jalen Hurts]]' declined throws capping [[A.J. Brown]] — treating receiver production as substantially a function of who is throwing and how the pocket holds.
- 2024-12-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-19]]): two same-week benchings gave a clean before/after on how much quarterback play drives receiver output. [[Jerry Jeudy]] went from 44th in NFL receiving yards through Week 8 and a 646-yard pace to a roughly 1,900-yard, 119-reception 17-game pace across seven [[Jameis Winston]] starts, and Harmon splits the credit between Winston's willingness to rip it (six-plus air yards per attempt, third in the league) and Cleveland's changed deployment. On the other side, [[Kirk Cousins]]' dead-last intermediate EPA neutralized [[Drake London]]'s designed middle-of-field role entirely. Harmon's generalizable point is that a scheme role only exists if the quarterback can physically execute the throws it depends on — the top-of-drop, plant-and-drive throw between zones — and that receiver production tied to one quarterback's specific traits can vanish overnight, as it did when Dorian Thompson-Robinson (minus 1.03 air yards per attempt) took over.
- 2024-12-30 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-30]]): he has always ignored advice to avoid pass catchers on teams with bad quarterback play, especially rookies. Co-host Bob Harris (NOT a tracked expert) presents the season-by-season evidence: [[Brock Bowers]] setting rookie tight end records with nondescript quarterbacks, [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] emerging with Mac Jones after losing [[Trevor Lawrence]], [[Malik Nabers]] producing across four quarterbacks including a since-released [[Daniel Jones]], [[Adam Thielen]] finishing WR17 (WR12 through 12 games) with a rookie [[Bryce Young]], [[Mike Evans]] with [[Baker Mayfield]], and [[DK Metcalf]] and [[Tyler Lockett]] both top-15 with [[Geno Smith]]. Waldman's refinement: the effect is a *ceiling* discount, not a production one — Bowers, [[Trey McBride]] and Nabers would all be nearer career years with quarterbacks who could feed them efficiently. Both agree the practical application is at the draft table, where perception of quarterback situation was the only thing separating [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] in early round two from Nabers in round four; in-season you can weigh the quarterback week to week, but do not be dismissive there either.
- 2025-01-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-23]]): Waldman uses Arizona to quantify how much a non-distributing quarterback suppresses an entire receiving corps. His thought experiment gives [[Joe Burrow]] the Cardinals' pass catchers: [[Michael Wilson]] produces close to what [[Tee Higgins]] did, [[Trey McBride]] roughly doubles his touchdowns, and [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] — while not equalling [[Ja'Marr Chase]] — clears 1,000 and probably reaches 1,200 yards. The mechanism he names is rhythm and even distribution rather than raw arm talent. [[Brandon Angelo]] adds that [[Kyler Murray]] can never play in rhythm consistently and is neutering good players, and that the fix is not development but replacement — he would take [[Shedeur Sanders]] purely on fit, because Sanders gets the ball to playmakers. The practical rule both imply: discount a receiver's fantasy projection to his quarterback's distribution, and treat a quarterback change as the unlock event.
- 2025-02-17 — According to untracked co-host Bob Harris on [[Matt Waldman]]'s show ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-17]]) [Best Ball]: [[Malik Nabers]] and [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] are 'poster boys for my argument that quarterback play should not scare you away from wide receivers' — Nabers produced with four different quarterbacks, none good, and Thomas did the bulk of his best work with Mac Jones, probably because the staff recognized double-digit targets to him were a good thing and Jones could throw enough passes even if they were not great ones. Waldman's parallel framing was that Nabers did it essentially one-handed and that upgrades should raise his efficiency and big plays. Note the counterexample in the same episode: Waldman reversed off [[T.J. Hockenson]] purely on quarterback uncertainty, so neither host applies the rule uniformly across positions.
- 2025-05-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-12]]): removing a bad quarterback does not automatically restore a receiver's value. On [[Derek Carr]]'s retirement he used the bombing of Dresden analogy — the bombing is over, you still have to deal with the cleanup — arguing that [[Chris Olave]] should not be moved up boards because none of the replacement options is clearly better and the room still has to process and execute together. He applied the same logic to [[Rashid Shaheed]], whose value is contingent on [[Tyler Shough]] actually being a functional downfield thrower. The inverse case in the same episode was [[Jerry Jeudy]], whose production came largely with [[Jameis Winston]] and now depends on [[Joe Flacco]].
- 2025-06-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-23]]): he quantifies the receiver tax of a quarterback who will not work through progressions from inside the pocket. [[Michael Wilson]] wanting to move from 11.7 to 13-15 yards per catch is framed as mostly not his problem — with a quarterback who climbs one or two steps and reaches the second or third read, Waldman thinks Wilson is an 800-900 yard receiver instead of a 600-yard one, naming Tampa Bay's number three role, Cleveland with [[Joe Flacco]], the Rams opposite [[Puka Nacua]] and [[C.J. Stroud]] as environments that would show it. The same logic underpins his skepticism about [[Garrett Wilson]] committing long-term to the Jets with [[Justin Fields]], and about [[Kyler Murray]] supporting more than one fantasy receiver.
- 2025-06-25 — According to guest Cory Kinnan (Reception Perception QB charting, NOT a tracked expert) with [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-25]]): Kinnan argues some receivers are simply 'teflon' — his example is [[Mike Evans]] producing a thousand yards with Jameis Winston, Ryan Fitzpatrick and every other quarterback on the list — and puts [[Garrett Wilson]] in that group given his catch-point and body-control wins. His softer version is that [[Justin Fields]], whatever his flaws, reliably finds his best playmaker on must-have downs, so he would not bet on Fields but would still bet on Wilson. Harmon's counterweight is the timing risk: some receivers reach the right situation too late (his example is Allen Robinson) while others get there in time (Terry McLaurin), and he warns against over-indexing on the [[D.J. Moore]] 2023 season as proof Fields elevates receivers. Both agree the more important variable is the play caller's design, not the quarterback's raw ability.
- 2025-08-20 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-20]]): the Colts are Harmon's case study that a quarterback change alone does not free a pass-catching room. Indianapolis has 'a treasure trove' of pass catchers and would be one of the league's most exciting offenses with league-average quarterback play, but Harmon does not expect they will get it from [[Daniel Jones]] — so he refuses the 'wheels up for [[Josh Downs]]' framing. His mechanism is specific: the ceiling comes from driving accurate on-time balls through zone over the middle (what [[Josh Allen]] does, what [[Gardner Minshew]] did adequately for [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] on intermediate crossers) and from a vertical threat, and Jones grades below league average on digs, comebacks and curls with nothing on go routes. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) counters with the coaching-environment case — the Kevin O'Connell exposure and Shane Steichen's offensive record — as reason to hope for the 'Minshew minimum'.
- 2025-09-05 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-05]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the standard bullish argument — this receiver is QB-proof, look what he did with bad quarterbacks, now he gets an upgrade — may invert. Harris' worry about [[Malik Nabers]] and [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] is that their historic rookie volume existed precisely because the Giants and Jaguars were awful and 'nothing else worked'; a more functional offense spreads the ball, so the rising tide 'doesn't necessarily lift his boat,' and the volume they got 'didn't work' anyway since both teams picked in the top five. He anchors it in repeated market mistakes: second-round prices on [[Tee Higgins]], [[DeVonta Smith]] and [[Chris Olave]] after volume-heavy seasons on bad teams, plus [[Brock Bowers]]. Guest JEFF ERICKSON (RotoWire, NOT tracked) adds the play-caller wrinkle — Liam Coen's bubble screens may go to [[Travis Hunter]] instead.
- 2025-09-16 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-16]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the [[Joe Burrow]] injury is his cleanest statement of the principle — you play your backup quarterback and your offense gets worse, that is why he is the backup. Maybe not in every quarter, but over the sweep of a season, everybody's path narrows and every skill player's floor drops, and the further down the target depth chart you are, the rockier it gets. He explicitly rejects the old dum-dum heuristic that the backup QB's friend among the receivers is a buy. Concretely: he doubts [[Ja'Marr Chase]] goes first overall if [[Jake Browning]] were the quarterback all year, and says [[Chase Brown]]'s road gets harder too — the whole operation downticks and there is not much you can do about it beyond a slight-discount sale if someone will give you real players. He applies the same logic to Cleveland, where [[Joe Flacco]]'s dreadful game is not the kind of effort that keeps any of his pass catchers worthwhile for fantasy.
- 2025-09-22 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-22]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: several Week 3 receivers were separated from their production purely by quarterback play. [[Malik Nabers]], a first-rounder in many leagues, managed one three-yard catch because Russell Wilson threw an end-zone interception hunting him and then flung him into double coverage without, in Harris's view, making a read. [[Marvin Mims Jr.]] got open deep twice and [[Courtland Sutton]] once, and [[Bo Nix]] — who Harris says lacks arm strength and therefore overcompensates and loses touch on deep balls — missed all three. Guest Patrick Doherty (Rotoworld, not tracked) applied the same logic to [[Calvin Ridley]], who got free down the sideline only for [[Cam Ward]] to miss the throw, and to [[Ja'Marr Chase]] with [[Jake Browning]] replacing [[Joe Burrow]].
- 2025-09-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-23]]): bad quarterback play should not be treated as a blanket statement, because what matters is which specific throws a quarterback will and will not make. [[Malik Nabers]] thrived on quick hitters, easy reads and RPOs with [[Daniel Jones]], a quarterback the industry dismissed and who is now a dark-horse MVP candidate in a well-designed Indianapolis offense; with [[Russell Wilson]], whose menu is vertical shots and checkdowns, the same receiver's usage becomes volatile at nearly 17 air yards per target for the season. Harmon's structural conclusion is about landing spots: a receiver drafted to a team with neither an established quarterback nor a young one to groom can be productive and still never reach his ceiling — he pegged Nabers's true ceiling at roughly 1,500 yards and 13 touchdowns versus last year's 109/1,200/7.
- 2025-10-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-06]]): Waldman applied the quarterback-ceiling argument to two receivers in one episode. He agreed that [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]]'s full potential likely will not be tapped until Arizona moves on from [[Kyler Murray]] — he believes Harrison can give a quarterback what [[Tee Higgins]] gives [[Joe Burrow]], but only if that quarterback can find him in a variety of ways and open the offense up to create easier opportunities. He made a parallel point about Tennessee, where [[Calvin Ridley]] looked good against coverage but both hosts warned the offense is still not fantasy friendly. The concept cuts the other way too: Waldman was untroubled by [[Jake Browning]] starting for [[Ja'Marr Chase]], on the grounds that a genuinely elite separator and YAC receiver survives worse quarterback play than a schemed-open one does.
