# Reception Perception Methodology

## Definition

[[Matt Harmon]]'s receiver-charting project (published at ReceptionPerception.com)
and his standing analytical framework on this show. Every route is charted from
all-22 tape and receivers are graded on **success rate versus coverage type**
(press / man / zone) rather than on raw box-score production, with further
splits by **route type** (e.g. curl-route success rate as a share of routes
run) and **alignment usage** (X / flanker / slot, plus motion and pre-snap
usage). Prospects are typically charted on a 3-game sample first — enough,
per Harmon, "to tell if a guy can play and if he cannot" — before expanding to
a full ~8-game sample for the site's published profile. Cross-class prospect
grades accumulate on Harmon's "stacked board," a running ranked list across
draft classes; a **tier-one** grade means a player is worth picking in the
top 10 as a "ready-made star." The site's charting approach extends to other
positions via other contributors — e.g. Derrick Klassen's quarterback
success-rate-against-pressure work — though that is not Harmon's own
methodology.

- 2024-01-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-01-18]]): the fullest demonstration of the methodology yet, applied to three early 2024 draft prospects ([[Marvin Harrison Jr.]], [[Malik Nabers]], [[Rome Odunze]]) off explicitly early 3-game samples. Concrete metrics named on air: **success rate vs. press coverage** (Harrison Jr.'s on pace for the best of the last 4 classes), **success rate on a specific route type as a share of total routes charted** (Nabers: 92% success on curl routes, 22.7% of his sample), and **alignment share** (Nabers over 40% of snaps in the slot; Harrison Jr. profiles as more X-only for now). Confirms the process: 3-game samples are enough "to tell if a guy can play," full site profiles come later off ~8 games. Introduces the **"stacked board"** — Harmon's running cross-class prospect ranking, where a **tier-one** grade means "worth picking in the top 10... a ready-made star." Only five receivers had earned that grade across the 2021-2023 classes as of this episode: Ja'Marr Chase (#1 on the board), Chris Olave, DeVonta Smith, [[Drake London]], and Garrett Wilson (Olave/Smith/Wilson not tracked in this wiki). See [[Ja'Marr Chase]] and [[Drake London]] for the player-page application.

- 2024-03-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-07]]): applies the full stat suite (success rate vs. man/zone/press, route-type share, alignment share) to explain a deployment-driven down year for two veterans rather than a talent decline — [[Calvin Ridley]]'s press-coverage success rate fell ~10 points on a route tree that was 48.6% comeback/curl/9-route and under 10% slants while lined up outside 81.8% of snaps; [[Marquise Brown]]'s slant success rate and after-contact tackle-breaking numbers both declined while Arizona lined him up on the line 77.4% of snaps despite him never topping 65% success vs. press in his career. Both used as examples of alignment/route-tree usage materially distorting a receiver's charted output relative to his true talent level. See [[Scheme vs Talent]].

- 2024-03-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-12]]: applies three straight seasons of charted data to [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] — success rate vs. man coverage of 74.3%/74.2%/74.8% across 2021-2023, over 80% vs. zone and press each year, and a 2023 route tree that was 61.4% slants/curls/digs (12.8% digs, 20% curls, 28.6% slants) — as evidence his production ceiling is capped by quarterback play rather than talent, comparing his usage profile to a "Bud Light version" of peak Michael Thomas (not tracked) with the New Orleans Saints.

- 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-19]]): puts an explicit percentile calibration on the man-coverage success-rate scores for the first time on air — any charted success rate vs. man coverage above roughly 75% pushes a receiver into "the 80th percentile," a threshold he says separates merely good charted receivers from extremely good ones. Applied live to [[Keenan Allen]] (dig/curl/slant/flat scores in the 83-93% range) and [[Curtis Samuel]] (a career-long ~75%+ success rate vs. man).

- 2024-04-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-04]]): a historical validation study of the methodology itself, sparked by a Discord member (J.P. Bennett). Looking back across charted prospects from the 2016-2018 and last four draft classes (~100 total), Harmon found 32 players who finished below the 35th percentile in two of the three main coverage metrics (success rate vs. man/zone/press) in college. Of those 32, only 9 (28%) had "moments" in the league, and only 5 (15%) are "inarguable hits": [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]], [[Cooper Kupp]], [[Rashee Rice]], Tyler Boyd and Juju Smith-Schuster (neither Boyd nor Smith-Schuster tracked in this wiki). Harmon's read: RP is "pretty damn accurate" at the NFL level once charted, but its college-level signal is weaker because of the gap in competition/coaching level, and college non-separators specifically need a "big slot" role transition to hit — every one of the five hits made that move. Applied directly to [[Keon Coleman]]'s 2024 prospect profile. See [[Scouting Bias and Player Archetypes]].

- 2024-04-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on *Reception Perception: The Show* ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-11]]): a charting-rules clarification prompted by [[Adonai Mitchell]]'s and [[Xavier Worthy]]'s Texas tape — declines to chart a receiver "sauntering" through a route with no real intent to be thrown the ball as an actual route attempt for success-rate purposes, since it isn't a legitimate route the quarterback could have targeted. Also applies the man/press/zone success-rate framework and route-type-share splits in full to Mitchell's finished profile: 84th percentile vs. man, 83rd vs. press, only 31st vs. zone; 82.8% of snaps on the line (highest of any prospect charted this cycle); 35% of routes run downfield (corner/post/nine) and 52% short/intermediate in-breakers, with 82.4%/89.5% success rates on digs/outs specifically.
- 2024-04-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-25]]): explained his contested-target charting rule on air -- a target only counts as "contested" if it was a genuinely winnable ball; short/underthrown passes a receiver has to reach through a defender for aren't counted, because the goal is isolating receiver skill from surrounding variables like quarterback accuracy. He noted this can make his contested-catch and contested-target rates diverge from other outlets' numbers, and can make specific players (e.g. [[Javon Baker]] at UCF) look worse by raw rate than the tape suggests when their own quarterback play was poor.
- 2024-06-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-06-04]]): when a full-season sample is skewed by a clear in-season inflection point, Harmon splits it to isolate signal -- for [[Jameson Williams]] he charted weeks 11-13 separately from a week-15-on sample and found the route-tree strengths/weaknesses looked similar between the two but the overall success-rate numbers diverged enormously (season-long ~57%/72%/53% vs. man/zone/press vs. a week-15-on 67%/85%/65%), because the early sample was 'non-functional NFL player' level bad. Harmon's stated benchmarks for a good player remain ~70% success rate vs. man and ~80% vs. zone. He also cautioned against overweighting how a sample ends just because it's most recent, comparing it to overreacting to a team's late-season hot streak, even while conceding the week-15-on data for Williams is more representative of his current baseline than the full-season figure.
- 2024-06-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-06-06]]): year-over-year RP success-rate metrics are largely 'sticky' -- most receivers stay in the same performance neighborhood from season to season until they age out, citing players like Josh Dotson and Chris Olave tracking eerily close to prior charting. He treats a large single-year jump (like Nico Collins' from 68% to 77.6% vs. man) as rare but not necessarily fluky when it's paired with individual route-level dominance rather than scheme/context alone; he references Brandon Cooks' 2017-18 jump (50.7% to 64.7% vs. man) as possibly the largest single-season jump in RP history as a historical comp.
- 2024-07-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-25]]): two methodological points surface. First, zone-coverage success rates are compressed — the difference between a 76.4% score (32nd percentile) and 78.8% (51st percentile) is small in absolute terms but moves a receiver from the bottom third to mid-pack, because most receivers clump together on that axis. Second, route charts must be read against usage and alignment: [[Rashid Shaheed]]'s all-red out-breaking routes partly reflect running nearly 40% nine/post/corner, and the joke that he cannot turn left ignores that receivers line up on both sides of the field. Harmon adds that in-breaking specialists with weak deep out-breaking routes are common even among great receivers, citing Allen Robinson and pre-2018 [[Davante Adams]], and that a 75/80 man-zone split is the shape typical of the game's top receivers.
- 2024-07-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-30]]): the 'quick and dirty' screen for whether a receiver is worth betting on is an outside receiver beating man coverage at over 70% success rate — that alone puts a player in the interested bucket, which is how [[Dontayvion Wicks]] (72.2%) qualified. For slot receivers the bar he wants is over 80% success rate vs zone. He also stresses sample construction matters as much as the headline number: he samples the first four and last four games of a season, and he warns that a full-season aggregate can hide two different players, as with [[Jameson Williams]], whose first three charted games were 'sub-[[Quentin Johnston]]' while his final five were above average across the board. He notes the site is losing Derek Klassen's quarterback charting to Robert Mays' show but that quarterback data will continue, and repeats that year-one to year-two route-running leaps are a real, observed pattern in the data.
- 2024-08-01 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-01]]): Harmon restates the interpretive thresholds for route success rates — anything above 55% is good, and once you break 60% it is really good — and reminds new listeners that percentile scores run the normal direction, so 1st percentile is the floor and 99th the ceiling. He uses a 70% vs man / 80% vs zone split as the rough marker of a functional NFL receiver. He also names two recurring cautions: press-coverage success rate must be read against opportunity, since top receivers like [[Tank Dell]] (8.3% of sampled snaps pressed), [[Keenan Allen]] (11.3%), [[A.J. Brown]] (12.1%) and [[Zay Flowers]] (12.2%) barely see it and can be schemed away from it; and aging-curve analysis is the work he is least confident in. He coins 'route shrub' for a route tree as narrow as [[Rashee Rice]]'s 38.3% slant rate, and notes in-season rookie charting is partial-sample until full profiles land before the August 26 last call.
- 2024-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-06]]): Harmon states the benchmarks he uses to separate great receivers from good ones — roughly 72% success rate vs man and 80% vs zone. He also notes how rare a full sweep is: clearing the 90th percentile against man, zone and press in a single season has happened only nine times since 2014, by [[Davante Adams]], [[Justin Jefferson]] (twice), [[Stefon Diggs]] (twice), Antonio Brown (twice), [[Michael Thomas]] and now [[Brandon Aiyuk]] — and never by Odell Beckham Jr. He cautions that alignment constrains the scores: near-exclusive X receivers such as [[DK Metcalf]] and [[Rashad Bateman]] almost never clear 80% vs zone, and he calls forecasting aging receivers the thing he is least confident about with the data.

- 2024-09-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-26]]): an explicit restatement of how the benchmarks should be read. The 70% success rate vs man and 80% vs zone thresholds are 'reserved for conversations regarding good players' — legitimately good NFL players — not a bar for merely belonging in the league, and clearing them is where the show starts being interested, not where a player is validated. Harmon stresses the inverse too: hitting near 70% vs man does not make you [[Chris Olave]] or the next [[Stefon Diggs]]. He applies an 80%-plus vs zone requirement specifically to inside receivers, and notes that a high-volume target earner like [[Rashee Rice]] needs to clear it. He also concedes charting limits by acknowledging NextGenStats measures something different from what he tracks when [[Brandon Aiyuk]]'s press numbers contradicted his percentile profile.
- 2024-10-01 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-01]]): route success rates are the most important RP metrics but not the whole evaluation — hands, contested catching and yards-after-catch ability also decide whether a charting profile translates, which is why [[Diontae Johnson]] and [[Garrett Wilson]] can post near-elite success rates and still leave plays on the field. He restated the working benchmarks for a startable outside receiver: over 70% success vs man coverage, over 80% vs zone. He also conceded a limit to the isolation premise: while RP exists to separate receiver play from surrounding variables, putting the season into the box score matters too, and a receiver funneled targets in a still-broken offense (his example: [[Rashee Rice]]) should raise an eyebrow rather than earn alpha status. Four games, he stressed, is not enough to move anyone into the elite tier.
- 2024-10-03 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-03]]): two methodological points. First, on interpreting success rates — Harmon stresses that separation is only one part of playing receiver ([[Garrett Wilson]] can be over 80% vs man, zone and press and still be sloppy at the catch point), and that a quarterback watching from field level is not playing Madden with the All-22 view, so an open charting rep is not automatically a missed throw. Koh adds that the human element, effort on every play, moves these numbers when the offense and team situation improve. Second, on how to use a profile publicly: Harmon's lesson to content creators from the [[Jameson Williams]] work is that he opened the offseason saying 'I don't have a strong take on this' and spent the time unpacking the profile into ranges of outcomes, rather than posting a bad route chart or a 15th-percentile success rate as a hot take and then rooting for the player to fail.

- 2025-01-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-09]]): for this awards episode Harmon states an explicit methodological disclaimer — he has not charted every player named, has done film and some charting plus a lot of data work, and is leaning on Next Gen Stats, Fantasy Points and prior-season RP profiles. [[Reception Perception]] counts results — how successfully a player runs each route type — and observes the process around them, so a receiver asked to run few alpha-type downfield routes yields a small sample and reads as flashes. The RSP instead grades the techniques underneath those routes and asks whether they project to succeed in the NFL, so for Waldman a small sample can suffice if the craft and concepts are clearly present and repeated within it. He concedes the counter: if he is wrong on Burden, the reason will be that the sample was too small.

- 2025-04-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-24]]): while [[Matt Harmon]] does great work, the modeling is built on the number of routes that produced success — did he get separation on this route — so there is process in it but the output is mostly result-driven. Waldman's stated complement is film that examines the process behind the result: a prospect can separate because he knows some footwork and hand counters and is bigger or faster than his opposition, without the craft that projects to the NFL. He described watching tape privately with Dave Kluge, isolating a receiver executing a two-quick/double-up into an arm-over, then replaying the same move by Chad Johnson or [[Davante Adams]] — 'night and day different'. The standard he wants scored is whether a move is patient, sudden, and executed at pro tempo rather than at one speed. He applied this directly to [[Tetairoa McMillan]], whom he ranks WR5 against a consensus of WR1-2.
- 2025-04-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-04-28]]): Harmon evaluates every landing spot in this episode by matching a player's charted route chart and coverage splits to the alignment his new team will actually give him, rather than to a positional label. Worked examples: [[Emeka Egbuka]]'s outward-breaking routes (78.6% outs, 82.6% flats) mapping onto [[Baker Mayfield]]'s documented preference for out routes; [[Matthew Golden]]'s route chart running 'like a clock' from out–corner–nine–post–dig into Matt LaFleur's McVay-derived in-breakers; [[Jayden Higgins]]' poor man (15th percentile) and press (16th percentile) marks being an artifact of Iowa State making him play outside on the line, with 78.6% versus zone the more relevant number in Houston. - 2025-04-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-29]]): on mini samples, a three-game chart such as [[Pat Bryant]]'s is meant to be 'directionally accurate' and get you in the ballpark of who a player is, not to settle the evaluation — a longer sample sometimes moves the numbers up and sometimes down. He also stresses reading the route participation chart alongside the overall success rates, since a prospect who runs his two worst routes most often (Bryant at 18.6% nine routes on 27.8% success and 19% slants on 55%) will show a depressed overall figure that misrepresents him. Harmon restates his benchmarks in the same episode: you want above 70% success rate vs man and above 80% vs zone.
- 2025-05-14 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-14]]): Harmon restates the benchmarks for listeners new to the charting — over 70% success rate versus man coverage, over 80% versus zone, and over 75% versus press. He stresses that route participation must be read alongside success rate, because a receiver asked to run a heavy diet of nine routes from the X will post a depressed zone number by role rather than by deficiency ([[Rome Odunze]] and [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] are the examples; Thomas ran nine, post or corner on 23% of routes). He also notes the value of comparing the in-season rookie report to the full-year profile to detect improvement (Odunze, Nabers' curl route, Thomas' zone number), and that the site now carries quarterback charting — route-type throw distribution — with a quarterback/receiver matching score in development.
- 2025-05-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-16]]): two methodological notes from this episode. First, on route-tree color grading — a single route flips a color, since one more successful slant would have made [[Ladd McConkey]]'s slant a yellow route and two more a green one, so Harmon refuses to haggle over one or two routes and reads the tree in aggregate. Second, on in-season versus full-season samples: the November rookie report charts roughly the first four games and the full profile adds the last four, and comparing them exposed [[Ricky Pearsall]]'s jump from 57.1% to 75% vs press. Harmon flags that leaning on a final-four-game split is 'sometimes dangerous,' but cites [[Jameson Williams]] as a case where it proved instructive because he went on to play like the late-season version. He also notes small prospect samples distort baselines — McConkey was charted on only 133 routes and faced press on 18 of them, producing a 44% press mark that his rookie 84.4% blew away.
- 2025-05-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-21]]): the episode makes the charting benchmarks explicit — 80% success rate vs zone is the line Harmon looks for (he credits both [[Keon Coleman]] as a prospect and [[Xavier Worthy]]'s finished rookie season with clearing or nearing it), while percentile context matters more than the raw number, as with Worthy's 60.2% vs man landing at only the 20th percentile and 53.8% vs press at the 16th. Harmon also stresses reading route participation against route success together rather than judging by the colours of the chart alone: Coleman's tree is 70% three routes, Worthy's usage largely overlaps the routes he is good at except that his out and dig routes are inverse, and [[Jalen Coker]]'s strengths are the slant, out and dig. He notes in-season rookie reports get revised by the full-season sample — Worthy improved substantially — and repeats that the podcast covers only 80-90% of profiles, with the charts themselves on the site and longer visual breakdowns on YouTube.
- 2025-05-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-23]]): Harmon restates the interpretive rules behind the charting. Route-level success rate benchmarks are explicit — roughly 50% on the nine route is the functional line (41% is 'non-functional'), posts want 70-75%, and slants should be near 80%. He warns repeatedly against reading a shared route chart image in isolation: 'you can't just take one number and be like see, boom' — the full context of who the player is, how he is aligned and how balanced his route tree is has to come with it, which is why the sortable tables live on the site rather than in a single graphic. He also defends the 15-minutes-per-player charting approach against industry advice to the contrary, citing over a million downloads. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) notes upcoming quarterback profiles from Corey Kinnan and that Harmon is currently charting [[Puka Nacua]]'s second season and [[Rashee Rice]].
- 2025-05-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-30]]): Harmon explains that his dynasty rankings only really move when he charts, so the calendar drives the board — the May 16 update followed year-two charting and the year-three drop lands June 9, with [[Puka Nacua]], [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]], [[Jordan Addison]] and [[Cedric Tillman]] all pending. He also raises a live methodological tension: NFL formations have condensed so much — the Rams had the league's lowest average formation width last season — that the slot/outside distinction is blurring, so a receiver can align outside and run a slot route tree, as [[Puka Nacua]] did as a rookie. Harmon says he will not change ten years of alignment charting over it, because the fix is reading the full profile in context rather than one number.
- 2025-06-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-11]]): Harmon lays out several working standards. On sample: an NFL player gets charted on his full sample once he has at least eight games — no partial roundups — with the only exception being an unavoidably short season like [[Rashee Rice]]'s ~3.1 games, which he says distorts the numbers. On labels: he has deliberately raised the bar for the word 'elite', arguing the eighth-best receiver in the league probably is not elite, and would rather be accurate later than 'fake early' with a call. Benchmark rates cited in this episode: over 70% vs man is good, 75% is 'cooking with gas', 80%-plus vs press is the elite-outside-receiver threshold, and above 80% is where a slot receiver's slant success rate should sit; he also flags that flat-route success is expected above 85%, over James Koh's (untracked co-host) habit of discounting the route entirely. Harmon separately notes his own alignment splits are strained by condensed formations.
- 2025-06-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-13]]): Harmon described his own charting discipline — he deliberately does not look back at a player's prior-season success rates until he has finished charting the current season, so the new numbers are not anchored to the old ones. That is how he was 'stunned' to find [[Jayden Reed]]'s year-one and year-two scores within a percentage point of each other. He also framed what the paid profiles add over the raw charts: context, what his eyes saw, and the explicit downside range for the player, which he contrasts with a screenshot of a chart circulating on social media with no interpretation attached. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) added that roughly 90% of the site's numbers can be pieced together free from social posts and podcasts, but the written profiles cannot.
- 2025-07-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-09]]): Koh restates the benchmark thresholds for a receiver to be considered good — 70% success rate versus man coverage, 80% versus zone, 75% versus press — while conceding those bars are somewhat biased upward because the charted sample skews to good players. Harmon adds two structural caveats: X receivers will always post lower zone numbers by the nature of running a perimeter vertical tree, and contested catch rates are fluid and less stable year to year than the man, press and zone success rates. - 2025-07-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-18]]): the episode shows how route participation and success rate are read together rather than separately. Harmon repeatedly discounts headline success rates that ride on a narrow route diet — [[Rashee Rice]]'s 89.8% vs zone is 99th percentile and fifth all-time, but comes from a three-game sample in which 40.6% of routes were slants or screens and only 4.4% were go routes. Conversely he treats high success rate at high participation on a demanding route as the strongest signal available, citing the list of receivers over 80% success on the dig at 20%-plus dig participation: [[Davante Adams]], [[Brandon Aiyuk]], [[Julio Jones]], [[Michael Pittman Jr.]], [[Ladd McConkey]], [[Nico Collins]], [[Justin Jefferson]], [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] and [[Chris Olave]]. - 2025-07-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-30]]): Reception Perception added running back charting for the first time, covering what Harmon considers the top 12 backs entering the NFL. It ports the receiver methodology across — splitting man/gap versus zone scheme and inside versus outside running, so a back who wins on both blocking schemes but not between the tackles is identifiable, plus explosive rates, run-stuff rates and, unusually, a heavy focus on pass protection effort, because Harmon argues blitz pickup rather than receiving is what actually keeps a back on the field.
