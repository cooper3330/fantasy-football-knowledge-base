## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-09-26 — According to [[Chris Harris]] and guest Cousin Josh (not a tracked expert) ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-26]]): the whole trade-target exercise is built on windows that close with one good game. Josh repeatedly frames it as act-now — if [[TreVeyon Henderson]] posts a 14-65 line, if [[Ashton Jeanty]] goes 21-110 with a score against the Bears, if [[Breece Hall]] explodes on Monday night in front of everyone, you are not getting them. Harris's version is to find the pressure point in the other manager: read the market's overreaction (Fantasy Pros ranks moving on one bad week), talk up the bad matchup that just happened and the hard one coming, and buy only if it is cheap. He argues the single largest edge is identifying the biggest potential panic sell — his pick is [[Brian Thomas Jr.]], with Josh adding [[Ladd McConkey]] and [[Drake London]] — while conceding the panic sellers might turn out to be right. Both stress they are shopping with couch-cushion money, not paying full freight.
- 2025-09-29 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-29]]) [Dynasty]: the best-case usage window is itself the sell signal. With [[Najee Harris]] hurt and the Chargers having no alternative, [[Omarion Hampton]] is getting the maximal version of his role, so Waldman calls him a dynasty sell-high as production accumulates over the following weeks — the point being to trade against a role the team was not designing on purpose. The inverse case in the same episode is [[Ladd McConkey]], where Waldman says the panic is warranted rather than a buy-low, because the cause is a permanent change in the target hierarchy ([[Quentin Johnston]], [[Keenan Allen]], [[Oronde Gadsden II]]) rather than a temporary dip in play.
- 2025-09-29 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-29]]): a week of live examples of how narrow these windows are. Harris had recommended trading for [[Ashton Jeanty]] on Friday's show and says that after two touchdowns it is now very much too late — the window shut in 48 hours. On [[George Pickens]] he flags the reverse: one huge game is not enough for him to change his full opinion, so Pickens becomes a sell candidate precisely because the averages now flatter him. Guest Jamie Eisenberg of CBS (NOT a tracked expert) adds a pricing discipline: he would absolutely buy [[Chris Godwin]] and [[Brian Thomas Jr.]], but what matters is the level of buying low — he would not send [[Emeka Egbuka]] for Thomas, and cites an 0-3 manager who traded [[CeeDee Lamb]] for Thomas as the kind of desperation deal to weigh carefully. Harris says he hates selling for quarters on the dollar, but a month in, patience is running out.
- 2025-10-03 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-10-03]]): Harris articulates the recency mechanism directly — 'our opinion changes so much with the last thing we've seen.' The worked example is the Bengals after a bad Monday Night Football showing: everybody now calls them the worst offense in the league, but they had played Minnesota and Denver, both on the road, in genuinely bad spots. His conclusion is not that the market is wrong about direction, only about magnitude: 'I don't think it's as bad as this, but I don't think it's as good' either — so you should be buying, at a price well below what the panic-seller thinks he's owed. The corollary he applies elsewhere in the episode is symmetric: sell into the big televised game ([[George Pickens]]) and buy into the ugly one ([[Chase Brown]], [[Tee Higgins]]), ideally acquiring a player you don't have to start immediately.
- 2025-10-10 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-10-10]]): the inverse exercise — hot starts you shouldn't sell and cold starts you shouldn't buy — is framed by a check on last year's version of the show, where the one mutual name was [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] as the hot start to ride, and riding him was right. This year Harris's test for a cold buy is whether the environment has been diagnosed yet: by Week 6 'we're getting our arms wrapped around what these teams are,' so a confirmed-bad team ([[Tony Pollard]]'s Titans) or a broken offensive line ([[Ladd McConkey]]'s Chargers, [[Tee Higgins]]'s Bengals) makes the discount a trap rather than an opportunity. He separates that from single-week game-flow reasoning, which he refuses: 'if your main reason for an opinion is that your assessment of the game flow is a certain thing, you have not been paying close enough attention to how the NFL works,' but season-long team quality is fair evidence. Guest Cousin Josh adds the seller's version — sell into a narrative bump (Flacco to Cincinnati) that informed listeners won't be fooled by.
