# Condensed Formations and Blurred Slot-Outside Lines

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-06-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-11]]): Harmon argues condensed formations will be one of the NFL's talking points in 2025 and that they are eroding the slot-vs-outside distinction fantasy analysis still leans on. Citing Next Gen Stats formation width, he lists the teams at roughly 22 yards or fewer as the Rams, 49ers, Lions, Texans (Bobby Slowik), Vikings (Kevin O'Connell), Packers (Matt LaFleur), Falcons (Zac Robinson), Buccaneers (Liam Coen) and Dolphins (Mike McDaniel) — all off the Shanahan/McVay tree — with the Saints in that ballpark early in 2024 before injuries. He notes the tree is spreading beyond Shanahan and McVay as Ben Johnson's staff proliferates. The practical effect: a receiver on the line but tight to the formation can chip in the run game and release to the flat like [[Cooper Kupp]], while the Z stays free of press and sees softer zone or off-man, so a nominally outside role can still be run slot-esque. Harmon says this makes his own slot/outside, on-line/off-line charting splits a little uneasy, and that the community's obsession with who is 'the slot' is archaic — it matters less for a player like [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]], with Seattle and the Ben Johnson Bears as the clearest examples.
- 2025-07-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-09]]): Harmon contrasts Detroit, one of the three most condensed formation offenses in the NFL and heavy on pre-snap shifts and eye candy, with the 2024 Jets, one of the five most spread-out and without any of that motion. He argues the alignment philosophy, not just the quarterback, dictates a receiver's route tree — Detroit's structure suppressed go routes even for a burner like [[Jameson Williams]], while the Jets' spread, on-the-line usage locked [[Garrett Wilson]] into nines and slants and cut his dig rate by ten percentage points. Koh adds that the big in-breakers popularized by the Rams and Shanahan's 49ers are what he expects new OC Tanner Engstrand to import, and that a fluid receiver should be moved rather than anchored at X.
- 2025-07-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-18]]): Klint Kubiak's 2024 New Orleans offense ran heavy 12-personnel condensed sets with effectively only two receivers on the field, which forced [[Chris Olave]] into a career-high 79.1% outside snap rate and 68.3% on the line of scrimmage after he was under 36% on the line in each of his first two seasons. Harmon treats holding separation rates steady through that kind of alignment shift — 76.3% vs man, identical 77.1% vs press across two very different roles — as strong evidence of a full-field separator rather than a scheme-dependent one, and expects Kellen Moore to swing the alignment back toward the slot, citing [[DeVonta Smith]] at 47.5% slot snaps posting his most efficient season.
- 2025-07-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-25]]): Harmon rejects the slot-only label for [[Jack Bech]] on charting grounds — 73.6 percent of sampled snaps outside and 73.2 percent on the line of scrimmage at TCU with a 91st-percentile press success rate — and cites [[Jakobi Meyers]] at just 33 percent slot usage as proof of concept that these players move. He argues the right deployment is condensed formations where a player wins against man and zone and then moves around the formation, the way [[Puka Nacua]] is used, and notes Chip Kelly used Jordan Matthews as a big slot in Philadelphia. James Koh (not tracked) adds that Las Vegas is already talking about heavier 12 personnel with [[Brock Bowers]] and [[Michael Mayer]] under Pete Carroll, which points to condensed looks. Harmon separately notes no true number one receiver — including [[Ladd McConkey]] — lives on the boundary full time.
- 2025-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-06]]): Harmon stresses that Miami's offense is very condensed, so conventional receiver roles get blurry and alignment labels mislead — [[Tyreek Hill]] takes slot and backfield reps, stacks and bunches off the line and full-speed motion to the snap, while [[Jaylen Waddle]] carries the on-line dirty work despite being a player you want to get the ball in space and give layups to. He expects the same blurring to help [[Garrett Wilson]] in New York, where a condensed offense with slot reps should be a marked improvement on the slants-outs-and-gos tree he ran under [[Aaron Rodgers]].
- 2025-09-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-09]]): the Jets' Week 1 offense under new OC Tanner Engstrand is a clean example of what modern passing games do and the antithesis of what New York ran the previous two years — pre-snap eye candy, motion at the snap, receivers aligned tight to the line with space created to the outside. Harmon described one play where a boundary tight end motions all the way across, a second receiver goes in full motion at the snap, and [[Garrett Wilson]] cycles from slot to outside back to slot before running a quick in-breaker to beat man coverage. He said there were essentially zero plays like that in the 2024 Jets offense. The same structure helps [[Justin Fields]] by producing large outside windows rather than static go and out routes on the boundary, and the Chargers used a related idea in Buffalo's mold by stacking receivers for free releases.
- 2025-09-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-11]]): Harmon argues alignment charting is getting wonky because pre-snap motion and motion at the snap blur what is and is not a slot receiver, and he is curious how other sources account for it — his own [[Rome Odunze]] numbers (70.6% outside) diverged from Next Gen Stats, and he says the more meaningful split is snaps on versus off the line of scrimmage. He adds that no good coaching staff lines its top dog up as an X-only player anymore, citing Ben Johnson moving [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] everywhere and Chicago giving slot reps to [[D.J. Moore]], Olamide Zaccheaus and Odunze alike. Same logic in Tampa Bay with [[Emeka Egbuka]] and the [[Chris Godwin]] 57.5% slot rate, and he contrasts modern slots with pure-slot archetypes like Cole Beasley, [[Josh Downs]] or [[Khalil Shakir]] who cannot be left on the field in two-receiver sets.
- 2025-09-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-18]]): personnel packages are driving 2025's alignment shifts more than player development. Cleveland's 12 personnel rate went from 16.5% (below the 25% league average) to 50%, third in the NFL, pushing [[Jerry Jeudy]] to 81% outside; Pittsburgh ([[Jonnu Smith]], [[Pat Freiermuth]]) and Atlanta are also top-three, with Atlanta leading the league in order to get [[Kyle Pitts]] into real routes with a blocking tight end beside him. The Rams' heavy 12 personnel is what lets them run [[Puka Nacua]] from the slot on 65% of snaps behind [[Davante Adams]] at X. The outlier is Miami: the only team in the NFL with zero 12-personnel snaps this season, down from 20% last year, running 10 personnel on 39% of snaps when 30 of 32 teams use it under 1% — which Harmon and Koh call insane and a symptom of poor roster building.
- 2025-09-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-23]]): Tampa Bay is one of the best teams in the league at blurring the line between what is and is not a slot receiver, and has kept those bones from the Liam Coen passing game. The practical consequence is a roster stacked with interchangeable slot-profile bodies — [[Emeka Egbuka]], [[Sterling Shepard]] and [[Tez Johnson]], the last of whom Harmon noted is around 150 pounds — which is how the Buccaneers absorbed the loss of [[Mike Evans]], [[Chris Godwin]] and [[Jalen McMillan]] without the passing game collapsing. Harmon contrasted this with the Giants, where failing to condense [[Malik Nabers]] to the line of scrimmage left him easy to double.
- 2025-11-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-11-06]]): parts of the fantasy community and NFL analysis remain 'a little archaic in terms of slots and outside receivers,' because teams condense formations and weaponize a big slot and a tight end on the field simultaneously — the roles are not mutually exclusive. His examples: Detroit mixing and matching where [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] and [[Sam LaPorta]] lined up early in LaPorta's career; Las Vegas being able to field [[Jack Bech]] alongside [[Brock Bowers]], who aligned out wide on 27% of snaps, slot on 40% and rarely in line; and New Orleans, where five non-running backs all run routes with slot rates bunched between 38% and 43% because motion blurs alignment entirely. Harmon warns the Saints may become more static without [[Rashid Shaheed]]. He also uses the framework in reverse for Jacksonville, where the motion-heavy F role carries a real learning curve — the reason Liam Coen puts [[Jakobi Meyers]] outside rather than inside.
- 2025-11-06 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-11-06]]): Harris argues that fans and analysts who do not watch the field care far too much about who is nominally 'in the slot' — that is not the NFL anymore, because the slot receiver motions out to flanker and back, and teams move players around to suit where they want the route run. His applied example is [[Jakobi Meyers]] in Jacksonville, where the reported plan is to play him outside despite his slot reputation: Harris is 'perfectly fine with him lining up wide and running a slant,' since Meyers's edge is winning in the middle of the field and in short areas, not an alignment. Guest Ben Gretch (Stealing Signals, not tracked) makes the related point that [[Trevor Lawrence]] has historically fed slot-type targets — Jamal Agnew, [[Christian Kirk]], and [[Evan Engram]] as 'basically just a big slot receiver' — while struggling to get the ball to true outside men.
