# Power Slot Receiver Archetype

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-03-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-26]]): the power slot is a receiver with outside-receiver size and strength deployed mostly from the slot, and Harmon rejects the industry habit of labelling the type low-upside. His argument: most players will never be number-one receivers, and size-speed freaks who cannot run routes or catch reliably will not be either, so a reliable power slot who elevates any passing attack is worth a first-round pick in a league where most teams need a WR2. He traces the type from [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] — whom he called a 'Bud Light [[Cooper Kupp]]' as a prospect and who started as a great zone beater and average man beater before improving every year — to [[Emeka Egbuka]], who already played the power slot role in college so requires no projection. Caveat: production depends heavily on landing in an offense and with a quarterback that feeds middle-of-field zone work, as [[Jared Goff]] and Detroit do.
- 2025-04-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-04]]): the recurring fix for big receivers who cannot consistently beat man or press is to stop asking them to be the isolated boundary X and instead make them oversized slots, flankers, or condensed/bunch receivers off the line of scrimmage. He applies it to [[Jayden Higgins]] and [[Keon Coleman]] — 'some big ass slot receivers' — and to [[Jonathan Mingo]], whose Carolina usage as a full-time outside receiver behind [[Adam Thielen]] in the slot he blames for the bust. His templates are [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] in Indianapolis and [[Mack Hollins]]' role in Buffalo. Koh notes in-breaking routes blur the slot/outside line anyway. Harmon's stated preference: get the receiver away from press, put him at flanker, and let him work slants, digs and crossers with a runway.
- 2025-04-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-11]]): the power-slot profile is defined by short and intermediate separation, physicality at the catch point and after the catch rather than speed — [[Jack Bech]] (6'1', 214) is the case study, separating explosively at break points, beating press at a 91st-percentile rate and going down on first contact on just 15% of in-space attempts, while failing badly on nine routes at 41%. Harmon says the right role is big slot who can also play flanker and survive in two-receiver sets, comping the type to [[Keenan Allen]] and [[Jakobi Meyers]], with the added blocking and physicality Bech brings. James Koh (not a tracked expert) pushes further, arguing this archetype can line up at X on dig and curl concepts and be a quarterback's best friend anywhere in the formation.
- 2025-04-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-18]]): the NFL has moved away from what he calls the 'pop gun slot receiver' — the small, quick, non-blocking underneath slot — because teams now emphasize and weaponize other slot types: the vertical slot ([[Jaylin Noel]] in this class) and the power slot ([[Emeka Egbuka]], possibly [[Jack Bech]], and [[Jayden Higgins]] if he develops that way). He draws the parallel to straight-line X receivers falling out of the league's pecking order for the same reason. The practical consequence for a pop-gun type like [[Xavier Restrepo]] is that there is almost no landing spot: it requires a pure spread-and-shred outfit that does not care whether its slot can block or is undersized, and Harmon struggles to name one — the Giants already have [[Wan'Dale Robinson]], maybe Cincinnati, maybe Houston under Nick Caley.
- 2025-04-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-04-28]]): Harmon sharpens the archetype by policing its edges. [[Travis Hunter]] and [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] can take slot reps but neither is a power slot, because neither will get in there and fit the run like [[Chris Godwin]]. Godwin himself, he insists, is slot-mostly and not slot-only (75.2% success rate versus man last year), and the same qualification applies to [[Emeka Egbuka]], who took 73.6% of his snaps from the slot and 81% off the line but is a slot-mostly player. The clearest projection is [[Jayden Higgins]], whom Harmon expects to be a true power slot in Houston under new OC Nick Caley, whose Rams and Patriots background carries a history of using one — a role that also uses Higgins' size as a blocker near the line of scrimmage.
- 2025-04-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-29]]): Chip Kelly has a repeatable preference for big-bodied slot receivers, running from Jordan Matthews — a 6-3 pure slot Kelly drafted in Philadelphia in 2014, now a tight end — through [[Emeka Egbuka]] at Ohio State, and Harmon expects [[Jack Bech]] (6-1, 214) to be weaponized as an interior player in Las Vegas even though Bech lined up outside in college and beat press at a 79.4% success rate. Harmon's point is that the power slot is not a slot-only limitation: Bech and [[Jakobi Meyers]], also not small and a repeat 70%-plus man-coverage beater, can coexist because one wins with separation outside and the other adds after-catch pop inside.
- 2025-05-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-21]]): Harmon says the receivers who share [[Keon Coleman]]'s prospect profile — below roughly the 30th-35th percentile in success rate vs man and press — and still became high-volume, consistent NFL starters all did it by converting to big slot, which is why he floats that path for Coleman while calling it only a theory with no guarantee. The archetype he actually endorses is the big slot who is not merely a zone-beating specialist: [[Jalen Coker]] at 6-1, 208 with a 42.5-inch vertical, 98th percentile explosion and 70%-plus success rates against both man and press is the model, and Harmon says he is always hunting that type. He notes [[Rashee Rice]] and [[JuJu Smith-Schuster]] as established big slots in Kansas City and [[Jalen Royals]] as a comparable addition, and cautions that in Buffalo the big-slot lane is already occupied by [[Khalil Shakir]], one of the league's best in that role — so Coleman's realistic path is off-the-line usage in condensed formations rather than displacing him.
- 2025-07-18 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-18]]): the requirements for a high-volume slot receiver are explicit — you do not have to beat man coverage, but you must be an elite zone beater with great hands and be good after the catch. He anchors the archetype with a man-coverage band around 63%: 2021 [[Hunter Renfrow]] at 63.7%, 2022 [[Christian Kirk]] at 63%, 2023 [[Rashee Rice]] at 63%, 2014 Jarvis Landry at 62.6% and 2016 Julian Edelman at 64%, with [[Khalil Shakir]] landing in the same range. The ceiling question is whether such a player makes the [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] or [[Cooper Kupp]] leap into a real press-man beater; Harmon puts that at under 50% for Shakir and says until Rice does it, you cannot run an offense through him. He also notes zone success rates for these players are partly inflated by heavy slant/screen route diets.
- 2025-07-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-25]]): Harmon uses the power-slot label to argue that a receiver's bad marks on certain routes can be irrelevant if the deployment never asks for them. [[Pat Bryant]] runs a 4.6 with a 27.8 percent nine-route success rate and a 55 percent slant rate — the worst Harmon has charted — yet Harmon expects him to work in Denver because Sean Payton will use him on digs and curls, where he charts at 75 percent and above, and because he settles down and presents a reliable target with good hands and real after-catch value. He extends the idea to [[Quentin Johnston]] as potentially the biggest big slot in the league. The counterpoint Harmon and James Koh (not tracked) both raise is that these are still giant red flags on the two routes a prospect ran most, so the archetype call is a bet on the coaching staff.
- 2025-08-08 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-08]]): Harmon uses the power-slot label twice in this episode, and both times as a role that only exists if the roster leaves it open. [[Luke McCaffrey]] shows power-slot indicators in the RP in-season rookie report — working digs, corners, outs and posts, the deeper intermediate breakers — and could emerge as a big-slot weapon in a thin Washington room. [[Jonathan Mingo]]'s only viable NFL path was the same power-slot/gadget role, but Harmon says Dallas cannot offer it with [[CeeDee Lamb]] on the roster, which is why he found the trade for Mingo peculiar. The lesson he draws is that archetype fit is contingent on incumbent talent, not just on the player's own skill set.
- 2025-08-15 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-15]]): Harmon uses [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] as the archetype's escape hatch — a player who entered as a pure zone-beating power slot (60.3% vs man, 80.4% vs zone as a rookie) and incrementally built a man-coverage game to 72.4% and a 78.6% press success rate, while shifting from 42% outside alignment in 2022 to 51% in 2024. The point is that the power slot label is a starting condition, not a ceiling: Harmon explicitly separates St. Brown from [[Rashee Rice]], [[JuJu Smith-Schuster]] and [[Deebo Samuel]], saying he is more than a slot merchant, though still not the kind of X who wins over the top on a go route. He pairs this with the argument that the alignment discourse should credit power slots without discounting X receivers.
- 2025-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-29]]): Harmon defended the archetype against the mockery that it is just 'stick the big guy in the slot against zone coverage' — noting Kansas City did exactly that with [[Rashee Rice]] and it worked — and drew a firm boundary he says the industry keeps blurring: a move tight end is NOT a slot receiver. Told [[Evan Engram]] would occupy Denver's power slot role, he insisted Engram is a move tight end and that the two roles coexist, citing the 2023 Lions with [[Sam LaPorta]] and [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] operating in concert. Applications this episode: [[Pat Bryant]] as Sean Payton's big slot, [[Xavier Hutchinson]] as a dirty-work power slot comped to [[Allen Lazard]], [[Devaughn Vele]] as a passable big slot, and [[Jalen Coker]] as a slot-mostly, [[Chris Godwin]]-shaped player.
