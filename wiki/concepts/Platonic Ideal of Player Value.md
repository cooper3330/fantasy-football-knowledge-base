## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-10-23 — According to Adam Harstad (not a tracked expert) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-23]]): Harstad proposes a Platonic framework for dynasty 'value' -- borrowing Plato's theory of forms, he argues there is an unreachable ideal 'capital-V' Value (how much a player truly helps you achieve what you care about, mostly winning, under perfect information) that real-world tools like KeepTradeCut, FantasyCalc, and Dan Hindery's Footballguys trade charts can only vaguely approximate. He singles out Hindery's charts as his favorite because they are fully customizable to league settings (roster requirements, scoring, number of teams), unlike KTC/FantasyCalc. Under the true capital-V ideal, a big game should actually decrease a player's remaining value, since points already scored are production 'gone' and unrecoverable -- the opposite of how markets actually react -- but Waldman and Harstad agree the practical, market-facing 'lowercase-v' value (what a player will fetch in a trade, as applied to [[Patrick Mahomes]]) is what matters for real decisions. They also stress value is inherently league-specific: it shifts with scoring format and starting requirements (e.g., quarterbacks are worth more in superflex than one-QB leagues) and even with league-mates' tendencies, such as how aggressively they hoard backup quarterbacks.
- 2025-10-31 — According to Adam Harstad (untracked Film and Theory co-host, [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-31]]): extending the platonic-idealism table-setter from the prior episode, Harstad distinguished lowercase-v value (the real-world approximations we actually use -- Dan Hendry's Football Guys charts, FantasyCalc's crowd-trade data, KeepTradeCut's Elo-style rankings) from capital-V platonic ideal value, which would require perfect foreknowledge of every player's remaining career output plus soft factors like individual league-mates' trade tendencies or team-fandom bias. Under the platonic ideal, a player's value should peak the moment he first produces and decline steadily with every subsequent game, since each game consumes one more unit of finite remaining production; the bigger the game, the bigger the decline. In the real world, value instead often rises after a breakout because the market is learning new information about a player's ceiling (e.g. [[Oronde Gadsden II]]) -- except for players already fully scouted, where value should decline per the model but instead holds flat, creating an exploitable inefficiency (see [[Settled Player Opinion and Flat Dynasty Value]]).
