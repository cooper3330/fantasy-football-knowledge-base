## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31 (Tapping Out, Archetypes and Modeling)]]): co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) called historical comps 'voodoo' that is trivially easy to prove is nonsense — invoking Magritte's 'this is not a pipe', since a representation of a thing is not the thing, and John Elway rupturing an Achilles 40 years ago cannot change what [[Anthony Richardson]] becomes today — but said he uses them heavily anyway because, carefully done, they work. The danger is degrees of freedom: the more choices an analyst has about who qualifies as a comp, the more room to editorialize rather than analyze, and if you already believe a player will be good you will subconsciously exclude the misses on pretextual grounds. He named this p-hacking or the garden of forking paths and tied it to the replication crisis. His practical rules: use objective sorting criteria, decide criteria before looking at the data, and never data-snoop for the filter that produces the names you wanted. His diagnostic: a genuine comp list has hits AND misses — a 50-60% hit rate is a good set, an 80-90% hit rate means you screwed up, because relationships that strong do not exist in the NFL. If a comp list has no trash players on it, run. Waldman agreed, conceding his own Richardson archetype list leaned on Hall of Famers and MVPs and should have included the washouts that fit the same physical mold.
- 2025-09-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-22]]): discussing Ryan Clark's Trent Richardson comparison for [[Ashton Jeanty]], Waldman relayed [[Adam Harstad]]'s (not a tracked expert) line from the previous week's Film and Theory episode that 98% of backs compared to Trent Richardson in year one go on to have great careers, with [[Jonathan Taylor]] the most recent example. The joke carries the point: the bust comp gets reached for so often, and applied so loosely, that it has no predictive content. Waldman's own objection to Clark's version was that it substituted a comp and an 'it factor' claim for any discussion of footwork, vision, decision making or pad level.
- 2025-11-13 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-11-13]]): relying on the NFL draft hype machine for prospect evaluation does not work because the coverage cannot stop itself from stacking comps — he mocks the pre-draft treatment of [[Ashton Jeanty]] as 'a combination of LaDainian Tomlinson and Emmitt Smith' as too dumb to yield truth, and argues you only get the real picture once you have seen a player play against men. Harris's own framing is that the useful comp is a testable on-field path rather than a ceiling ornament: he offers Ray Rice and D'Angelo Williams for Jeanty as outcomes that would be genuine wins, and rejects the popular Mark Ingram comp on specifics — Ingram was bigger, less talented, less fast and less elusive, but wanted to punish tacklers more than almost anyone, which is precisely the trait Jeanty needs to add.
- 2025-12-01 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-12-01 (Feel It or Fk It)]]): This episode leaned heavily on historical player comps to project current players -- Chris Bell to Dwayne Bowe or Martavis Bryant depending on unresolved long-speed questions, Chris Brazzell II to a 'more promising George Pickens,' KC Concepcion to Steve Smith II (not Steve Smith Sr.) and to a downfield Amon-Ra St. Brown, and Bob Harris's (untracked) broad Bryce Young-to-Joe Montana/Bill Walsh-system comp. Waldman flagged the underlying risk directly with George Pickens, arguing some analysts project Dez Bryant/A.J. Green outcomes onto him based on shared size/speed/length traits without him having shown the same complete route tree those players had.
- 2026-05-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-05-21]]): Harmon highlighted Reception Perception's new player-comparison tool as most useful for contextualizing disappointing rookie-year profiles like [[Elic Ayomanor]]'s, whose top comps (Preston Williams, Geronimo Allison, Marquise Lee, Kelvin Benjamin, Courtland Sutton) skew heavily toward flame-outs, with Davante Adams flagged as the rare outlier exception. He also made a deliberately bold call that [[Keon Coleman]] could follow the Adams outlier path rather than the bust path, while separately comparing [[Adam Randall]]'s size, patience and power-plus-shifty style to Le'Veon Bell -- a comp he said he essentially never makes -- as an example of using historical comps to frame a range of outcomes rather than a single prediction.
