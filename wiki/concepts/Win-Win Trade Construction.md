## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-09-12 — According to co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman]]'s show ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-12]]), summarizing his Dynasty in Theory column [Dynasty]: the old trade — 'I think player A is a star, you think player B is, let's swap' — is a dead letter. Consensus value tools (Dynasty League Football's ADP, then KeepTradeCut and FantasyCalc; he also rates Dan Hindery's Footballguys trade value charts as closest to his own process) have sharpened the market, so you must now search for *agreement*, not disagreement. His last true disagreement trade was JuJu Smith-Schuster for [[A.J. Brown]] in 2019. Two workable archetypes: (1) short-term producer for long-term asset, e.g. [[Cooper Kupp]] for [[Brian Thomas Jr.]], where both sides agree on the players but value the current season differently; (2) positional rebalancing, e.g. [[Breece Hall]] for [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] when one roster is back-heavy and the other receiver-heavy. His third, subtler lever is the roster spot: he made a trade that every calculator said he lost badly, because it freed the spot that saved him from cutting [[Tank Bigsby]] — add Bigsby to the calculator and he wins by a lot. Sourcing tip: don't hunt the team holding the player you want, hunt the team most *dissimilar* to yours. Harstad adds that not scouting helps him here — he does not fall in love with players, he loves everybody at the right price. He also cautions to trade around the consensus cost even when you are high on a player: pay WR18 prices for your personal WR10, never WR10 prices. [[Matt Waldman]] frames it as shaping the forest rather than counting trees, and says he will approach the season doing deliberate one-for-twos.
- 2025-05-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-20]]) [Dynasty]: prompted by the backlash to guest JJ Zachariason's [[Rome Odunze]]-for-1.08 trade, Waldman said most people fancy themselves negotiators and want to win at all costs, which is not how it works — he lives with a professional negotiator, and a good negotiation is a win-win most of the time, because win-wins create partnerships and more future opportunities to deal. He illustrated the adversarial alternative with a contract an outlet quietly altered after terms were agreed, which his wife told him was normal corporate practice and the negotiator's job to catch. Zachariason (not a tracked expert) agreed: a good fair trade is one where you could genuinely go either way, and that is precisely how deals get done in fantasy in the first place.
