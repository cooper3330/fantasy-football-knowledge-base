# Slot to Outside Conversion Risk

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-17]]): Harmon and co-host James Koh (not tracked) noted that the league constantly tries to move outside receivers inside, but 'we rarely see' the reverse work. Cited failures: [[Skyy Moore]] in Kansas City, 'the poster boy of don't move this guy outside,' whose confidence Harmon says is now in the toilet; [[Elijah Moore]] kicked to X by the Jets, a deployment Harmon hated at the time; and [[Jalen McMillan]] in Tampa Bay, who lost the job to [[Sterling Shepard]]. The underlying reasons are size and ball-winning — smaller slot types can separate but cannot win contested targets on the boundary — and spatial skills that do not transfer. Harmon adds a measurement caveat: teams like the Rams use condensed splits and heavy pre-snap motion, so alignment percentages can be fudged and an outside receiver like [[Puka Nacua]] can still run a slot-shaped route tree.
- 2024-11-14 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2024-11-14]]): the episode is a running argument that alignment is often assigned by prospect narrative rather than by skill, in both directions. [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] is played almost exclusively as an ISO X at 83.3% on-line usage because he was drafted as a big freaky outside receiver, when his actual strengths are in-breaking digs and slants; Harmon says the Cardinals drafted the idea of the player and typecast their fourth overall pick as [[Mike Williams]]. He would like Harrison nearer 30% slot rather than 22.8%, but concedes Arizona has no one else to play outside. Conversely, [[Keon Coleman]] is being used almost exclusively as an X when Harmon wanted him as a power slot. The counterexample is [[Ladd McConkey]], a 63.5% slot who runs curls, digs and outs above NFL-average rate rather than being propped up by screens and flats.
- 2024-11-20 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-20]]): the clearest current case study is [[Ja'Lynn Polk]], a college flanker/slot who played 35-40% of sampled snaps inside and is now outside on 84.9% of his NFL sampled snaps and on the line of scrimmage 68.2%, running nine routes 20.6% of the time. Harmon calls this the same set-up-to-fail deployment he described for [[Quentin Johnston]] and the reason [[Jonathan Mingo]] was traded midway through year two. His added wrinkle is that the failure compounds: a rookie dropping passes and losing contested reps has no standing to ask for role catering, so it becomes self-fulfilling and things 'slip away very quickly.' [[Keon Coleman]] is the mirror image — 5.5% slot, 78.4% on the line, when Harmon's RP data points toward a big-slot role Buffalo simply will not use.
- 2024-11-26 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-26]]): two Week 12 cases push in opposite directions on where a receiver's alignment should be set. Harmon dislikes Carolina moving [[Xavier Legette]] to X out of the bye, because his rookie-report work found Legette best off the line of scrimmage and moving around, and he would rather the team find a true vertical X. Conversely, co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) argues Green Bay is wasting [[Jayden Reed]] by treating him as a three-receiver-set slot only, saying there is enough evidence Reed can win off the line as a flanker to justify playing him in two-receiver sets. Harmon's counter is that alignment flexibility is only worth it if the player reliably gives you something, and Green Bay sometimes uses Reed as run-game eye candy instead.
- 2025-01-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-23]]): the New England receiver room is their case study in role miscasting destroying rookie evaluation. Waldman argues the organization is only good at picking slot receivers — [[Ja'Lynn Polk]], [[Demario Douglas]] and [[Kayshon Boutte]] are all primarily slot players who can sometimes play flanker — and the second type they collect is the speedster who does nothing else, the [[Tyquan Thornton]] mold. Angelo counts about four primary slot receivers on the roster and says not every receiver can play X, calling [[Javon Baker]] a natural Z getting garbage-time snaps at X. The consequence is that a rookie's tape gets judged in a role he was never suited to: Polk was best inside as a chain mover in 12 personnel and was instead played on the outside. Waldman notes the Patriots have not drafted a 1,000-yard receiver in a decade — Julian Edelman being the last one he can name.
- 2025-02-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-21]]): mid-season role changes can damage a rookie receiver's confidence in ways the tape then misattributes to talent. [[Xavier Legette]] performed when used off the line of scrimmage and in motion, then declined once Carolina moved him to full-time X after the bye, including four drops from week 11 on — a hands problem Harmon says did not exist in his earlier charting or in college, and which he reads as confidence eroding rather than a skill deficit. He cites Steve Smith making the same argument about [[Ja'Lynn Polk]] in New England, and the irony Smith noted of a team protecting its rookie quarterback from overload while freely overloading its rookie receiver by shuffling him between slot, flanker and X. The practical takeaway is to check whether a rookie's decline coincides with a deployment change before downgrading the player.
- 2025-03-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-07]]): the reverse conversion carries the same cost. Indianapolis moved [[Adonai Mitchell]] inside during camp purely to solve a snap-allocation problem — [[Alec Pierce]] was playing well, [[Josh Downs]] got hurt, and the staff wanted all three on the field — and Harmon says it 'just overloaded his brain'. Koh calls it unfair to ask of a young player who had never played slot at any level, high school or college, at the volume the Colts used. Harmon's evaluation is that Mitchell's movement skills mark him as a pure perimeter player and he does not want to see him in the slot again; the fix is a full offseason focused on outside X work.
- 2025-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-19]]): Harmon's stated test for whether a slot receiver can move outside is man-coverage success rate, not alignment history. He acknowledges the standard objection — beating man from the slot is not the same as beating it outside — but holds the skills transfer, citing [[Justin Jefferson]] as a slot-only final college season who converted immediately, and applying the same logic to [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] (above 75% vs man as a prospect, 72% as a rookie, NFL-leading 4.56 yards per route run vs man from week 10 on). His summary claim: if a receiver can beat man coverage, he will be a coverage-dictating player wherever he lines up. Koh registers the dissent, doubting JSN specifically is a matchup dictator regardless of alignment.
- 2025-04-02 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-02]]): the slot-only label matters less than it used to because 'outside receiver' in modern Shanahan and McVay condensed formations does not mean what it did in 2015-16 spread 11 personnel — the widest receiver may line up close to the tackle, so out routes to the field from X look nothing like the old version. He applied this to [[Luther Burden III]] (82.9% slot) via the [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] precedent, and noted [[Justin Jefferson]] was a full-time college slot while [[Malik Nabers]] was roughly 50/50 but showed enough vs man that nobody doubted the outside projection. Harmon's standard: the conversion risk is really about whether the player has demonstrated man-coverage wins, not about where he lined up.
- 2025-05-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-23]]): this episode gives the concept both a success and a failure case. [[Jalen McMillan]] was an 83.1% slot player at Washington whom Tampa Bay moved to the perimeter (67.7% of rookie snaps), and Harmon says the move worked because McMillan's college separation came from speed against man — expressed vertically it produced 63% on nines, 82% on corners and 72.7% on posts — even though his slant and curl grades cratered to bottom-20 all-time marks. [[Ja'Lynn Polk]] is the failure case: a 35.8% slot, 47% off-the-line flanker at Washington whom New England deployed 81.3% outside and 61.8% on the line, running the nine on 22.5% of routes for a non-functional 41% success rate. Harmon's rule is that the profile has to match the alignment the team intends, and that a role change can produce mental lapses simply because the player is doing something unfamiliar. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) calls this the reason 'we're the alignment bros'.
- 2025-06-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-11]]): Harmon frames the slot-to-outside move as a question of man-coverage evidence rather than alignment history. He concedes there is a real difference between beating man in the slot and beating it on the perimeter, and that without a full season outside there is no true proof of concept — but says the routes a player already runs can be translated, so his concern about [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] moving outside is 'pretty close to zero' given three consecutive charted profiles (college, rookie, year two) at 72–75% success rate versus man plus 72% versus press. He pairs this with the condensed-formation argument: an offense can keep a converted slot's role slot-esque while listing him outside, so the label moves before the job does. The corollary runs the other way too — [[Cooper Kupp]]'s current profile, he says, now restricts him to a near slot-exclusive role.
- 2025-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-29]]): [[Isaac TeSlaa]] is a live test of the conversion — a collegiate slot whose route-tree success signals hinted at X ability, whom Detroit has deployed as a perimeter vertical player, and who so far has looked better than Harmon expected. He framed the post-draft uncertainty honestly ('what the hell is he going to be'), and the open question remains man coverage: a 66.2% success rate versus man on a small prospect sample is below the charting average even if it is not a weakness. The countercase in the same episode is [[Xavier Legette]], whose rookie-year 17th percentile success rate versus press is treated as verifiable proof that a movement receiver should not be parked outside.
- 2026-01-22 — According to Matt Harmon ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-01-22]]): discussing [[Ladd McConkey]]'s projected shift outside under new Chargers OC Mike McDaniel, Harmon said the 'he's just a slot guy' narrative is the same one wrongly applied to [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] last year -- both were more productive per route outside as rookies and have shown by Reception Perception charting that they can win against man coverage, so a move outside shouldn't be treated as a risk.
- 2026-01-29 — According to JJ Zachariason (not tracked) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-01-29]], sharing new research: the pure slot archetype is disappearing. From 2011-2025 the NFL averaged 24.5 WRs per year running 300-plus routes at a 50%-plus slot rate, 18.3 at 60%-plus, and 11.6 at 70%-plus (Wes Welker-level pure slot); in 2025 those counts fell to roughly 15, 8 and 3 respectively, a steep drop tied to more 12-personnel snaps leaving fewer slot-only roles. Of the three 70%-plus slot-rate/300-route WRs in 2025, only [[Khalil Shakir]] hit 10 PPR points per game, and barely (versus a 15-year average of 5). Zachariason says he loves Shakir as a player but is 'weaning himself off' pure slot bets like [[Josh Downs]], [[Jayden Reed]], [[Elijah Moore]] and [[Jahan Dotson]] - all technically good separators who lack the size to block in the run game or win contested targets over the middle against zone, which increasingly matters more than route-running skill alone. He frames this as a redraft/dynasty warning against paying a premium for any incoming prospect who was primarily a slot receiver in college.
