## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-11-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-11]]): consistent pressure deteriorates quarterback play for all but the very greatest, and it does so in two ways — it forces occasional bad decisions (one or two are enough to lose a game), and it creates emotional impulsivity because the quarterback ends up accounting for more than his actual job. [[C.J. Stroud]] is the clean case of a proven-good quarterback degraded by it; [[Caleb Williams]] (38 sacks in Weeks 1-10, second-most for a rookie since 1970 behind David Carr's 49 in 2002) is the ambiguous case. Waldman's framework for the ambiguous case is that the outcome distribution is wide, not determined: David Carr is one branch, but Troy Aikman, Andrew Luck, Cam Newton and [[Russell Wilson]] all took heavy early punishment and came through it, and in Aikman's case arguably better for it. He warns against short-term views, and notes the downstream fantasy cost — the Bears produced no top-20 weekly receiver from Week 6 onward.
- 2024-12-30 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-30]]): constant pressure degrades quarterback play regardless of experience — 'very rarely are you going to overcome that if it's constant' — and the effect is cumulative within a game, showing up as a third-quarter collapse. He applies it to [[C.J. Stroud]] behind a guard group that could not handle twists, games or cross blitzes; to [[Caleb Williams]], where a scheme calling five- and seven-step drops the line could not protect meant he was getting beaten up by the third quarter until the ball started coming out faster; and to [[Kirk Cousins]], whose late-game interceptions he attributes to accumulated pressure. Waldman adds that the industry's measurement of this is unreliable: sack and pressure charting is highly subjective, poorly defined across the parties doing it, and typically managed by people who have never studied how to reduce variation in a charting process.
- 2025-01-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-06]]): the evaluative use of a bad situation. Waldman's 2024 RSP framing was that the question for a quarterback landing on a broken franchise is whether he can survive the onslaught, and he says [[Caleb Williams]] passed that test — down-the-stretch production under Thomas Brown's play-calling is the evidence, and the roster finger-pointing narrative is noise. He applies the same lens to [[Trevor Lawrence]], arguing anyone judging him on results alone is missing how much worse his circumstances were than [[Geno Smith]]'s or [[Sam Darnold]]'s, and to [[Drake Maye]], who handled pressure and produced without help on a roster full of holes. Co-host Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) added the counterweight — Doug Pederson is good with quarterbacks and still could not get Lawrence there consistently — so surviving a bad situation is a floor signal, not proof of a ceiling.
- 2025-01-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-01-20]]): interior pressure is more damaging to a quarterback than edge pressure — constant pressure up the middle ruins the ability to even start a rep on a solid basis and degrades play over the course of a game and a season. He argues the draft industry and NFL teams over-prioritise tackle because the athletes are rarer there and the good-to-great gap is starker, but notes good teams still take centers in the first round, citing the Eagles as a case that worked. Houston's center-guard combination gave up a lot of interior pressure and, in his view, that is a large part of what happened to [[C.J. Stroud]] and to [[Caleb Williams]]. The corollary he applies repeatedly in this episode: for [[Drake Maye]] and [[Trevor Lawrence]] (whom he puts at 97 percent offensive line), protection matters more than the coordinator hire.
- 2025-03-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-18 (Feel It or F-It Free Agency Winners and Losers)]]): Waldman applies the pressure-tolerance filter twice in one episode. On Indianapolis, he calls [[Daniel Jones]] 'pocket deaf' and among the worst starters at feeling and managing pressure, concluding that if [[Anthony Richardson]] could not succeed behind that offensive line there is no way Jones will look better behind it — the line, not the quarterback swap, is the binding constraint. On Houston, he says there is only so much any quarterback can get away with in a bad pocket, and downgrades [[C.J. Stroud]] toward QB2 after the Laremy Tunsil trade despite Stroud moving reasonably well as a pocket passer. The general principle: judge a quarterback change against whether the pocket changed, and treat rushing ability as no substitute for pressure sense.
