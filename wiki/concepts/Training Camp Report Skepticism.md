# Training Camp Report Skepticism

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-08-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-05]]): training camp is a speculative process, not a news cycle, and reports need context before they move a ranking. Three specific filters he gave. First, timing — early camp produces highlight athletic plays that inflate everyone, while weeks three and four are when offenses test whether receivers can adjust to what quarterbacks want against complex defenses; he cited [[Aaron Rodgers]] warning the 2022 Packers receivers including [[Romeo Doubs]] that none would play if they could not adjust with him. Second, rep context — Ed Valentine reported Brian Daboll putting [[Daniel Jones]] in nothing but third-and-long against Wink Martindale's most devastating blitzes purely to see how he handled it, and staffs sometimes give first-team reps only to confirm a player cannot do something before cutting him; Waldman applied this to the [[Joe Milton III]] over [[Drake Maye]] reports. Third, teammate hype — Waldman called teammates evaluating teammates the biggest avoidable minefield in camp, noting a defensive end has almost nothing in common with a receiver evaluatively and that players get enamored with speed, size and power while rarely discussing technique or concepts, as with the NFL Top 100. Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) partly disagreed: the more minute the detail of a player's observation, the more stock he puts in it, and some players do play fantasy well — though he added that most know very little, citing being in a draft where [[Kendrick Bourne]] took Robbie Gould in round two.
- 2024-08-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-12]]): two extensions of the framework. First, preseason clip hype — analysts who review viral plays are often seeing that player for the first time and are also chasing engagement, so a highlight usually just confirms what a season or two of charted tape already showed ([[Jaylen Wright]]'s reversal of field, [[Ben Sinnott]] working the middle, [[Caleb Williams]] escaping). Preseason resembles the college game because not every top athlete is on the field at once; the jump comes in September. Second, coach-speak: Waldman argues head coaches are scheme-obsessed front-facing managers evaluating results and situations, not receiver or back process, so their scouting claims mark their blind spots — Jim Harbaugh on [[Quentin Johnston]]'s ball tracking, Green Bay's staff calling [[Marshawn Lloyd]]'s fumbling an easy fix. What preseason IS good for: roster-bubble players who look comfortable and mistake-free (he names [[Daijun Edwards]]), as injury-leverage stashes, plus utilization data of the kind Dwain McFarland (not tracked) produces, which hints at what coordinators want schematically.
- 2024-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-19]]): preseason football is closer to college football than to NFL football, and that is the whole reason to discount August production. The mechanism is specific — vanilla coverages, no disguises, little complexity, slower players and constant personnel changes mean no rapport on either side of the ball. Playing well now only means you have the system down reasonably well in an easy environment. He cites players saying in camp that it feels like the SEC again, then struggling once the real games start and admitting the next year that it was a big adjustment. Historical mirages: Travis Jervey may still lead the NFL in preseason yards per carry (~10.7) because teams pitched it outside against defenses with no game plan, and Brett Hundley led the league in preseason passer rating. Waldman also notes the box-score leader in a given preseason week is often the player who gets cut, because the reps are effectively a showcase. Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) adds that October is the new September because September is the new August, given how little starters now play.
- 2024-08-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-26]]): the preseason is 'a great time for false positives and false negatives — it's basically false reaction time,' and this episode applies that frame repeatedly. On the negative side, joint practices are scripted: coaches ask opponents to run third-and-long scenarios and put a quarterback in deliberately uncomfortable situations, or run plays a unit has struggled with to check communication — so a bad day ([[Anthony Richardson]]) tells you little, and beat writers unfamiliar with a player amplify trash talk into a narrative. On the positive side, viral clips deceive: rolling left untouched and hitting a receiver with no defender within 15 yards is not evidence of a Herbert-caliber arm ([[Drake Maye]], [[Jayden Daniels]]). Waldman warns the false positive can fool the quarterback too — rookies have told him the SEC felt harder than the preseason, right before real defenses amp up massively. His summary instruction when a back or receiver flashes: 'hold your fire, kids.'
- 2024-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-29]]): cut day exposed how little weight a 'strong camp' report carries. Harmon's central complaint is that insiders reported [[Tim Patrick]] was killing it and would be Denver's WR2, and reported Terrace Marshall was having a strong camp in Carolina, days before both were released — 'that doesn't make a lot of logical sense.' His framing is about respect for the audience rather than the reporters' access: 'can you not treat the people who follow you like they're stupid?' The corollary he draws is that teams also brief the reverse — Denver simultaneously signalled [[Troy Franklin]] and [[Marvin Mims Jr.]] were not ready — leaving a depth chart with no plausible starters, which is itself evidence the briefings are not describing reality. He applies the same discount to [[Tyquan Thornton]]'s reported camp and to [[John Metchie III]]'s, noting Houston was shopping Metchie at the same time.
- 2024-09-05 — According to co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-05]]: Koh flags that in Indianapolis every receiver is reportedly having a great camp — [[Alec Pierce]], [[Adonai Mitchell]] and [[Josh Downs]] before his injury — and that the only player he has heard nothing about is [[Michael Pittman Jr.]], the one established, paid commodity, so he does not know what to believe. [[Matt Harmon]] applies the inverse asymmetry to [[Jermaine Burton]]: he refuses to dock a rookie for preseason usage, but treats persistent negative professionalism reporting as meaningful signal because it corroborates pre-draft concerns. Harmon also cautions that a camp narrative can be engagement farming, citing Detroit's [[Jameson Williams]] clips.
- 2024-09-10 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-10]]): both hosts hammer beat coverage that produced no signal before Week 1. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) says there was not one press clipping all offseason about [[Tyler Johnson]] before he broke a ~60-yard catch and run for the Rams — 'how the hell is Tyler Johnson even on this team' — and Harmon draws the parallel to the [[Brandon Aiyuk]] doghouse story of a few years earlier, where no beat reporters flagged it in advance and everyone retroactively claimed to have been worried. Harmon's stated inference from silence cuts the other way too: the absence of camp reporting makes him think Johnson's game 'could just be kind of a one-week flash in the pan.' He then deliberately balances it, commending the Indianapolis beat writers who consistently said [[Alec Pierce]] had a good camp and would be in the game plan even while fans chased [[Adonai Mitchell]] — and were right.
- 2024-11-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-22]]): Waldman argued late preseason is the informative window for receivers and early preseason is the misleading one, because early work is one-on-ones and contested-catch highlights packaged for entertainment, while late work involves full installs, adjustments and hot routes read against a defense. His example is the Packers a couple of years earlier: [[Romeo Doubs]] dominated the highlight cycle, but he could not catch the ball in the areas a primary needs to and his routes were not there, and by the final preseason week [[Aaron Rodgers]] was telling the media the receivers had not been on the same page for three days — with veteran Sammy Watkins the exception who consequently opened the season as a starter. Untracked co-host Adam Harstad added that coaches praise all 90 men on the roster in camp, so words are worthless; watch snap counts, since jobs are on the line, listen to beat reporters on who is genuinely safe from the bubble, and treat a player mentioned unprompted by a coach or quarterback as a roster-worthy signal. Waldman's parallel tell is the language of trust — a quarterback describing route detail, as with [[Ladd McConkey]], rather than a highlight.
- 2025-04-22 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-22]]): practice cut-ups deserve the same skepticism as camp reports, and Reception Perception explicitly does not give takes on practice tape. Reacting to a two-minute clip of [[Luther Burden III]] route running that NFL teams had asked Missouri to compile and that Matt Miller circulated, Harmon said every route in the clip already appears on the game film, so scouts did not need it and neither did the public. Because the clip was posted without a stated verdict, he argued the comment section became pure confirmation of priors — bulls saw separation, bears saw false steps and unclean breaks — with a defender lined up seven yards off making the reps uninformative either way. James Koh (not tracked) agreed the footage itself was bland and that the heat came from the commentary around it, not the content.
- 2025-05-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-12]]): May is structurally the month for leaked optimism. Discussing the ESPN report that Pittsburgh believes [[Roman Wilson]] is a different player ready for a year-two leap, Waldman said he is open to the possibility but rejects the reporting itself as evidence — teams leak to reporters in May specifically to pump a young player up, build his confidence and keep him working. His rule is to wait for observable behavior instead: for a receiver, beating defenders one-on-one at the boundary on timing and deeper routes against real press man coverage, visible in scrimmages, preseason games and the early season. He also applied the same skepticism to the [[Aaron Rodgers]] schedule-release story, calling it clickbait speculation.
- 2025-05-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-21]]): discussing the good minicamp reviews for Carolina rookie Jimmy Horn Jr. — a 5-8, 174-pound small-and-fast slot Harmon has no take on because he did not chart him — co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) invokes what he calls the Paris Campbell corollary, quoting the late Chris Wesseling: small, fast receivers always rip it up in minicamp because in padless practices they are very hard to cover, and every year was supposedly Paris Campbell's year. Both hosts agree the test is whether that carries into padded training camp and the regular season, and Harmon separately dismisses [[Travis Kelce]]'s reported 25-pound weight loss as an offseason story he cannot read as clearly good or bad.
- 2025-05-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-26]]): Waldman broadens the [[Efton Chism III]] hype question into a rule for the whole spring-through-camp window — with OTAs, minicamps and training camp reports incoming, 'we're looking down a drinking straw'. A report describes one specific day of practice; it may well have looked like that on that day, and only doing it consistently over a year earns the comparison. His examples of practice legends who never converted: Braxton Miller at the Senior Bowl, a Patriots tight end who looked like Gronkowski to observers because he was facing a certain type of off coverage, and Sammie Stroughter. The mechanism he keeps returning to is coverage type — off coverage and two-way goes flatter exactly the stop-start separators who struggle against press and live speed. He applies the same discount to a coach's praise of a rookie (Zac Taylor on [[Jermaine Burton]], [[Adam Thielen]] on [[Tetairoa McMillan]]), which is often an attaboy meant to build confidence. Related caution: camp injuries like [[Sam LaPorta]]'s hamstring linger longer than expected and should inform lineup decisions.
- 2025-05-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-30]]): Harmon draws a line between OTA noise and a genuine signal. He dismisses the genre of 'nobody's covering this guy' route clips, citing a video comparing a [[Kyle Williams]] curl route to [[Ja'Marr Chase]] running the same route in full pads in an NFL game — 'it's something, but it's not football' — and stresses these are meaningless OTAs. But he accepts that repeated positive reports build what he calls the drumbeat: [[Jordan Whittington]] taking extra slot work during [[Puka Nacua]]'s excused absence is how momentum starts, and if it continues into camp and [[Matthew Stafford]] asks for more of him, that is real. Separately he treats missed offseason time as a genuine negative for rookie receivers specifically, because chemistry and route-detail reps are what get lost.
- 2025-06-02 — According to Bob Harris (co-host on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-02]], not a tracked expert): a caution on how offseason attendance is read. OTAs are the voluntary portion — players come of their own free will and cannot be compelled — yet the industry gets 'really worked up' when someone skips. Sometimes it means something, sometimes nothing, occasionally it defines a season. The mandatory minicamps are where the signal sharpens, because missing roughly three days costs a player about 100,000 dollars, so a player skipping those is making a deliberate and expensive point. [[Matt Waldman]] applies related skepticism in the same episode to offseason 'bulked up' reports on [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] and [[Jayden Daniels]], valuing the added mass mainly for contact absorption rather than as a production signal, and dismissing Larry Fitzgerald's public praise of Harrison as lip service.
- 2025-06-04 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-04]]): the whole episode is a case study in discounting OTA noise. On [[Ricky Pearsall]]'s hamstring, Harmon argues people overreacted because nothing before the pads come on really matters for evaluation, and quotes Scott Pianowski's line that you should not draft into injuries because injuries will find you anyway. On the [[Stefon Diggs]] release rumor, he and co-host James Koh (not tracked) attribute the local radio report partly to a station needing to fill four hours a day in a dead news window, and set it against national reporting that no release is planned. Harmon's rule of thumb is to note when a report is a genuine role signal — coordinator comments on alignment, actual snap distribution — versus when it is filler, and to re-evaluate only if a problem bleeds into training camp itself.
- 2025-06-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-05]]): early minicamp buzz is shorts-and-t-shirts evidence, a Braxton Miller-at-the-Senior-Bowl phenomenon — he had read the descriptions, not seen the film. His filter is that speed seduces people: four of the five buzzed receivers he listed ([[Jimmy Horn Jr.]], [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]], [[Jordan Watkins]], [[Isaiah Neyor]]) are fast or quick, and the fifth, [[Konata Mumpfield]], is the one who is not — he catches the ball and paces routes. Waldman's framing question to [[Brandon Angelo]] is which impressions are substance rather than 'he's big, he's quick, he caught some deep balls today' catching a beat reporter's eye between coffee and gossip. Waldman applies the same skepticism to narrative more broadly in the episode, calling the case against [[Quentin Johnston]] media wish-casting to hype [[Tre Harris]].
- 2025-06-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-06]]): June injury headlines get overreacted to because 'we don't have games for quite some time,' and the correct discount depends on how established the player is. He applies it two ways in one episode — [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]]'s knee cleanup barely matters because he is already a top-10 NFL receiver who doesn't need camp to work himself into anything, whereas the same news for [[Ricky Pearsall]] (prior episode) mattered more because Pearsall is still establishing himself and mastering the offense. The counterweight, raised by co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert): a reported injury that traces back months, as St. Brown's knee did to February, stops being a routine offseason item.
- 2025-06-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-09]]): a June episode built repeatedly on discounting OTA and minicamp signal. [[Dyami Brown]] draws practice raves every offseason — Mike Dempsey of SiriusXM reports him hammered with targets daily, while ESPN's Mike DiRocco (neither is a tracked expert) notes this is Brown's annual pattern, good in shorts and less good in season. [[Tetairoa McMillan]] looks best in shorts and t-shirts precisely because nobody is pressing him. [[Hunter Renfrow]] is getting rave reviews 'as often people do in their short pants'. And the [[Sam Darnold]] panic after two red-zone interceptions ignores that it is practice with a new team, new receivers and a new scheme. Waldman added a media-literacy note: rookies having their heads spin in OTAs is normal, and calling that feedback 'brutal' in a headline is an AI-flavored attention word readers should expect to see much more of.
- 2025-06-27 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-27]]): the lowest-hanging fruit for a beat writer in camp is the vertical game — no coverage snafus to untangle, lots of one-on-one work, and a deep ball generates the ooh-and-ah that gets clicks — but that is not where most of receiver play lives. The parts that matter show up in the third or fourth week of the preseason, when a receiver has to be on the same page with the quarterback against real coverage looks. He cites [[Romeo Doubs]] dominating early camp on deep routes, then fading once [[Aaron Rodgers]] made clear that young receivers who do not read a defense the same way he does will not play. Applied here to Denver hype around [[Pat Bryant]] over [[Devaughn Vele]], and paired with his other caution that 'he's bulked up, he's quicker, he knows the offense' coach-speak only sometimes works out.
- 2025-06-30 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-30]]): summer role talk should be discounted heavily. On Duce Staley saying [[Dylan Sampson]] could play the slot on third downs, Waldman said this is the time of year when every running back is going to 'play more in the slot', and that such talk almost never means a Larry Centers-style 100-target role — it usually means a rotational part, or a target once every other game. He kept his projection at roughly 11 catches and 77 yards rather than move it on a coach's quote, and co-host Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) added that Staley says a lot of motivational things to get players jumping through hoops. Bob Harris offered the receiver version of the same rule, bifurcating [[Dyami Brown]] into the May-and-June monster in shorts and shoulder pads and the far less monstrous in-season player.
- 2025-07-07 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-07]]): the July window between minicamp and camp is when fantasy players start 'looking down the drinking straw' — reacting moment by moment to single cuts made in shorts, with ADP moving on one rep. Waldman's evergreen RSP piece on how to watch training camp exists to force that step back. He applied it concretely: West Coast offensive coaches have learned that running back practice tells you far less than running back performance in games, which is why he refuses to call the Rams' RB2 job ([[Jarquez Hunter]] vs [[Blake Corum]]) before week three or four, and why Andy Reid tinkers with the Kansas City backfield all season. Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) framed it as 'don't be that guy' who moves off practice noise, and cited [[Dylan Laube]] — great in shorts, one carry and a fumble.
- 2025-07-10 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-10]]): rookie minicamp is close to worthless as signal — a day one or day two pick beating his own rookie class will look good by definition, and [[Travis Hunter]] in shorts or [[Cam Ward]] spinning it around the field are easy observations. Reps only start to matter when a player runs with the ones and twos in 11-on-11 with shells on, where you can see what he is tasked with, how he handles the playbook and how he fares against veterans. Angelo's filter is to watch players whose room has an actual vacancy, like [[Brashard Smith]]. [[Matt Waldman]] adds two recurring traps: the receiver winning vertical shots (the least-targeted part of the passing game) against cautious veteran corners — he cites [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]] and [[Isaiah Neyor]] and compares it to [[Romeo Doubs]]' rookie camp and [[Denzel Mims]] hype — and the running back who 'will play in the slot', like [[LeQuint Allen]]. Waldman also notes he upgrades his respect for analysts who give technique and concept breakdowns rather than 'he's big, he's fast, he's strong'.
- 2025-07-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-28]]): camp reports should be treated as data points, not events — his phrase is don't look at things through a drinking straw, when a player is one thing one day and not the next. He applies it to [[Keon Coleman]] missing day one then dominating day two, and says none of it should move ADP. On practice highlights generally, Waldman says the missing ingredient is high-end conceptual adjustment: you will not see receivers and quarterbacks reconciling coverage reads, and you will not see genuinely contested targets because top defenders are protecting their health. He cites Kyle Shanahan's line that you do not know what you have in a running back until live game action and extends it to receivers. On first-team reps specifically, Waldman says they matter for young players not projected to start, because the real question being answered is whether they make the active roster — a single day of first-team work usually means someone needed a breather or the staff wanted a look at a specific personnel matchup. It becomes a big deal only if a player is still taking most of the first-team reps in the final week or two of camp. He names [[Brashard Smith]] as a rookie whose first-team reps will be overread.
- 2025-08-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-04]]): the organizing principle of this episode — 'don't judge anything on a single piece of evidence.' A single look can give you a gut feeling, but you should add evidence to build a case. Practice, he argues, is one of the worst environments to judge running backs specifically: it is low contact, backs will not run over their own blockers, and rapport with new linemen is still forming. He cites Kyle Shanahan saying you never truly know what you have in a back until live action with real hitting; DeMarco Murray being written off for no burst at the Senior Bowl while Sam Gash and Ernest Byner called him a thoroughbred; a former NFL safety declaring [[Nick Chubb]] lacked burst as a rookie; and Terrell Davis and Isaiah Crowell, neither of whom would have made their rosters on camp play alone. He adds that beat writers of losing organizations tend to be overreactive, and that Ben Johnson's blunt public criticism is partly media retraining. His own method: plug each report into 'a continuum of information that is ever evolving' — historical usage, scheme, play caller, workload projection — rather than treating any single day as a verdict.
- 2025-08-07 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-07]]): most 'this guy will see more time at receiver' camp fodder is 'a bunch of hooey,' though he conceded backs really are logging slot snaps now — the error is assuming the alignment converts to targets. [[Matt Waldman]] added that running backs are the hardest group to judge in camp, quoting Kyle Shanahan's line that you do not really know what you have until preseason games, and noted receivers are nearly impossible to read because a player working with the second or third string will not be the first read and so will not get the ball. He closed with a cautionary story: as a rookie, [[Nick Chubb]] was written off publicly on Twitter by a former Giants safety watching preseason, while Waldman and Brian Baldinger were showing tape arguing the opposite.
- 2025-08-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-11]]): Waldman offers a rule for adjudicating conflicting camp reporting — weight beat writers who describe what they watched in the reps over ones reciting practice box scores ('he's 17 for 20 with two touchdowns'). - 2025-08-14 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-14]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harris runs a joint-practice review but repeatedly discounts what camp chatter implies about roles. On Jacksonville: camp had [[Tank Bigsby]] as the unquestioned lead back, then [[Travis Etienne Jr.]] took the entire first-team preseason series — 'let this just be a constant reminder, we don't know the roles, we're not going to know the roles, they're not going to tell us the roles.' - 2025-08-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-15]]), in conversation with guest Dan Hatman of the Scouting Academy (a former Giants and Eagles scout, NOT a tracked expert): camp reporting is mostly noise for structural reasons, not because reporters are lazy. Hatman: there is a survivor bias baked into camp rosters, so every one of the players across all 32 teams is there because something about them is noteworthy, and flashing is the baseline rather than the signal. If a player is not flashing, that is the signal. Practice is scripted like a scientific experiment: coordinators trade plays to test one isolated question, so a back breaking free in a tag-off period is, in Waldman's phrase, 'running through a car wash curtain'. Hatman was floored by seeing someone assign wins and losses to joint practices. Both note observers simply follow their priors, exactly as with the combine, and that redraft decision season inflates the weight given to practice because it is the freshest evidence available.
- 2025-08-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-18]]): Waldman laid out why practice reporting misleads. Even excellent beat writers watching live on deadline are tracking 22 players at once without film review, so they cannot see a back like [[Nick Chubb]] whose value is subtle decision-making, minute footwork and late-arriving power against tacklers he described as 'car wash curtains.' Veterans also deliberately test non-viable throws and reads in camp — his examples were [[Tom Brady]] and [[Patrick Mahomes]] — so camp statistics are the wrong instrument. He added a rep-count rule: consistent first-team reps for a late-round pick usually mean roster-bubble evaluation, for a mid-round pick they mean readiness to play off the bench or in specific packages, and only a sustained diet of them implies an actual starting role — applied to [[Tory Horton]]. Both hosts also warned that glowing camp reports can invert overnight, citing [[Christian McCaffrey]] in 2024 and [[Matthew Stafford]]'s newly opaque back situation, and that uncertainty creates a vacuum that pulls in the worst possible outcomes.

- 2025-08-22 — According to guest Dr. Deepak Chona of SportsMedAnalytics (not a tracked expert) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-22]]: team injury messaging outside a game week is close to information-free, because clubs are not required to report and, as the 49ers demonstrated last year, it is not to their advantage to tell the truth. His workaround is to treat return-to-practice reports as the objective and systematic signal, since they are more reliable than anything said in August — hence watching [[De'Von Achane]]'s practice status rather than Mike McDaniel's 'mild' label, and doubting Tampa's claim that [[Chris Godwin]] is 'right on track' when a second surgery could not plausibly have been pre-planned. - 2026-08-17 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-08-17]]): Waldman laid out a position-by-position framework for weighting camp/preseason reports. Quarterbacks: preseason defensive concepts and competition level are rarely tougher than what they saw in college, and continuity across drifting rosters is poor, so he wants 12-15 regular-season games before moving a rookie QB meaningfully; great preseason stats don't move him. Receivers: alignment and coverage matter more than box-score plays -- winning against loose zone from the slot is hype, while winning tight man coverage on timing routes (curls, digs, comebacks, outs) against press is the real signal, since it shows processing in sync with the quarterback. Tight ends: for non-blocking specialists, learning to block determines whether they're stuck in an ancillary role. Overall he said this rarely moves a player more than a spot or two on his board.
