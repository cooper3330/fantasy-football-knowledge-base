# Usage as Evidence of Ability

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-05-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-15]]): a direct counter to Steve Smith's principle that 'teams show you what a receiver can't do by showing you what they're asking him to do' — that what a staff declines to ask of a player is probably what he cannot do. Waldman grants the heuristic usually holds, but says it only holds where a program had better alternatives. He offers the LSU room of [[Ja'Marr Chase]], Terrace Marshall and [[Justin Jefferson]] as a case where the inference is fair, versus Missouri, where [[Luther Burden III]] was the best athlete on the field and the staff simply took the easiest route to getting him the ball: 'why are you going to put him on the outside and do difficult things that he can do but make it more difficult on your quarterback?' [[Brandon Angelo]] adds the mechanical version — Missouri could not pass protect long enough for timing concepts and the quarterback could not throw them — so the absence of downfield route usage is a system artifact, not a skill verdict. Waldman also credits Smith's technique-first analysis on [[Cooper Kupp]] while noting his current work leans more toward entertainment.
- 2025-06-27 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-27]]): Steve Smith's mantra — they will tell you where a player is good by how they use him — is true, but has a corollary analysts ignore: if a team has nobody else, the question becomes where you can put your best player to get him the ball easiest and most often. With the 2024 Missouri Tigers unable to name a second outside receiver on par with a third-round NFL pick and chaos at quarterback, putting [[Luther Burden III]] inside on manufactured touches was the obvious answer and says little about his ceiling. [[Matt Harmon]] agreed and extended it to how coaching staffs later expand a role, citing [[Quentin Johnston]]'s year-two slant usage as a staff correcting its own deployment.
- 2025-07-10 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-10]]): efficiency has to be read against situation, because defenses distribute respect unevenly within a backfield. He argues 2024 Cleveland invited runs at a minus-10 point-margin differential and stacked the box only 17% of the time — among the league's lowest — letting complementary backs gash them for five yards a carry while loading up for [[Nick Chubb]], who still scored the touchdowns; he makes the same argument for [[Rachaad White]] absorbing the known-run touches while [[Bucky Irving]] got the surprise ones. [[Brandon Angelo]] supplies the other half: running back is a trust position, and West Coast coaches evaluate it best in live game action — [[Matt Waldman]] quotes Kyle Shanahan saying practice does not show how a back adjusts in real time or rebounds from mistakes. Angelo notes uber-talented backs get phased out every year when a staff cannot trust them, citing Zach Evans versus [[Kyren Williams]] under Sean McVay.
- 2025-07-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-16]]): Harmon and co-host James Koh (not tracked) attack the 'if he were good, he would produce' argument used against paying [[Garrett Wilson]]. Their counter is that receivers cannot magic themselves the football, and that lumping every bad situation together — [[Malik Nabers]] and [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] produced with poor quarterbacks, so why not Wilson — ignores differences in scheme and design. Harmon calls 'targets are earned' directionally accurate rather than always true, and prefers target rate per route as the evidence that ability is being recognized: Wilson ranks 10th among receivers 25 or younger, which he reads as the team throwing to him because he is open and because they believe he is good, even while yards per route run stays mediocre.
- 2025-08-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-15]]): on what first-team reps for a late-round or undrafted player actually mean, guest Dan Hatman (former Eagles and Giants scout, NOT a tracked expert) described the nightly Philadelphia practice meeting where coaches and personnel staff discussed whether the fourth back in a room had earned a look. Reps get handed out because somebody will get hurt and the 89th man may have to play, and the test is not whether you can beat our backups, because if I stick you in a game in October you are not playing against backups. Hatman's key point is continuation: one first-team look is nothing, but staying in that mix week after week means trust is being earned. It also may simply mean the starter's job is settled and reps are being redistributed to resolve the next fight down the depth chart, so it does not put a player in starter territory by itself.
- 2025-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-19]]): technique on a handful of reps tells you more about a skill than the raw opportunity count does. Defending his read of [[RJ Harvey]] as an excellent pass receiver, Waldman notes that people prefer to count how many targets a college back saw rather than assess the techniques that actually work, and lists [[Jonathan Taylor]], Melvin Gordon and [[Leonard Fournette]] as backs whose receiving ability was doubted purely because their carry totals dwarfed their target totals — yet whose catching mechanics and deployment on limited reps showed what they could do, and who later produced 40-50 catch seasons. He is careful to say this does not make them [[Christian McCaffrey]] or [[Austin Ekeler]]. Waldman applies the same logic in reverse to [[Tetairoa McMillan]], arguing that counting charted data does not always tell you how a skill will project against NFL press man coverage.
- 2025-09-02 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-02]]): the waiver segment produced an extended, unresolved argument with guest BRANDON FUNSTON of The Athletic (NOT a tracked expert) over whether volume or talent comes first. Funston's position is that opportunity is the first thing he looks for — 'this is our whole game, is volume' — so his dice rolls ([[Xavier Legette]], [[Demario Douglas]], [[Roman Wilson]]) are all players with a visible path to playing time. Harris's counter is that if the player stinks, the volume does not matter: he has a choice of 32 teams all of whom will produce volume, so he picks the players he has actually seen be good. He rejects the chicken-and-egg framing directly — coaches do not throw someone out there and discover afterwards whether he is good; they decide a guy is good and then hand him the ball to prove it, so volume is downstream of a talent judgement, not evidence generating one. Funston counters that the best players get the volume, which is the same thing.
- 2025-09-08 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-08]]): Week 1 is the sound of mind palaces shattering, because all the offseason guesses about depth charts, snap counts and target share now look exactly like guesses, and it is time to use your eyeballs on real games. His method is deliberately to watch every game rather than read box scores. The concrete role reversals he catalogued: [[Kenneth Gainwell]] out-snapping [[Jaylen Warren]] 7-4 in the first quarter with [[Kaleb Johnson]] on two offensive snaps, [[Kareem Hunt]] taking basically every third down and short-yardage snap from [[Isiah Pacheco]], [[Zach Charbonnet]] out-snapping [[Kenneth Walker III]] 29-20 and taking the goal-line score, and [[Bucky Irving]] posting an 18-3-1 touch advantage that overturned Harris's own August skepticism.
- 2025-09-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-18 (Film & Theory)]]): a restricted early role is weak evidence about talent, because over short timelines players land in strange roles for many reasons — development, earning trust, a coach protecting them, occasionally genuine coaching malpractice, which he says is less common than fans assume — but over long timelines players find the role their ability warrants. His examples: [[Ashton Jeanty]] losing pass protection, third down, short yardage and two-minute work as a sixth overall pick; Adrian Peterson starting nine games behind Chester Taylor as a rookie; Jamaal Charles running behind Thomas Jones while Jones averaged about 3.2 yards per carry and Charles posted 1,000-plus rushing yards on under 200 carries; [[Nick Chubb]] a second-round pick opening his rookie year on punt duty as the third-string back; Barry Sanders pulled at the goal line. Waldman's rule is that when a team has a competent veteran at something the rookie is weak at, letting the veteran do it while the rookie ramps up is reasonable, not a talent verdict. Harstad (Football Guys, not a tracked expert) adds that he wishes teams used real starters on special teams more, believing injury risk is overstated and that [[Alvin Kamara]] could have been an all-time kick returner.
- 2025-10-02 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-10-02]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harris pushed back repeatedly on 'chase volume' as a reflex, posing the question directly — does volume arise because you are simply handed it, or does volume happen because you are good, making the job to pick out good players whom volume eventually finds? He applied it to the Arizona backfield ([[Michael Carter]], [[Emari Demercado]]), where neither man has ever won over a staff, and to the depleted 49ers, where the vacated targets do not make [[Kendrick Bourne]] good. He acknowledged the opposing spreadsheet logic — someone has to absorb 15 targets, so a projection model forces a [[DeMarcus Robinson]] into your lineup — and said he understands it but cannot live with it. Jeff Bell of Football Guys (not tracked) agreed that the analyst instinct that 'there must be an answer' is often wrong: sometimes it is not any of these guys, and the situation is one to avoid.
- 2025-10-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-10-07]]): relaying a theory from Ron Stewart (YouTube, not a tracked expert), receivers at stockpiled programs like Ohio State and LSU are never fully maximized in college because they are queued behind other stars — Stewart compares it to Kentucky basketball. [[Emeka Egbuka]] was a top recruit, productive every year, and never 'the guy'; [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] sat behind [[Garrett Wilson]] and [[Chris Olave]] and got labelled a slot-only player for it. Harmon's charting shows both had tiny press-coverage samples with outstanding success rates — Egbuka faced press on only 7.6% of routes but at a 92nd-percentile success rate. He endorses the Daniel Jeremiah line that if you show you can do it, that counts, while maintaining that repeatable evidence and a catalog of reps remain critical; the reconciliation is that small-sample elite success plus a clear reason for the small sample should not be discounted as absent ability.
- 2025-10-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-10-09]]): Harmon made a deliberate meta point that the fantasy and dynasty communities have overcorrected into appeal to authority — treating a player's lack of playing time as proof he is not good, on the assumption teams know best. He argued teams make mistakes constantly, and used [[Jack Bech]] as the case: the read that it was a bad sign he could not get on the field for an opportunity-rich Raiders team ignores the possibility the Raiders simply gave the snaps to the wrong guys, choosing a fourth-round developmental prospect in [[Dont'e Thornton Jr.]] because he flashed in padless practices, which Harmon said is not football. He also flagged that respected but older staffs can be stuck in a past era of receiver archetypes. Related note: he still respects usage as signal when it is intentional, praising Houston's designed goal-line target for [[Jaylin Noel]] and Denver's designed touches for [[Troy Franklin]].
- 2025-11-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-11-13]]): Harmon draws a deliberate asymmetry in the 'targets are earned' argument. Over a long sample he accepts that players seeing 120-130 targets are earning them — you do not fall backwards into that volume. What he rejects is the inverse: calling a player not a target earner, because low volume is usually circumstantial. [[Jaylen Waddle]] is his clean example — an 80% zone beater and 70% man beater in last year's charting whose efficiency numbers cratered because of a quick-throw quarterback, [[Tyreek Hill]]'s creative touches, screens to [[De'Von Achane]] and manufactured looks to Jonnu Smith behind a bad offensive line. He pairs it with his own bias check on undersized separators like [[Jayden Reed]], [[Josh Downs]] and [[Jaylin Noel]] getting pigeonholed, noting [[Jaxon Smith-Njigba]] was labelled slot-only before becoming, in Harmon's words, the best receiver in the NFL.
- 2026-01-22 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2026-01-22]]): pushed back on the 'target earner' framing using [[Michael Wilson]] as his example -- Wilson barely saw targets early in his career and became excellent immediately once volume arrived, which Harmon says proves the talent was always there rather than that he 'earned' targets through some binary switch. He argued the framing is 'founded in something smart but misses the mark' because it obscures that talented players get overlooked purely due to a crowded target pool, not a lack of ability.
- 2026-02-13 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-02-13]]) [Dynasty]: for tight ends, college receiving usage is a hard evidentiary floor -- he said almost no tight end with under about 640 receiving yards in his best college season has gone on to be a fully productive NFL receiving tight end, treating that number as a rough threshold for whether a prospect was ever the focal point of his college passing game. He flagged [[Colston Loveland]] (649 yards, limited by a weak Michigan environment and injury) as an exception worth adjusting for context rather than dinging on the raw number alone.
- 2026-05-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-05-12]]): Harmon pushed back on the wider industry's enthusiasm for [[Luther Burden III]], arguing much of his rookie production was generated touches (screens, flat and slant routes made up nearly 40% of his route mix) rather than proof of 'real receiver' separation skill -- even though his underlying RP success rates (71.1% vs man, 80.4% vs zone) were genuinely strong. Untracked co-host James Koh countered that clearing those thresholds is 'real enough,' illustrating a disagreement between the two on how much weight to give usage/box-score stats versus route-level charting.
