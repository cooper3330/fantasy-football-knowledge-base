# Manufactured Touches vs Natural Separation

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-04-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and James Koh (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-16]]): the premium archetype is a receiver who is dangerous after the catch AND separates on his own, because the offense does not have to reimagine plays to feed him. Koh contrasts [[Kyle Williams]] — line him up as the backside iso X, he runs a slant or curl, wins quickly against press and then does damage — with what he calls the 'Mickey Mouse' [[Kadarius Toney]] archetype, good with the ball but requiring designed touches. Harmon applies the same axis to [[Isaiah Bond]] and [[Jalen Royals]], concluding that both need manufactured underneath touches (drags, quick slants with space cleared, motion looks) because neither yet separates on nuance, which caps how early a team should invest.
- 2025-04-22 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-22]]): the gadget-player label is being misapplied to prospects whose college offenses chose to manufacture their touches. On [[Luther Burden III]], Harmon argued that how Missouri used him — screens and create-a-touch designs, deployed to win games — says little about whether he can run a real route tree, because the real route reps do exist on the film, just not in volume. He rejects both the anti-Burden camp that treats manufactured usage as proof of a limitation and the pro-Burden camp that cannot see why the league is cooling on him. The evaluative question he substitutes is whether the drafting team shares the vision of him as a full-time receiver and how hard the receiver coach rides him, not the ratio of screens to routes in his college charting.
- 2025-04-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-29]]): if an offense is going to carry a gadget receiver who cannot run routes and get open, the one thing that player must do is catch the ball reliably — the [[Kadarius Toney]] failure mode is a manufactured-touch player who never ran routes, could not get open, and then could not be trusted to catch what was schemed to him. [[Savion Williams]] clears that bar (one drop over three games, 100% contested catch rate) even with a 46.4% success rate vs man, so Harmon accepts him as a create-a-touch weapon. The mirror-image error is running a genuine route runner as a gadget player: Harmon argues [[Jayden Reed]], an 87th-percentile press beater as a prospect, has been used like a Deebo Samuel-lite jet-sweep piece when he should be running real routes.
- 2025-05-14 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-05-14]]): Harmon and Koh use 'layups' as the explanatory variable for why two similarly-charting rookies produced very differently. [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] got one screen in an eight-game sample and was not even thrown to on it, almost no flat routes and low slant participation (14.3%), finishing with only four in-space catches. [[Rome Odunze]] got 36% of catches in space and led all receivers in uncatchable target share because his route chart was almost entirely downfield. By contrast [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] ran screens on 6.5% of routes at over 10 yards per target under the NFL's best screen coach, and [[Malik Nabers]] had a slant success rate of 84.9% that the Giants simply fed. Harmon's point is that separation ability alone does not produce fantasy points — a receiver whose only opportunities are contested downfield targets from an old-school static X alignment will under-produce his charting, and manufactured, in-space touches are how a team converts good route running into volume and efficiency.
- 2025-06-25 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] with guest Cory Kinnan (Reception Perception QB charting, NOT a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-25]]): Harmon uses charted route distribution as the measure of how much an offense is asking a receiver to earn versus be handed — [[Garrett Wilson]] ran a go or slant on 47.9% of charted 2024 routes while tethered to the line of scrimmage, versus [[Jameson Williams]] running a go on under 14% in Detroit — and argues Wilson's degree of difficulty should fall sharply under a more dynamic design, producing 'so many more layups.' Kinnan's application is that Tanner Engstrand should use Wilson closer to an [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] motion-and-rub role than a field-stretcher, precisely because [[Justin Fields]] cannot be trusted to find him against post-snap rotation. The same lens is applied in Carolina, where Harmon sees [[Xavier Legette]] as viable only on manufactured, YAC-oriented touches and Kinnan sees [[Jalen Coker]]'s juice showing up on the same kind of usage.
- 2025-06-27 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-06-27]]): a usage profile dominated by screens, RPOs and crossers does not disprove separation ability, because the techniques that produce separation are not flukes — when a player shows stem work, weight drops, sudden stops and top-of-break positioning in combination, it came from deliberate practice, not luck. He applied this to [[Luther Burden III]] and cited [[Brandon Aiyuk]] as the player he himself missed on for exactly this reason. [[Matt Harmon]] added the charting version: Burden faced man coverage at the lowest rate of anyone he charted that year, so the reps are scarce but the quality of the few reps is the evidence. Both invoked the [[Ladd McConkey]] precedent — critics pointed to a college production profile heavy on schemed touches, and both hosts were 'full bore' on him anyway.
- 2025-07-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-16]]): the 2024 [[Marvin Mims Jr.]] profile is Harmon's cleanest example of a role built entirely to avoid a player's route-running deficit — RP-record 30.7% backfield snaps, 24.4% screens, 79.7% zone faced, an 80.3% in-space catch rate, and simultaneously a first-percentile 44% success rate versus man with no in-breaking route at NFL average. Harmon praises the design as innovative and productive, but frames the limit clearly: the player needs the role catering, so the efficiency is not evidence of latent separation ability. He draws the same distinction for [[Quentin Johnston]], whose improvement came from tripled in-space touches rather than from becoming a better man-coverage beater.
- 2025-07-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-28]]): he defines a matchup player as a receiver who can win one-on-one against tight man press on timing routes — the guy you can throw to on third-and-15 as the first or second read when the defense knows it is coming. A scheme player is an elevated gadget player: rarely wins one-on-one, and instead gets open by sneaking behind a defense flowing the other way. Gabriel Davis is his league example. Waldman applies the distinction to [[Travis Hunter]], noting Colorado tape showed him pinned to the boundary a lot by physical corners, and argues a broader point about the current receiver class — results can look good while the process of creating space with nuance is weaker than advertised, and the two should not be conflated.
- 2025-08-15 — According to Cousin Josh (untracked co-host of the [[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast]]) ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-15]]): the late-2024 [[Xavier Worthy]] surge is the case study — the Chiefs specifically manufactured red-zone touches for him during a stretch with no [[Rashee Rice]], a hobbled [[Isiah Pacheco]] and a diminished [[Travis Kelce]], and the market then priced that as a permanent role. He points to 18 targets of 20-plus yards with three caught as evidence the downfield winning was not there. [[Chris Harris]] characterized the scoring the same way — gadget plays, handoffs, two-yard screens in the red zone — and noted these are exactly the touches Rice would be better at once healthy and available, which makes them a fragile foundation for a top-50 pick.
- 2025-08-15 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-15]]): Harmon's 2024 [[D.J. Moore]] case study is his sharpest statement of the cost of manufactured usage — an Andre Johnson-style alignment (76% of sampled snaps on the line of scrimmage, out at the boundary) married to a [[Kadarius Toney]] route tree of 10.5% screens and flats, producing the league lead in screen targets and catches while stranding the receiver in space with no blockers. He quotes Moore that it is scary getting the ball with nobody blocking for you, but good when there is a guy ahead of you blocking it up. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) compares it to Lane Kiffin's USC offense with Marquise Lee, built on wide bubble screens, and argues those are cheap yards you cannot sustain drives with — the same objection he raises to a [[Tai Felton]] underneath role in Minnesota.
- 2025-08-20 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-20]]): Harmon raises a durability corollary to the manufactured-touch role — receivers who are moved around the formation and fed the ball quickly underneath absorb hits from linebackers and interior defenders rather than from 180-pound corners near the boundary, which he says makes their injury profile 'a little bit like running backs'. He applies it to [[Malik Nabers]], whose camp back, toe and shoulder issues are on his radar, and connects it to [[Chris Godwin]]'s history of missing time in a high-volume power-slot role. He presents it as an open question on his radar, not a settled downgrade.
- 2025-09-15 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-15]]): Kansas City has assembled a receiver room in which every member 'needs some role catering' — [[Rashee Rice]], [[Xavier Worthy]] and [[Jalen Royals]] are each a particular archetype requiring manufactured touches — and has doubled down on that type since [[Tyreek Hill]] left, leaving nobody who can drop back and rip it over the middle of the field. He warns against reading Rice's first-down-per-route-run rate as separation quality: measured against the other names on that leaderboard, his came from screens, 'three air yards and hopefully a cloud of dust.' [[Chris Harris]] adds that Andy Reid seems to have decided the key to winning is three-step drops and two-yard passes, which is not where the rest of the league is going. Conversely Harris says Miami's screen volume to [[De'Von Achane]] now looks like healthy design rather than the symptom of a sick offense.
- 2025-10-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-13]]): [[Tucker Kraft]] and [[Tyler Warren]] are being schemed into open space rather than winning it as route runners, and Waldman drew a hard line between the two hats. As a fantasy analyst you want exactly this all day long — the Packers use insert-block motion toward a line gap to pull the corner inside, leaving space wide open, then delay-release Kraft off the block into it, including off play action with [[Savion Williams]]. As an NFL evaluator, though, he said he would call bullshit on any analyst who used that production to claim Kraft is on [[George Kittle]]'s level as a route runner. The design does not diminish the finishing skill — Kraft made a diving low seam catch and ran through a defender on the touchdown — but the separation is manufactured.
- 2026-05-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-05-05]]) [Dynasty]: charting [[Zachariah Branch]]'s final Georgia season, Harmon found 28.9% of his sampled routes were screens -- the highest screen-route rate he has ever charted for a full prospect profile, well clear of the next-highest names (Rondale Moore 20.9%, Amari Rodgers 18.9%, Darius Stewart 16%). He argues that inflates Branch's 81.3% zone-coverage success rate, and that the more diagnostic number is his non-screen work against zone, where Harmon felt Branch's spatial awareness and feel still need development, versus his genuinely strong man-coverage and dig-route work in true one-on-one reps.
