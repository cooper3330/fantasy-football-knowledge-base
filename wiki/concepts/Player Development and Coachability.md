# Player Development and Coachability

## Definition

Why two players with the same measurables end up in different places: whether
the player will accept coaching, whether the organization is set up to give it,
and whether coach and front office even agree on what the player is for. The
[[Dynasty]] version of the question is *which situations actually improve a
player*, since the same prospect lands very differently in a Detroit-style
locker room than in a scheme-first one.

Distinct from — and paired with — [[NIL and Player Development]], which covers
the *structural* incentive question (does anyone in college or the NFL have a
reason to develop players at all).

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]: the problem is two-sided. "A lot of pros don't want to be developed — they've been told their whole life they're the best." And a coach "doesn't want to change philosophically" either. His example of the coach half is Atlanta: "personnel over scheme is not the case, it's scheme over personnel" — a top-eight pick in [[Bijan Robinson]], "a rhythm runner who gets better with the more touches he gets," splitting work with Cordarrelle Patterson. See [[Scheme vs Talent]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the positive case: the Detroit Lions "are not as talented as some of those teams in the NFL, but they play an unselfish brand of football that works with those guys' personalities." His read is that Detroit recruits for cultural fit — he names [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]], [[David Montgomery]], Jared Goff and [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] ("listen to him speak") — and that the locker room is strong enough to absorb and "massage along" a player who fits less naturally, citing Jameson Williams. His stated marker of a good organization: players who believe "I don't know everything, I want to get better at my craft," and coaches who hold the same view.

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode, the front-office failure mode, relayed from a former scout and biomechanics/analytics consultant who has worked with roughly 30 teams and is a longtime RSP subscriber. Waldman's published floor scenario for [[Sam LaPorta]] was that he "winds up on the bench getting fattened up and put on special teams because the coach and the GM are in a pissing match" — the coach wanting an inline blocking tight end, the GM having drafted a weapon, and the player being told to gain 20 pounds and block. The consultant's reaction: "that's literally the RIP for so many NFL players." Recorded here as a *scouting* rule — a prospect's floor is often organizational, not physical.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, the long-form player-side case study: [[Drew Lock]]. Waldman's evaluation was that Lock's game was "much more surface level" than the Patrick Mahomes comparisons implied — "the difference between a pop musician who plays an instrument and a jazz musician." His read of the cause was developmental, not athletic: a high-school phenom at a mid-tier SEC school that needed him too much to bench him, so undisciplined technique never got enforced; against Alabama/Georgia/LSU you could see him trying to do it correctly and failing, "like the kid who was naturally gifted at math but now getting stretched." The story Waldman tells as the crux: Peyton Manning called Lock after the draft offering help, Lock said thanks and ended the call — where, in Waldman's telling, a Tom Brady-type would have offered to wash cars and babysit for film sessions. Lock called Manning back in year three, when Teddy Bridgewater arrived, and it was too late in Denver. Waldman quotes Lock's father (via **Cecil Lammey**, not a tracked expert) saying he had been telling his son since high school to apply himself and "he's never done it." Waldman's explicit generalization: "you don't have to be a bad person — you just may not understand the gravity of the situation. You're great for where you are; you're not great for where you're going." Angelo's label for it: "being situationally naive." Both note Geno Smith as the counter-arc from a similar starting point, and Angelo's contrast is Tom Brady at Michigan being told he could transfer or treat every snap like the Super Bowl.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman and Angelo**, same episode, the upside of self-knowledge: [[Gardner Minshew]] as the model backup — "he knows exactly who he is," has no illusions about being a starter, and is valuable precisely because he can "execute schematically, not make mistakes, just give me a chance to win." Angelo's stated league-wide condition: "there's a lot of bad quarterbacks right now in the NFL."

- 2024-01-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-04]]: a positive organizational-patience example in [[Jordan Love]] — Green Bay giving him extended bench time to develop before starting, in the lineage of how the franchise handled Aaron Rodgers, Brett Favre, Matt Hasselbeck, and Mark Brunell. Read alongside the [[Drew Lock]] case above, the contrast is *organization* rather than *player*: both were unpolished young passers, but Green Bay's development pipeline gave Love the runway Denver didn't give Lock.

- 2024-01-11 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-11 (Going Deep - Draft Prospects)]]) [Dynasty], applied to a 2024 quarterback prospect, [[Michael Penix Jr.]]: the physical-trauma version of the same "needs a runway" argument. Waldman's framing question is whether a beaten-up young passer can still become a good pro once his tools decline — his historical yes-cases are Jim Plunkett, Steve Young, and Terry Bradshaw, all of whom got physically and publicly battered early before recovering (Young explicitly credits sitting behind Joe Montana in Bill Walsh's offense for turning him from a "backyard quarterback" into a technician); his no-cases are Trent Edwards and Carson Strong. Angelo's stated mechanism for why this is hard: "every time a player gets injured, the mind has to become that much stronger than the body" — cognitive/decision-making skill has to rise as physical tools decline, and "some guys come back, and you can't." Their shared prescription for Penix is the same [[Jordan Love]] runway already logged above: sit behind a veteran rather than start as a rookie, because his path to sustained success runs through decision-making more than arm talent. Waldman's related in-season coaching note, same episode: young quarterbacks who get hurt need **intermittent playing time** pulled back proactively — "once they start to get into that trouble and they're out in the deep end... you need to get them out of the water right then and there" — rather than leaving them exposed once bad reps start compounding.

- 2024-02-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-19]]), a positive organizational case study applied to [[George Pickens]]: singles out an in-season incident where Pickens publicly declined to throw a block, then was visibly blocking again a week later, as evidence of Pittsburgh handling a young player's immaturity internally — accountability inside the building rather than a public callout — rather than letting a bad habit calcify. Framed in the same episode as part of a broader case for why HC Mike Tomlin gets "a longer leash" than a coach like Bill Belichick: not just message/culture, but a stronger recent draft record at premium skill positions (wide receiver specifically) giving the roster more talent for that culture to work with.

- 2024-02-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-22]]) [Dynasty], applied to a 2024 running back prospect, [[Braelon Allen]]: the same "needs a runway" prescription used elsewhere on this page for young quarterbacks, applied to a big power back instead of a passer. Waldman's explicit ask is an experienced offensive line, established complementary backs, and — ideally — a same-archetype veteran to sit behind and eventually replace (his stated wish is a multi-year [[Derrick Henry]] understudy role), rather than an immediate workhorse assignment as a 19-year-old rookie. Framed against the standing "Wisconsin curse" precedent — Jonathan Taylor and Melvin Gordon (neither tracked) both struggled and were briefly called busts before their NFL adjustment caught up — as the reason patience, not an early bench, is the correct read on a rocky rookie season if it happens. Full take on [[Braelon Allen]].

- 2024-03-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-23]]), solo RB-class cast, relaying an anecdote DeMarco Murray (not tracked) told him at the Senior Bowl: applied to explosive Monmouth back Jaden Sheridan (not given a page — a small-school prospect whose speed is unlikely to translate one-for-one to Sundays), Murray said that in high school in Las Vegas he was "the best athlete on the field" and maybe "one of the best athletes in the city," but at Oklahoma "I wasn't even the best athlete on my depth chart, much less my team," and he had to relearn how to run with that in mind. Waldman's generalization: young backs who lean on straight-line speed and boom/bust plays because "they got away with it" in high school and college have to learn to manage down-and-distance and situational football with maturity, since NFL coaches want an expanded playbook and de-value boom/bust decision-making — "no shame" in needing that adjustment period.

**Why this is in the wiki:** it is the mechanism behind a recurring dynasty
mistake — buying a talented player into a situation that will not develop him,
or selling one whose problem was situational. It also supplies the reason
Waldman's floor scenarios are written as organizational stories rather than
injury/athleticism stories.
- 2024-04-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-23 (Evaluation vs. Valuation)]]): Waldman argues 'a team's ability to coach a guy up' is an overvalued factor in draft evaluation — some prospects are close to maxed out on what coaching can add regardless of the strength of their college program, and it isn't always clear from tape alone which players still have real developmental room left. He points to Kyle Shanahan's public comments that modern NFL teams practice and teach fundamentals far less than in past eras, leaving players largely responsible for offseason technique work with personal position coaches while the team's in-season focus shifts almost entirely to game-planning — meaning a prospect's realized ceiling depends heavily on self-directed development and situation, not just being placed with a 'good' coaching staff.
- 2024-07-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-22]]): two quarterback cases where the open question is receptiveness rather than tools. [[Drew Lock]] he describes as a privileged player who did not know it until he lost the Denver job, whose father said he had ignored the coaching points he needed since high school — Waldman reads the Seattle relief win and the interview afterward as a sign he finally got the memo, while allowing that the memo may instead have been that he is content as a backup. [[Sam Darnold]] is the parallel: the physical and creative traits are all there, but the reads (underneath coverage, blitz and coverage disguises) and the confidence damage from pressure have not been fixed, and Waldman cites Josh Dobbs as evidence that writing such players off entirely is premature.
- 2024-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-19]]): two related development arguments. First, reps are a factor in quarterback development but not the factor — there are many quarterbacks who got the reps and never amounted to anything — and preseason reps in particular are low-value because of vanilla coverage and personnel churn, so sitting [[Michael Penix Jr.]] costs little. Second, the sequencing of a young starter matters more than the timing: shoving a rookie in, letting him fail publicly and benching him at 0-5 leaves the owner off him, the fan base off him and the player wondering if he ever gets the job back — 'forever flushed down the toilet of quarterback potential.' The better path is a veteran who already knows what he is and what his limitations are, functioning as a coach on the field and in the room, with the young player taking intermittent snaps while working on his weaknesses. That is his read on Las Vegas naming [[Gardner Minshew]] over [[Aidan O'Connell]].
- 2024-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-29]]): misdeployment can destroy a young receiver independent of his talent. His case study is [[Skyy Moore]], who Harmon says was never going to be special but should not have become one of the least efficient receivers in the league — that came from Kansas City forcing him outside instead of using him as a short-area slot. Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) goes further, calling it 'textbook 101 how not to treat a young wide receiver' and saying the Chiefs killed his confidence, while noting Moore looked good in the slot as a rookie. A second thread is that development requires a team willing to wait: Harmon says [[Luke McCaffrey]]'s selection would have been defensible for a team planning to build him into a high-volume slot, and was a bad fit only because Washington needed instant impact, with Koh adding that it is extremely difficult to learn the position on the job in the NFL.
- 2024-09-02 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-02]]): Waldman used [[Jonathan Mingo]] to draw the dividing line among young receivers. A lot of players think they have made it once they reach the NFL, and that is where they mess up; others know they have not made it but are only hoping someone will tell them something useful, because they are in their early twenties and do not really understand how to approach work or how to seek out help. The rare third case is the player who goes looking — Mingo, after a bad rookie season, asked who would give him 'the unvarnished truth' about himself as a receiver and would work with him if he asked, found Steve Smith, and did the work. Waldman treats the reported improvement in Mingo's routes as the visible output of that process, and the process itself as the reason to bet on a second-year bounce-back rather than the box score.
- 2024-09-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-05 (Film and Theory)]]): his working theory is that coaches coach to scheme, not to the development of a player's individual skills, so camp praise about technique should be treated as a test of the coach rather than evidence about the player — his live case being Jim Harbaugh's claim that [[Quentin Johnston]] has always been a good pass catcher, when Waldman's tape work shows Johnston mixed at best on exactly the contested and vertical targets that matter. Adam Harstad (not a tracked expert) sharpened it: the common belief that coaches lie is mostly wrong — coaches are surprisingly honest, they just frequently do not know what they are talking about, as with Bruce Arians on his starting running back, who was honest at the time and simply changed his mind. Waldman's structural explanation is that football is siloed and nobody manages every silo expertly, and when development is left to the player and his private coaches, a head coach has no obligation to know. His tell for a player who studies the craft is one who breaks down technique and concepts rather than praising size and speed, the way he says roughly 95% of NFL 100 interviews do.
