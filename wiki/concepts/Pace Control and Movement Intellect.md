# Pace Control and Movement Intellect

## Definition

The cross-positional trait [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] argue
separates elite NFL movers: not top-end speed, but the ability to **control
your own gears** play-to-play — and, paired with it, to **gauge the pace and
angles of everyone else** — and then to take only what the play actually offers.
Angelo's shorthand is "one of the biggest signs of high-level cognitive
athletes."

This is the **sibling** of [[Running Back Size and Movement Skills]], not a
replacement for it. That page holds the *physical* argument (change of direction
inside tight creases, and the ~205–215 lb ceiling Waldman puts on it). This page
holds the *cognitive/tempo* argument, which the same episode applies to
quarterbacks and receivers as well as backs. Read them together.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]: the framing take. The thread linking [[Lamar Jackson]], [[James Cook]], [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] and [[Christian McCaffrey]] is "how well they control the pace of their own game" — athletes who "control space and time" or "know how to control their own gears on a given play." Jackson is his purest case: "Lamar Jackson never reaches top gear." The negative exemplar is [[Isiah Pacheco]] — "you get zero or a hundred." Defenders struggle because these players are "moving at a different pace on every single play."

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the second half of the trait: taking what is blocked instead of hunting the explosive. "Every play in the NFL is not a score mission." Elite movers know what a given play can yield "based on their own capabilities, based on what's blocked, based on what the defense is," and they also manage *contact type* — high, low, direct, indirect — to negate punishment over a 200-hit season. He credits [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] with the version of this that young players usually lack: when it isn't there, Gibbs protects himself and goes down, "and he's okay doing that." The counter-case is [[Breece Hall]], who Angelo says tries to hit the home run every down. His summary of the payoff: these players "become the engines of their own offense."

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode: pace control is only half of it — "it's not just them controlling their own pace, it's also being able to gauge the pace of others, and it's a marriage between the two." All of the named players "can survey the situation around them and have a great gauge for what defenders are doing within the scope of the play, and how they can leverage their skills — how much or how little." His mechanism is **body awareness plus boundary knowledge**: knowing your own limits well enough to spend exactly the right amount. His analogy is Muhammad Ali on the ropes, and his counter-example is Johnny Manziel, who had the trait "in spades in college" but in the pros needed to become a student of what he could and could not still get away with, and never did.

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the structure argument (a direct challenge to a common framing): "it's constraints versus no constraints, structure versus lack of structure." The received view on [[Lamar Jackson]] is that he is great without structure and poor within it. Angelo's version: "they're so good without structure that they don't need structure — it's not that they're not good in structure." These players are in fact excellent inside the structure of a play precisely "because they understand the bandwidth of what the play can give them." See [[Scheme vs Talent]] for the scheme-side version of this argument.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, extending the trait to receivers: [[Malik Nabers]] "excels at pacing variation" — using pace with his footwork and inside his routes to set defenders up. "The best receivers tell stories well like that." Michael Crabtree is his historical example of a receiver who was a terrific route runner "for his size" specifically because he understood how to use pace. See [[Zone vs Man Route Running]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the tackling-difficulty inversion this produces: ask an NFL linebacker who is harder to bring down, [[Derrick Henry]] in his prime or [[Christian McCaffrey]] / [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] / [[James Cook]], and "the answer might surprise people." Sheer size is a known quantity; "I have to deal with Christian McCaffrey's feet, I have to deal with the re-acceleration of Jahmyr Gibbs" is a guessing game. **Waldman's** caveat in Henry's favor, same episode: in the fourth quarter, after 15–18 touches against a tired defense, you would rather not face Henry — though he adds that the movers are just as unwelcome late, because "if they're on a roll you're always guessing with them," whereas with Henry "you're not guessing — you're just praying you can hit him low."

- 2024-01-11 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-11 (Going Deep - Draft Prospects)]] [Dynasty]: applies the framework to a 2024 prospect, [[Blake Corum]], and states the underlying mechanism most explicitly yet — "the faster you play, the faster you need to process the information." Comparing [[Kyren Williams]] to a pure speed player like Anthony McFarland: a slower mover "can see the changing picture much clearer" because he can act on it in real time as it develops, while an elite top-gear player is trying to act on a picture that's already changed by the time he processes it — "it's just a guessing game."

- 2024-01-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode: extends the trait into a named mechanism, **compensatory skill** — "if they don't have certain physical traits, then they have to have other physical traits and mental processing traits that compensate for what they lack," and if they have enough of it, "they can be exceptional... enough to be good enough to play in the league." His worked examples: Frank Gore, a slow, undersized back who nonetheless out-produced faster committee-mates (Marlon Mack, Kenyan Drake) because forced precision and patience in his footwork let him press blocks deeper and bait defenders in ways faster backs never had to learn; [[Devin Singletary]], whom Waldman calls "the picture in the dictionary of football" for a compensatory talent. States the exception that proves the rule: Adrian Peterson, a "physical freak" strong and fast enough to use the space-inefficient jump-cut (the [[Running Back Size and Movement Skills]] alternative) *and* still get away with it — a combination normally unavailable to non-elite athletes, and part of why his game aged unusually.

- 2024-02-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-05]]): asked what most people get wrong about evaluating running backs, restates the compensatory-skill mechanism as a direct speed critique — "I think they overemphasize speed." His framing: there is a minimum baseline of speed a back needs, but "anything beyond that... if they don't have the rest of the baseline skills, like good decision-making, managing the game, good balance and footwork, who cares about speed." Names non-elite-speed backs who succeeded on those other traits instead — Devin Singletary (already this page's named exemplar of compensatory talent), LeGarrette Blount, Emmitt Smith, and Walter Payton ("wasn't extraordinarily fast") — as the standing evidence, and flags this as the recurring pre-draft trap: college "scene players" who flash two or three big plays a game at 20-plus mph get "vaulted up draft boards" without the baseline skills to sustain it as pros.

- 2024-02-22 — [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-22]] [Dynasty], opening a full 2024 running-back-class episode by naming the specific skills they consider hardest to develop once a prospect reaches the league — a direct, structured restatement of this page's framework aimed at evaluating draft prospects rather than veterans. Angelo's #1: **creating space as a runner**, because the college-to-NFL jump removes the athletic-advantage gap that let a back get away without full mastery of it — his negative worked example is Ronald Jones, dominant on college athleticism alone with "no more tools in the toolbox" once the NFL caught up. Angelo's #2 (paired with Waldman's independently-named #1, oscillating gears): the ability to read leverage in real time — "do you see flashes of color and overreact, or do you see the flashes of color... and know, okay, I can press this deeper, or no, I've got to do something right now." Waldman's explicit mechanism: faster players are the ones who struggle most with this, because a 4.3 runner has far less margin to recover from misreading a leverage tell than a 4.5-4.6 runner who can rely on in-between gears — directly linking gear control to decision-making speed rather than treating them as separate traits. Both flag this leverage-reading skill as also the one most linked to fumbling issues, since misread leverage tends to produce overplayed, exposed-ball contact. Waldman's pad-level addition: a "cultivated," largely instinctive trait — backs who reach the league without already leaning into contact rarely add it later, the standing counter-examples being Travis Etienne and Rachaad White (not tracked pages), who did add it.

**Why this is in the wiki:** it is the film-level explanation for why a group of
non-elite-athlete producers keep outperforming their measurables, and — via the
"take what's blocked" half — a reason to be cautious with backs whose highlight
profile is boom/bust. It is also the trait Waldman uses to separate rookie
receivers, so it feeds [[Dynasty]] rookie evaluation directly.

- 2024-03-28 — [[Brandon Angelo]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-28 (Going Deep - If We Were NFL GMs)]]): restates the framework under the label "problem solver" — how a player dictates, orients, understands and reacts to the moving picture in front of him, and how well he can articulate/predict what's likely to happen and adjust in real time. Applies it to [[Blake Corum]] and [[Caleb Williams]] as the episode's two clearest examples, contrasted with [[Trey Benson]] as a good player who is not a problem solver ("I see managers run" — Benson's own description of his style). [[Matt Waldman]], same episode, extends the framework to [[Jonathan Brooks]]: elite processors "dictate the chain of movements" they want and have a quick counter when something disrupts that chain, rather than purely reacting.

- 2024-04-11 — [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-11 (Going Deep - Fave Evals, Coach-Killer Candidates, and High-End Career Outcomes)]]), applied to [[Jonathan Brooks]]: Waldman names the mechanism **sequential information** — understanding and dictating the sequence of decisions a play creates in real time, rather than reacting to a defender's counter. States some backs simply can't do it ([[DeAndre Swift]], Miles Sanders and [[Marshawn Lloyd]], none dictating their own sequence). Angelo frames the same idea as "attack first" — striking a defender rather than absorbing the hit dictates the movement chain and forces the opponent to react — and both tie it to **down-to-down control**, the trait they say separates elite backs across a 17-game season: [[Christian McCaffrey]] (exceptional; "the best running back in the league" in their view) and [[Nick Chubb]] (fascinatingly good) as the standard, [[Saquon Barkley]] as the cautionary post-ACL comp whose athleticism changed but whose game evolved to stay a top-10 back anyway.
- 2024-05-17 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-17]]): both hosts group [[Trey Benson]] and [[Marshawn Lloyd]] into an [[Isaiah Pacheco]]-style 'see it, hit it' archetype — talented, aggressive runners whose perceptual-cognitive processing hasn't caught up to NFL speed, leading to bounced runs and poor angles as rookies. Waldman notes a useful distinguishing detail: Benson and Lloyd actually played behind competent college blocking (unlike Pacheco, who ran behind a genuinely bad line at times), so their slow-game issues are more purely cognitive and, in his view, more correctable with reps — similar to how David Montgomery's rookie struggles stemmed partly from years of bad Iowa State blocking before he became a reliable NFL runner once he adjusted. Both expect Benson and Lloyd to sit and 'bide their time' behind veteran starters before inheriting bigger roles.
