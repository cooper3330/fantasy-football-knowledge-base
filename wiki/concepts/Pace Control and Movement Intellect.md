# Pace Control and Movement Intellect

## Definition

The cross-positional trait [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] argue
separates elite NFL movers: not top-end speed, but the ability to **control
your own gears** play-to-play — and, paired with it, to **gauge the pace and
angles of everyone else** — and then to take only what the play actually offers.
Angelo's shorthand is "one of the biggest signs of high-level cognitive
athletes."

This is the **sibling** of [[Running Back Size and Movement Skills]], not a
replacement for it. That page holds the *physical* argument (change of direction
inside tight creases, and the ~205–215 lb ceiling Waldman puts on it). This page
holds the *cognitive/tempo* argument, which the same episode applies to
quarterbacks and receivers as well as backs. Read them together.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]]: the framing take. The thread linking [[Lamar Jackson]], [[James Cook]], [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] and [[Christian McCaffrey]] is "how well they control the pace of their own game" — athletes who "control space and time" or "know how to control their own gears on a given play." Jackson is his purest case: "Lamar Jackson never reaches top gear." The negative exemplar is [[Isiah Pacheco]] — "you get zero or a hundred." Defenders struggle because these players are "moving at a different pace on every single play."

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the second half of the trait: taking what is blocked instead of hunting the explosive. "Every play in the NFL is not a score mission." Elite movers know what a given play can yield "based on their own capabilities, based on what's blocked, based on what the defense is," and they also manage *contact type* — high, low, direct, indirect — to negate punishment over a 200-hit season. He credits [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] with the version of this that young players usually lack: when it isn't there, Gibbs protects himself and goes down, "and he's okay doing that." The counter-case is [[Breece Hall]], who Angelo says tries to hit the home run every down. His summary of the payoff: these players "become the engines of their own offense."

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode: pace control is only half of it — "it's not just them controlling their own pace, it's also being able to gauge the pace of others, and it's a marriage between the two." All of the named players "can survey the situation around them and have a great gauge for what defenders are doing within the scope of the play, and how they can leverage their skills — how much or how little." His mechanism is **body awareness plus boundary knowledge**: knowing your own limits well enough to spend exactly the right amount. His analogy is Muhammad Ali on the ropes, and his counter-example is Johnny Manziel, who had the trait "in spades in college" but in the pros needed to become a student of what he could and could not still get away with, and never did.

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the structure argument (a direct challenge to a common framing): "it's constraints versus no constraints, structure versus lack of structure." The received view on [[Lamar Jackson]] is that he is great without structure and poor within it. Angelo's version: "they're so good without structure that they don't need structure — it's not that they're not good in structure." These players are in fact excellent inside the structure of a play precisely "because they understand the bandwidth of what the play can give them." See [[Scheme vs Talent]] for the scheme-side version of this argument.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, extending the trait to receivers: [[Malik Nabers]] "excels at pacing variation" — using pace with his footwork and inside his routes to set defenders up. "The best receivers tell stories well like that." Michael Crabtree is his historical example of a receiver who was a terrific route runner "for his size" specifically because he understood how to use pace. See [[Zone vs Man Route Running]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Angelo**, same episode, the tackling-difficulty inversion this produces: ask an NFL linebacker who is harder to bring down, [[Derrick Henry]] in his prime or [[Christian McCaffrey]] / [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] / [[James Cook]], and "the answer might surprise people." Sheer size is a known quantity; "I have to deal with Christian McCaffrey's feet, I have to deal with the re-acceleration of Jahmyr Gibbs" is a guessing game. **Waldman's** caveat in Henry's favor, same episode: in the fourth quarter, after 15–18 touches against a tired defense, you would rather not face Henry — though he adds that the movers are just as unwelcome late, because "if they're on a roll you're always guessing with them," whereas with Henry "you're not guessing — you're just praying you can hit him low."

**Why this is in the wiki:** it is the film-level explanation for why a group of
non-elite-athlete producers keep outperforming their measurables, and — via the
"take what's blocked" half — a reason to be cautious with backs whose highlight
profile is boom/bust. It is also the trait Waldman uses to separate rookie
receivers, so it feeds [[Dynasty]] rookie evaluation directly.
