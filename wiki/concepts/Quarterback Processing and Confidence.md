# Quarterback Processing and Confidence

## Definition

Matt Waldman's framework for why NFL teams miss on quarterback evaluations so
often: the position is an **improvisational performance** skill, not a purely
intellectual one, and the deciding trait — processing speed under pressure —
is rooted in confidence and intuition rather than study. Teams have
historically overrated the "academic" half of the evaluation (scheme
knowledge, film study, testable intelligence) and are only beginning to
properly weight the half that actually separates outcomes.

## Expert Takes

- 2024-02-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-05]]), answering **Bob Harris**'s (co-host — not a tracked expert) question "why do NFL teams miss on quarterbacks": calls it the most complicated position to evaluate in sports because it is fundamentally a **performance position** — "you're on a stage, you have to interact with your teammates, it's like a band or like other actors — there's an improvisational element to it." It combines technique, scheme concepts and theory with athletic ability *and* intuitive, in-the-moment improvisation, and "there's no real great formula for that." His analogy is Steve Jobs iterating Apple products by feel rather than spec — "I'll know it when I see it" — which he explicitly flags as an unreliable standard for the teams that have to apply it under draft pressure.
- 2024-02-05 — **Waldman**, same episode, the specific mechanism: "the processing part is rooted in confidence and intuition — you have to have that confidence of the moment you see it to let it go." His negative example is Alex Smith, cast as "the academic who saw it and said, well, we need to do some more studies for the next two to three steps before I decide I'm going to let it go — and now it's too late, it's already passed you by." Frames this as an emotional-intelligence skill that the league (and evaluators generally) are still early in learning how to identify, value, and trust — including a pointed aside that "the people in power" making these decisions often lack that same intelligence themselves and are asked to defer to people who have it, which breeds exactly the kind of organizational friction covered in [[Player Development and Coachability]].
- 2024-02-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-13]]), reacting to Super Bowl LVIII: an independently-arrived-at framing of the same idea, applied to [[Patrick Mahomes]] and [[Brock Purdy]] rather than named as Waldman's framework. Mahomes wins now not primarily because he's the most physically gifted quarterback in the league — Harmon says he is, but that's not the differentiator anymore — but because his pre-snap coverage diagnosis has caught up with and now exceeds his physical tools, to the point he "barely ever makes any mistakes" and is no longer "a reckless gunslinger" the way he once profiled. Harmon's explicit prediction for Purdy is the same growth path: physical tools are largely fixed (he "doesn't have the gun" a Jared Goff has), so his path to closing the gap runs through pre-snap/post-snap processing experience accumulating "the longer you play" — and through staying composed under pressure the way Mahomes does and Jimmy Garoppolo did not.

- 2024-02-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-26]]), on which pre-draft quarterback metrics actually predict NFL success: hand size matters up to a minimum threshold (grip on the ball), but the popularly-cited minimums are stricter than necessary — cites [[Jared Goff]] and [[Joe Burrow]] as small-handed passers whose hand size never limited their success. On the S2 cognitive test (the tool credited with flagging C.J. Stroud's rise): calls it a promising idea still being calibrated, and cautions that the same people now championing it because of Stroud were "equally unfairly" burying him pre-draft — the test's early public rollout was too rigid/certain about its own predictive power and needs more time and more open-minded application before it's proven. His own preferred proxy for quarterback success remains charted, catchable/pinpoint accuracy pulled from film — not any combine or lab metric — "you're back to film" regardless. General takeaway: too many divergent successful quarterback archetypes exist (Steve McNair vs. Tom Brady, Drew Brees vs. Josh Allen, [[Jalen Hurts]] vs. [[Kirk Cousins]]) for any single physical or testable metric to be a reliable common denominator. See [[NFL Combine and Pro Day Skepticism]] for the broader combine/pro-day framework this sits inside.

- 2024-03-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-04]]): applies the framework to two situations. On Kenny Pickett: handles anticipated pressure fine, but "short circuits" when pressure arrives by surprise, especially in high-leverage moments — diagnosed as a confidence/recognition issue rather than a physical one, with a possible Kirk-Cousins-type outcome if he corrects it. On [[Bo Nix]]: cites poise and low-mistake decision-making as what separates him from "uber athletic" quarterbacks who try to escape trouble by scrambling — "he knows his lane," throwing the ball away or running rather than forcing a bad decision — and rates it a maturity/processing trait rather than an arm-talent one.

- 2024-03-08 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-08]]), solo cast on the 2024 QB class, restating a longstanding methodology point: completion percentage isn't "contextually sound data" — it doesn't show pass-placement precision relative to the route break or coverage, whether a bad outcome was the receiver's fault, or whether the throw came out with "the appropriate speed of confidence." His RSP charting instead grades every throw's accuracy by situation, arm platform, target distance/zone and coverage type against next-gen-stats-derived thresholds, independent of what the receiver did with the ball. Worked example: Baker Mayfield was overvalued by box-score completion percentage (Oklahoma's scheme let imprecise placement get away with it), while [[Lamar Jackson]] was undervalued by it — charting showed he was more accurate than his numbers suggested once drops by his receiving corps were factored out. "If they could have seen his charting on a regular basis, they wouldn't have even remotely questioned his positional viability." Flags [[Drake Maye]] and [[Michael Penix Jr.]] as a possible repeat of that Mayfield/Jackson dynamic — Maye seen as the pro-ready lock, Penix as a system-QB arm — which he thinks big-media draft evaluation currently has backwards. Restates his own hit/miss track record for context: correctly ahead of the curve on [[Russell Wilson]], [[Patrick Mahomes]], [[Lamar Jackson]] and [[Brock Purdy]]; correctly skeptical of Baker Mayfield, [[Drew Lock]], Mitchell Trubisky and Zach Wilson; and names [[Dak Prescott]] and [[Justin Herbert]] as his two biggest misses of the past 18 years.

- 2024-04-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-11 (Going Deep - Fave Evals, Coach-Killer Candidates, and High-End Career Outcomes)]]): applies the processing framework to [[Jayden Daniels]] — flashes multi-read progressions but is consistently "a half-beat too late" recognizing an open receiver, the same issue Waldman has flagged in [[Dak Prescott]] at Mississippi State, and a component of why Waldman names Daniels a [[Coach Killer Prospects|coach-killer]] candidate alongside [[Drake Maye]] and [[J.J. McCarthy]].
- 2024-05-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-20]]): Waldman contrasted two processing-speed cases. On [[Malik Willis]], he said Willis has always been a slow processor of the middle of the field, with the arm and movement traits but not the accuracy/placement or processing speed to start, capping his outlook at a Tyler Huntley-level backup. On [[Jared Goff]], he argued McVay's heavy in-ear scripting and micromanagement, while it produced early wins, prevented Goff from developing independent processing — leaving him a "deer in headlights" against disguised pressure until Detroit gave him real ownership of the offense and he handled it fine.
- 2024-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-19]]): the failure mode for a young quarterback facing hybrid looks is mechanical, not intellectual. When a passer starts to get confused and has to think about what he is seeing, the baseline skills he owns — footwork above all — slow down and get sloppy, and that ruins the whole game. So the standard is not whether you can show isolated plays with clean feet ([[Drake Maye]]), it is whether the footwork is solidified enough to survive looks that boggle the mind. He extends the same logic to [[Jayden Daniels]]: he does not expect the processing to look faster against complex defenses, so Washington will simplify to one-read and occasional two-read concepts, which can work for a season. Waldman also endorses Phil Perry's observation that Alex Van Pelt's system requires the feet in a specific spot at release with tight timing, a system fit issue distinct from talent.
- 2024-08-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-22]]): the diagnostic for a slow processor is whether a quarterback can pair a known coverage with his own route concepts and anticipate — take one hitch and throw the deep crosser or over route into the vacated zone — rather than turn his back to the defense, spin out and run. He warns that isolating a slow processor's few good second-and-third-read plays fools evaluators; those plays are the thin meat between thick slices of bread that show he does not do it well. He also separates developing IN a system from developing AS an NFL quarterback: an RPO- and first-read-heavy scheme can produce production while hiding the deficiency, which is his objection to [[Jayden Daniels]] in Cliff Kingsbury's offense. The predicted pattern is early success followed by defenses adjusting, as with Marcus Mariota and, in a different way, Robert Griffin III.
- 2024-08-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-26]]): this episode sorted young quarterbacks almost entirely on processing and pocket behavior rather than arm talent. He describes a failure spiral he calls 'the [[Sam Darnold]] formula for beginning to see ghosts' — an inconsistent pocket presence and field reader, paired with young receivers who may not share his read, facing disguised NFL coverages, rushes everything until the game gets beyond him and the team benches him with a face-saving explanation ([[Drake Maye]]). He separates manipulation of defenders from raw speed: [[Kyler Murray]] has excellent vision and knows how to work defenders away, while straight-line runners in the Robert Griffin III / Marcus Mariota mold need wide-open field ([[Jayden Daniels]]). He credits knowing your own limits as a processing skill — throwing it away, getting out of bounds, knowing where to take chances ([[Bo Nix]]). And he argues confidence is recoverable and the market is far too impatient with it ([[Trey Lance]]).
- 2024-08-27 — According to guest Derek Klassen (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-27]]): a recurring evaluative frame across the episode — accuracy charting can flatter a quarterback who never attempts the hard throw. [[Bo Nix]] grades out green because Oregon manufactured space with the college hashes and he never misses gimmes, yet Klassen's concern is the throws he leaves on the table, since taking the next step requires being willing to make a mistake over the intermediate and deep middle. The same lens applied to [[Jayden Daniels]] failing to replace a blitz in preseason, to [[Caleb Williams]] being better on time from true three- and five-step drops than his highlight reel suggests, and to [[Jalen Hurts]] facing full-field one-two-three progressions and post-snap protection duties for the first time under Kellen Moore — where Klassen names [[Justin Herbert]] and [[Dak Prescott]] as the elite pre-snap processors that offense was previously built around.
- 2024-09-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-12]]): the tell he watches for is pocket management under multi-directional pressure. The savvy manager climbs the pocket, keeps his options open on both sides of the field, and efficiently finds the next read without spending time with the ball in his hand; the lesser response — [[Baker Mayfield]] in Cleveland, and [[Kyler Murray]] as the archetype — is to turn your back, break outside and buy time, which collapses the structure and makes it far harder to reset and find anybody. Waldman distinguishes an obvious pocket compromised in one area, where a few hitch steps and a rhythm throw suffice, from one compromised in several. He also recalls his retired RSP 'knockout factor': quarterbacks who could not handle the pocket in specific ways were disqualified regardless of other strengths, because the deficiency makes them a net detriment — [[Derek Carr]] and Jimmy Garoppolo both failed it. Harstad (NOT tracked) adds the parallel that a quarterback who never stresses a defense hurts you not on any single play but through the absent threat.
