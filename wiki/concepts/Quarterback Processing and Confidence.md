# Quarterback Processing and Confidence

## Definition

Matt Waldman's framework for why NFL teams miss on quarterback evaluations so
often: the position is an **improvisational performance** skill, not a purely
intellectual one, and the deciding trait — processing speed under pressure —
is rooted in confidence and intuition rather than study. Teams have
historically overrated the "academic" half of the evaluation (scheme
knowledge, film study, testable intelligence) and are only beginning to
properly weight the half that actually separates outcomes.

## Expert Takes

- 2024-02-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-05]]), answering **Bob Harris**'s (co-host — not a tracked expert) question "why do NFL teams miss on quarterbacks": calls it the most complicated position to evaluate in sports because it is fundamentally a **performance position** — "you're on a stage, you have to interact with your teammates, it's like a band or like other actors — there's an improvisational element to it." It combines technique, scheme concepts and theory with athletic ability *and* intuitive, in-the-moment improvisation, and "there's no real great formula for that." His analogy is Steve Jobs iterating Apple products by feel rather than spec — "I'll know it when I see it" — which he explicitly flags as an unreliable standard for the teams that have to apply it under draft pressure.
- 2024-02-05 — **Waldman**, same episode, the specific mechanism: "the processing part is rooted in confidence and intuition — you have to have that confidence of the moment you see it to let it go." His negative example is Alex Smith, cast as "the academic who saw it and said, well, we need to do some more studies for the next two to three steps before I decide I'm going to let it go — and now it's too late, it's already passed you by." Frames this as an emotional-intelligence skill that the league (and evaluators generally) are still early in learning how to identify, value, and trust — including a pointed aside that "the people in power" making these decisions often lack that same intelligence themselves and are asked to defer to people who have it, which breeds exactly the kind of organizational friction covered in [[Player Development and Coachability]].
- 2024-02-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-13]]), reacting to Super Bowl LVIII: an independently-arrived-at framing of the same idea, applied to [[Patrick Mahomes]] and [[Brock Purdy]] rather than named as Waldman's framework. Mahomes wins now not primarily because he's the most physically gifted quarterback in the league — Harmon says he is, but that's not the differentiator anymore — but because his pre-snap coverage diagnosis has caught up with and now exceeds his physical tools, to the point he "barely ever makes any mistakes" and is no longer "a reckless gunslinger" the way he once profiled. Harmon's explicit prediction for Purdy is the same growth path: physical tools are largely fixed (he "doesn't have the gun" a Jared Goff has), so his path to closing the gap runs through pre-snap/post-snap processing experience accumulating "the longer you play" — and through staying composed under pressure the way Mahomes does and Jimmy Garoppolo did not.

- 2024-02-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-26]]), on which pre-draft quarterback metrics actually predict NFL success: hand size matters up to a minimum threshold (grip on the ball), but the popularly-cited minimums are stricter than necessary — cites [[Jared Goff]] and [[Joe Burrow]] as small-handed passers whose hand size never limited their success. On the S2 cognitive test (the tool credited with flagging C.J. Stroud's rise): calls it a promising idea still being calibrated, and cautions that the same people now championing it because of Stroud were "equally unfairly" burying him pre-draft — the test's early public rollout was too rigid/certain about its own predictive power and needs more time and more open-minded application before it's proven. His own preferred proxy for quarterback success remains charted, catchable/pinpoint accuracy pulled from film — not any combine or lab metric — "you're back to film" regardless. General takeaway: too many divergent successful quarterback archetypes exist (Steve McNair vs. Tom Brady, Drew Brees vs. Josh Allen, [[Jalen Hurts]] vs. [[Kirk Cousins]]) for any single physical or testable metric to be a reliable common denominator. See [[NFL Combine and Pro Day Skepticism]] for the broader combine/pro-day framework this sits inside.

- 2024-03-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-04]]): applies the framework to two situations. On Kenny Pickett: handles anticipated pressure fine, but "short circuits" when pressure arrives by surprise, especially in high-leverage moments — diagnosed as a confidence/recognition issue rather than a physical one, with a possible Kirk-Cousins-type outcome if he corrects it. On [[Bo Nix]]: cites poise and low-mistake decision-making as what separates him from "uber athletic" quarterbacks who try to escape trouble by scrambling — "he knows his lane," throwing the ball away or running rather than forcing a bad decision — and rates it a maturity/processing trait rather than an arm-talent one.

- 2024-03-08 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-08]]), solo cast on the 2024 QB class, restating a longstanding methodology point: completion percentage isn't "contextually sound data" — it doesn't show pass-placement precision relative to the route break or coverage, whether a bad outcome was the receiver's fault, or whether the throw came out with "the appropriate speed of confidence." His RSP charting instead grades every throw's accuracy by situation, arm platform, target distance/zone and coverage type against next-gen-stats-derived thresholds, independent of what the receiver did with the ball. Worked example: Baker Mayfield was overvalued by box-score completion percentage (Oklahoma's scheme let imprecise placement get away with it), while [[Lamar Jackson]] was undervalued by it — charting showed he was more accurate than his numbers suggested once drops by his receiving corps were factored out. "If they could have seen his charting on a regular basis, they wouldn't have even remotely questioned his positional viability." Flags [[Drake Maye]] and [[Michael Penix Jr.]] as a possible repeat of that Mayfield/Jackson dynamic — Maye seen as the pro-ready lock, Penix as a system-QB arm — which he thinks big-media draft evaluation currently has backwards. Restates his own hit/miss track record for context: correctly ahead of the curve on [[Russell Wilson]], [[Patrick Mahomes]], [[Lamar Jackson]] and [[Brock Purdy]]; correctly skeptical of Baker Mayfield, [[Drew Lock]], Mitchell Trubisky and Zach Wilson; and names [[Dak Prescott]] and [[Justin Herbert]] as his two biggest misses of the past 18 years.

- 2024-04-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-04-11 (Going Deep - Fave Evals, Coach-Killer Candidates, and High-End Career Outcomes)]]): applies the processing framework to [[Jayden Daniels]] — flashes multi-read progressions but is consistently "a half-beat too late" recognizing an open receiver, the same issue Waldman has flagged in [[Dak Prescott]] at Mississippi State, and a component of why Waldman names Daniels a [[Coach Killer Prospects|coach-killer]] candidate alongside [[Drake Maye]] and [[J.J. McCarthy]].
- 2024-05-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-20]]): Waldman contrasted two processing-speed cases. On [[Malik Willis]], he said Willis has always been a slow processor of the middle of the field, with the arm and movement traits but not the accuracy/placement or processing speed to start, capping his outlook at a Tyler Huntley-level backup. On [[Jared Goff]], he argued McVay's heavy in-ear scripting and micromanagement, while it produced early wins, prevented Goff from developing independent processing — leaving him a "deer in headlights" against disguised pressure until Detroit gave him real ownership of the offense and he handled it fine.
- 2024-08-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-19]]): the failure mode for a young quarterback facing hybrid looks is mechanical, not intellectual. When a passer starts to get confused and has to think about what he is seeing, the baseline skills he owns — footwork above all — slow down and get sloppy, and that ruins the whole game. So the standard is not whether you can show isolated plays with clean feet ([[Drake Maye]]), it is whether the footwork is solidified enough to survive looks that boggle the mind. He extends the same logic to [[Jayden Daniels]]: he does not expect the processing to look faster against complex defenses, so Washington will simplify to one-read and occasional two-read concepts, which can work for a season. Waldman also endorses Phil Perry's observation that Alex Van Pelt's system requires the feet in a specific spot at release with tight timing, a system fit issue distinct from talent.
- 2024-08-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-22]]): the diagnostic for a slow processor is whether a quarterback can pair a known coverage with his own route concepts and anticipate — take one hitch and throw the deep crosser or over route into the vacated zone — rather than turn his back to the defense, spin out and run. He warns that isolating a slow processor's few good second-and-third-read plays fools evaluators; those plays are the thin meat between thick slices of bread that show he does not do it well. He also separates developing IN a system from developing AS an NFL quarterback: an RPO- and first-read-heavy scheme can produce production while hiding the deficiency, which is his objection to [[Jayden Daniels]] in Cliff Kingsbury's offense. The predicted pattern is early success followed by defenses adjusting, as with Marcus Mariota and, in a different way, Robert Griffin III.
- 2024-08-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-08-26]]): this episode sorted young quarterbacks almost entirely on processing and pocket behavior rather than arm talent. He describes a failure spiral he calls 'the [[Sam Darnold]] formula for beginning to see ghosts' — an inconsistent pocket presence and field reader, paired with young receivers who may not share his read, facing disguised NFL coverages, rushes everything until the game gets beyond him and the team benches him with a face-saving explanation ([[Drake Maye]]). He separates manipulation of defenders from raw speed: [[Kyler Murray]] has excellent vision and knows how to work defenders away, while straight-line runners in the Robert Griffin III / Marcus Mariota mold need wide-open field ([[Jayden Daniels]]). He credits knowing your own limits as a processing skill — throwing it away, getting out of bounds, knowing where to take chances ([[Bo Nix]]). And he argues confidence is recoverable and the market is far too impatient with it ([[Trey Lance]]).
- 2024-08-27 — According to guest Derek Klassen (not a tracked expert) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-27]]): a recurring evaluative frame across the episode — accuracy charting can flatter a quarterback who never attempts the hard throw. [[Bo Nix]] grades out green because Oregon manufactured space with the college hashes and he never misses gimmes, yet Klassen's concern is the throws he leaves on the table, since taking the next step requires being willing to make a mistake over the intermediate and deep middle. The same lens applied to [[Jayden Daniels]] failing to replace a blitz in preseason, to [[Caleb Williams]] being better on time from true three- and five-step drops than his highlight reel suggests, and to [[Jalen Hurts]] facing full-field one-two-three progressions and post-snap protection duties for the first time under Kellen Moore — where Klassen names [[Justin Herbert]] and [[Dak Prescott]] as the elite pre-snap processors that offense was previously built around.
- 2024-09-12 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-12]]): the tell he watches for is pocket management under multi-directional pressure. The savvy manager climbs the pocket, keeps his options open on both sides of the field, and efficiently finds the next read without spending time with the ball in his hand; the lesser response — [[Baker Mayfield]] in Cleveland, and [[Kyler Murray]] as the archetype — is to turn your back, break outside and buy time, which collapses the structure and makes it far harder to reset and find anybody. Waldman distinguishes an obvious pocket compromised in one area, where a few hitch steps and a rhythm throw suffice, from one compromised in several. He also recalls his retired RSP 'knockout factor': quarterbacks who could not handle the pocket in specific ways were disqualified regardless of other strengths, because the deficiency makes them a net detriment — [[Derek Carr]] and Jimmy Garoppolo both failed it. Harstad (NOT tracked) adds the parallel that a quarterback who never stresses a defense hurts you not on any single play but through the absent threat.
- 2024-09-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-17]]): the [[Bryce Young]] benching is a case study in a quarterback whose failure is decision-latency rather than tools. Co-host James Koh (not tracked) argues Young can still make the reads and the throws but physically cannot pull the trigger when he sees it, comparing it to early-career [[Tua Tagovailoa]], and says receivers giving up on him traces directly to that hesitation — 'bro, we're open, just throw the ball.' Harmon reframes it as a compounding bet: because Young's physical profile is marginal, he would have had to be perfect between the ears, pre-snap and post-snap, perfectly confident and daring, just to be functional — so drafting him at 1.1 was betting on perfection from every small thing at once. Harmon applies the same lens to [[Caleb Williams]], a far more gifted passer who he says is not yet ready to carry the mental load in his second NFL game.
- 2024-09-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-19]]): the Fran Tarkenton segment produced a framework for grading improvisational quarterbacks — the question is not whether a passer can create off-script but whether he can also stay in the design. Co-host Adam Harstad (not a tracked expert) argued Tarkenton could do everything [[Patrick Mahomes]] does physically but essentially never operated in structure, drove coaches out of patience and holds the all-time records for sacks taken and sack yardage lost, at roughly 10 yards per sack, putting his offenses in holes they could not escape; Mahomes' rarity is having the same creativity without depending on it. Waldman raised the counterpoint that much of the friction was era-specific: Norm Van Brocklin, Tarkenton's first head coach, actively coached the quarterback to run the show, in an era when Johnny Unitas said you had not arrived until you could tell your coach to go to hell. Both agreed a Dungy, Carroll, Harbaugh or Tomlin would accommodate that player today while heavy West Coast-influence micromanagers like Sean McVay would not. Waldman also credited Tarkenton's layered throws between defenders, opposite-hash strikes on the move at 50-55 yards and back-shoulder throws now credited to Aaron Rodgers.
- 2024-09-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-20]]): 'playing too fast' is a bandwidth problem, not an intelligence or talent problem. He framed quarterbacking as a performance craft like stand-up, acting or music, and used his own experience learning a Miles Davis solo from *Kind of Blue* on bass — everything felt faster than the music actually was until the notes were under his fingers, at which point the tempo felt slow enough that his dog or his wife walking in no longer disrupted him. That is the game slowing down. A rookie QB knows his three- and five-step drops, play-action looks, protection checks and adjustments on the blackboard, but year one loads him with a bigger playbook, faster and more complex defenders, plus appearances, sponsorships, interviews, a move and new financial responsibilities. When a disguised coverage or a cheating safety consumes the remaining bandwidth, things he already had down unravel — which is why development is not linear. Applied to [[Bryce Young]] (frenetic, overwhelmed), [[Anthony Richardson]] (advanced audible one year, staredown interception the next) and the case for benching a struggling passer to let him reset.
- 2024-09-26 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-26 (Win-Now Trades and Jayden Daniels)]]): Waldman laid out his working distinction between progression reads and non-progression 'scheme plays'. A progression read gives the quarterback three to five options in order — or, in a non-pure system, lets him read coverage and choose where to start — and demands he manipulate the defense as a field general through one to four reads. A non-progression play (RPO, screen, throwback off misdirection, rollout to one side) gives him at best two receivers and usually one, sometimes only a look-off used as window dressing to set up a predetermined target. His point: on those plays the burden of getting a receiver open sits with the scheme and the other ten players, not the quarterback, so volume of them tells you what a staff actually trusts a passer to do regardless of what coaches and teammates say publicly. He applied it to [[Jayden Daniels]] and used it to separate him from the [[Brock Purdy]] / [[Jared Goff]] / [[Matthew Stafford]] tier of read-makers.
- 2024-10-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-14]]): Waldman argues that many film analysts evaluate quarterback reads on coverage type and defender location while missing leverage — the defender's actual body position. Using [[Brock Purdy]]'s 76-yard touchdown to [[Deebo Samuel]], criticized elsewhere as a hanging ball a safety nearly cut off, he shows Purdy manipulating the safety with his drop and a hip flip to sell an inside breaking route, then throwing off his back foot knowing the deep defender was effectively three steps behind. The same lens applies to [[Jayden Daniels]], where Waldman warns that many look-offs credited to the quarterback are embedded in the play design, and that Daniels's real next step is throwing accurately with interior pressure high in the pocket.
- 2024-10-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-23]]): how a quarterback responds to a mistake is a durable evaluation input, and pocket-passer improvisation is not the same thing as scrambling — for a pocket passer it looks like stepping up and finding the second or third read, whereas [[Anthony Richardson]] or [[Lamar Jackson]] improvise with their legs. His examples of the good version: [[Matthew Stafford]] and [[Carson Beck]] throw an interception and keep gunning it downfield as if it never happened, never becoming 'check down Charlie'; [[Lamar Jackson]] shook off mistakes without spiralling. The bad version: [[Jordan Love]] used to get more erratic after an error, doubling down, and [[Zach Wilson]] would go blind to what he was doing. Co-host Felix Sharpe (not tracked), a former defensive back, adds that there is a gap between the media narrative and what defenders actually read on the field.
- 2024-11-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-12]]): letting it rip requires confidence both in what you are doing and in what is around you, and [[Caleb Williams]] plays like a man who has neither — the same throw-it-into-coverage gunslinger from USC now will not squeeze the football. Harmon ties the same mechanism to [[Daniel Jones]], functional only when the environment is perfect and unable to adjust once the left tackle went down. Koh (not a tracked expert) supplies the inverse case in [[Bryce Young]], who plays freely now because the worst has already happened and he is no longer afraid of being benched, and warns that [[Anthony Richardson]] being hammered on social media every time he logs on is a genuine confidence killer for a young quarterback. The pattern both hosts draw: quarterback play is not purely mechanical, and organizational handling moves it in both directions.
- 2024-11-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-14]]): Waldman distinguishes mechanical development from team-side development — scripting, game-planning and role clarity that let a young quarterback make clear reads, ferret out disguises and find flow. When veterans have simple well-defined roles that suit their talents, as with [[Brock Purdy]] in San Francisco, the quarterback only has to act out decisions; when linemen miss stunts and receivers run routes wrong, he starts playing what-if and slows down. Waldman likens play-calling to storytelling: repetition builds rhythm and momentum, then you pull the switch — and returning to concepts a struggling quarterback sees well is how you rebuild confidence. Angelo's parallel frame is 'time and timing': without time to throw, timing, spacing and alignment all break, so you cannot evaluate the quarterback at all — his argument for patience with [[Caleb Williams]] and his explanation of [[C.J. Stroud]]'s year-two dip.
- 2024-12-12 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-12]]): Angelo frames quarterback development as building an 'inventory of answers' for situations that arise in a game — that inventory does not appear on day one, it expands with reps, and crucially 'there's an inventory of answers you can only get by watching.' That is why [[Bryce Young]], [[Anthony Richardson]] and [[Will Levis]] all improved after a benching or injury: discovering the problem was cognitive rather than physical is 'almost more comforting', and veterans like [[Andy Dalton]] and [[Joe Flacco]] model answers without superior traits. Waldman adds the pressure-release angle: a benching means 'the thing I was most afraid of already happened', which lets a player stop overthinking and stop trying to be the pinnacle of his own athletic ceiling on every snap. Both stress the position's baseline is distributing like a point guard; the extraordinary is for a handful of high-leverage moments.
- 2024-12-17 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-17]]): most modern offenses are not really teaching the quarterback position anymore — a point he attributes to conversations with Kurt Warner. Older quarterbacks like Peyton Manning and [[Aaron Rodgers]] worked from a built-in catalog: get to the line, identify two-high or man, check into the right beater, pick on a matchup. Today's offenses run pure progressions — one, two, three, irrespective of the coverage — with the motion man often existing to open the true first read. Harmon argues heavy motion is a tradeoff: it manufactures advantages but removes the time to check plays, which is why Rodgers objected to Matt LaFleur's volume. He adds a general principle he is grappling with league-wide: the throws a quarterback declines to take matter more than the ones he makes. James Koh (not a tracked expert) framed Miami as a natural experiment — same coach, quarterback, players and system, wildly different results.
- 2025-02-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-03]]): the symptoms-versus-cause problem in quarterback development. Using [[Will Levis]] and his offseason work with private coach Jordan Palmer, Waldman argued that mechanics and pocket-presence training treats symptoms, while the real limitation is processing information well enough to be dynamic and fluid conceptually. He added an incentive layer: a private coach will never publicly say a paying client's problem is conceptual, both because the client funds his marketing and because that verdict is a death knell with NFL teams — nobody knows how to fix it. Waldman extended this to why drafting quarterbacks is so hard at all: teams cannot reliably evaluate processing and cannot fix it, so they work the physical and technical symptoms and hope it all clicks. He made a related point on [[Jalen Hurts]], whose habit of holding the ball under disruption dates to college and worsens the more invested he gets in a play.
- 2025-03-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-31]]): confidence is a trackable trait, not an intangible, once defined as the confidence to close the gap between identification and action. Identification is the whiteboard — recall of plays and coverages — and teams praise top prospects annually for talking about the game like their coaches, but none of that matters on the field if the passer waits for further confirmation of what he already sees rather than acting on it. Waldman names [[Kenny Pickett]], [[Desmond Ridder]] and, going back further, Alex Smith as passers who saw the leverage but patted the ball or took an extra hitch or two with their feet already in position, so the ball came out late, the pocket collapsed, and a golden opportunity became a sack, throwaway or check-down. The contrast is Brett Favre, [[Joe Burrow]], [[Brock Purdy]], [[Patrick Mahomes]] and Tom Brady — the moment they see it, the ball is out. He calls undervaluing confidence the single biggest miss teams make in quarterback evaluation, and applies the lens in this class to [[Dillon Gabriel]] (will not pull the trigger on favorable zone windows) and [[Tyler Shough]] (a step or two late on a decision he identified on time).
- 2025-04-11 — According to guest Cory Kinnan of Daft on Draft (not a tracked expert), on [[Matt Harmon]]'s show ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-04-11]]): deep-ball accuracy is far more anticipation and receiver ball-tracking than arm talent — every quarterback can throw it 50 to 60 yards, so arm strength really shows up driving the ball into intermediate tight windows. He credits Ohio State's receivers, coached by Brian Hartline to throttle down and stay stacked with their back to the defender, for inflating [[Will Howard]]'s 76.9% nine-route success rate, and notes how easy it is to diagnose one-on-one coverage on [[Jeremiah Smith]] pre-snap. Kinnan applies the same lens to [[Brock Purdy]], who spot-throws roughly half his attempts on pre-snap understanding, versus Jimmy Garoppolo, and to [[Quinn Ewers]], who checks down while a designed clear-out opens.
- 2025-11-11 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and co-host James Koh (not tracked) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-11-11]]): the hosts use [[Tyler Shough]] and [[J.J. McCarthy]] as a paired case study in what processing does to receiver production. Koh's framing is that when a receiver is open and expecting the ball at a moment and the throw comes a beat late — even if he is still nominally open — the rhythm of the play breaks and it shows up as drops or recovered coverage. Shough is credited with on-time, in-rhythm delivery so the ball was where receivers expected it; McCarthy is described as sped up and late at once, ripping every ball at maximum velocity, possibly to compensate for missing the read. Harmon connects it directly to catchable target rate as the downstream measurable, citing 100% for [[Juwan Johnson]] and 63% for [[Chris Olave]] against 42% for [[Justin Jefferson]] — while cautioning that catchable is a subjective charting stat.
