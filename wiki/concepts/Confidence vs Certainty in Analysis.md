## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31 (Tapping Out, Archetypes and Modeling)]]): co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) drew the line between being confident and being certain, using his refusal to sell [[Drake London]] for [[Zay Flowers]] as the case study — he was about 55% on London and about 40-45% on Flowers, but 100% sure that the process leading you to swap after one zero-catch game was bad. In [[Dynasty]], he said, a 55-45 edge is massive and 60-40 gargantuan; stacking 55-45 edges wins leagues, and they are rare enough that giving one away on an emotional reaction is malpractice. He separates confidence in the process from confidence in any individual edge: he can be 90% confident in a process that only produces 52-55% edges. He also conceded the communication problem is his — social media strips nuance, and 'I prefer London' reads as 'Flowers will be a trivia answer'. Waldman said he gets the same 'you think you know everything' feedback and answers it the same way: the process that produced Marshawn Lynch near [[Adrian Peterson]] level, [[Nick Chubb]] over [[Saquon Barkley]], [[Lamar Jackson]] as one of the class's best pocket passers and [[Patrick Mahomes]] is the same one that produced [[Skylar Thompson]] and [[Trey Sermon]] — confidence in the method, not in each outcome.
- 2024-11-07 — According to co-host Adam Harstad (not a tracked expert) and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-07]]): in a closing discussion of disagreement, Harstad laid out what he called the standard model — you first assume someone who disagrees is ignorant (they lack information you have), then that they are an idiot (they have it but cannot reason to your conclusion), then that they are evil (they can reach it and refuse). Most stop at evil, which is comfortable because it lets you write anyone off, but the reality is that people frequently disagree in good faith with the same information and the same understanding, and land elsewhere because of differing value trade-offs. Harstad added that human minds did not evolve to be right individually — they evolve arguments for their position, and truth-seeking is a group process where the best arguments win out. Waldman's parallel point for analysts and public figures: lead with a positive case rather than fear or shame, and when things go badly look first at what was inside your own control. Both also flagged that every organization's incentive is to exaggerate the magnitude of any given outcome.
- 2025-02-17 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-17]]): when draft-media talking heads say a quarterback 'needs more development', Waldman reads that as code for 'I don't know how to value this guy' — an analyst waiting for GM friends and sources to tell them what to think, then repeating it as mimicry. His counter-argument is practical: the player is already working on footwork and mechanics on the side all week while the team teaches scheme, so 'needs development' describes the evaluator's uncertainty rather than the player's deficiency. He raised it in defense of [[Anthony Richardson]].
- 2025-08-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-11]]): a long segment on whether quarterback-turned-analysts are the definitive authority on film study — both hosts say no. Guest Dave Kluge (Footballguys, not tracked) argues film study is deeply subjective: reaching a declaration about one play requires roughly a dozen assumptions, and audiences mistake those assumptions for fact. On the [[Anthony Richardson]] preseason sack, he notes we assume the line slid left, assume both the tight end and back had hot-route assignments (when with two hots one usually chips), and only the linemen, back, tight end, quarterback and Shane Steichen actually know. Waldman praises Kurt Warner, Dan Orlovsky and Chase Daniel for educating fans but says they are coached by producers to present a point of view as authority, and holds up JT O'Sullivan's habit of saying 'we don't know exactly what the play call was' as the marker of nuanced analysis. He cites Jason Brown of Last Chance U, a former quarterback and quarterback coach, saying that none of those analysts has coached the position and that coaching differs everywhere; Brown's own read was that the safety sitting 12 yards off the wide left side told you pre-snap where the ball should go. Waldman adds that playing the position does not confer definitive understanding — it confers a valuable but incomplete perspective — and notes some of these analysts quietly took scouting-academy film classes.
- 2025-08-26 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-26]]): the entire episode is an annual exercise in naming the takes you hold but trust least. Harris frames it as refusing to be a tout — 'we are predicting sports and if that were easy those casinos in Vegas wouldn't be so big' — and says the value is hearing analysts not proclaim themselves NFL knowers beyond reproach; he cites being wrong on [[Malik Nabers]] at ADP on last year's edition. Guest Ben Gretch (Stealing Signals, NOT tracked) frames his whole newsletter around the phrase 'the NFL is chaos': teams guard plans like state secrets, beat writers are barred from reporting formational detail, and eleven-on-eleven football means an offensive line breakdown can decide a skill player's day. Gretch's method point is that the useful discipline is not listing the players you love, but checking whether you have considered the counter positions on the players you are most out on and most in on. Both note last year's list produced real misses (Gretch on [[Alvin Kamara]]) and real hits. [Redraft (Standard)]
