# Quarterback Game Management and Bandwidth

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-11-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-18]]): Waldman's framework for why unspectacular young quarterbacks succeed. Game management — throwing the ball away when a read is not there, protecting the ball in the red zone and inside your own 20 — is not a consolation trait but a compounding one: the better you manage the game, the more possessions your offense gets, and the fewer mistakes you make, the less mental bandwidth is consumed second-guessing what went wrong. That freed bandwidth is what lets a young quarterback experiment and make adjustments without being overwhelmed. He argued the trait is systematically undervalued because evaluators glossed it as something players 'grow into' with experience, and because many scouts come from the former-quarterback circle and evaluate mechanically. Applied to [[Bo Nix]] (does not make mistakes, so Denver keeps getting chances and he keeps learning) and to [[Brock Purdy]] (a good offense meant his teammates were not adding bandwidth), and inverted for [[Anthony Richardson]], whose receivers' drops and the pressure in his lap add exactly that unnecessary load.
- 2025-02-27 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-27]]): both push back on the shorthand that a struggling rookie 'isn't smart enough' or cannot process — Waldman says most of them read coverage fine, and what actually overwhelms them is the preparatory life of the job. A rookie is simultaneously learning a far bigger playbook, a new training regime, a new city and the demands of a big contract, and every one of those is a bandwidth tax on sitting and watching tape. Angelo describes the day as everything from waking to sleeping revolving around the team and next week's preparation. Waldman relays that Chiefs scouts and assistants treated Friday scout-team sessions with [[Patrick Mahomes]] as the event of the week while Alex Smith started, and that the year's value was watching how he prepared and handled mistakes. He adds the environmental variable — confidence is paramount, and a coach who berates a quarterback daily (he names Brian Flores as the archetype) can sink one. Waldman draws the parallel to his own career: ten years editing at a magazine did not teach him to write, it taught him how to operate in that environment.
- 2025-03-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-31]]): the black zone (backed up inside a team's own 20) and the red zone are where Waldman separates this quarterback class, because compression magnifies the gravity of every mistake. [[Cam Ward]] will throw the ball away everywhere on the field except the red zone, where his peripheral awareness lapses. [[Quinn Ewers]] hunts an extra read inside his own 20 after already beating one point of pressure with that side's routes exhausted, when the throwaway is the correct answer. [[Shedeur Sanders]] takes leverage-losing intermediate risks in his own red zone and should be far more conservative in the black zone early in games. [[Dillon Gabriel]] takes black-zone sacks because he bolts rather than climbs. On the other side, [[Jackson Dart]] earns credit for eating the ball in the red zone on a scripted play without a post-snap numbers advantage to preserve field goal position, and [[Kurtis Rourke]] for a pocket clock that gets him to the check-down on the move or to a throwaway after two or three reads.
- 2025-10-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-20]]): Waldman argued that if you could measure how often a quarterback gets an offense into the right play, out of the wrong one, and pointed in the right direction, [[Joe Flacco]] would grade out much closer to [[Joe Burrow]] than his traditional stats suggest -- a dimension he thinks 'metrics heads' undervalue relative to arm talent and box-score production, and part of why he considered a Bengals in-season trade for an experienced game manager smarter than betting on an inexperienced rookie to get up to speed as fast.
- 2026-02-13 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-02-13]]): the top non-physical trait they look for in quarterback prospects is game management -- reading down-to-down what a game plan actually requires (sometimes a run-heavy, mundane workload rather than 350 yards and 2 touchdowns) and not needing to be 'Superman' every play. Both flagged the 'black zone' (backed-up-in-your-own-territory situations) as where young quarterbacks fail most -- spinning away from an open checkdown to try to extend a play, taking unnecessary sacks or throwing into coverage instead of living to fight another down. Waldman cited Ty Simpson's game-management lapses undercutting otherwise good tape, and noted even [[Fernando Mendoza]]'s one real weakness is trying to be a hero in situations he shouldn't. They cited [[Josh Allen]], [[Will Levis]], [[Trey Lance]] and [[Zach Wilson]] as proof that elite physical tools fail without this skill, while [[Caleb Williams]] was cited as a quarterback who has learned to pick his spots for improbable plays rather than force them every down.
