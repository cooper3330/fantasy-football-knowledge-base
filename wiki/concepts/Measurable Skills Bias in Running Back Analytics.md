# Measurable Skills Bias in Running Back Analytics

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-11-06 — According to Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-11-06 (Players We Think We Know)]]): the early analytics movement could only confidently quantify tackle breaking — not reading leverage, not shifty movement in the hole — so backs whose best skill was running through contact, [[Javonte Williams]] and [[DeAndre Swift]], were briefly valued as top-three dynasty assets for excelling at the one measurable thing. Waldman extends the critique to the measurement itself: tracking services credit [[Saquon Barkley]] with 70 yards after contact when a defender grazed his thigh pad, while [[Nick Chubb]] spinning off a head-on hit from a downhill blitzer and dragging an end another three yards reads as almost nothing. He breaks tackling down to reaches, wraps and hits, and cited a former Penn State linebacker who teaches tackling with a 29-point KPI list, who watched a celebrated Sonny Michel highlight and pointed out the safety was already on his knees with no leverage or forward momentum. Waldman applies the same argument to charted drops, which punish the receiver who lays out for a bad throw and ignore the one who never extends.
- 2026-03-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-03-19]]): Relative Athletic Score (RAS) is a useful conversation starter and a real layer of information, but it measures players against each other historically rather than against the minimum threshold actually needed to play the position -- it's a percentile, not a pass/fail bar. He cited Devin Singletary (short, slow, poor accelerator, but great vision/decision-making/quickness) and Dalvin Cook (poor vertical jump and three-cone numbers for a starter, but elite bend and contact avoidance) as backs whose value came from compensatory skills RAS doesn't capture, and pointed to David Montgomery -- on his third NFL contract despite modest testing -- as further proof thresholds must scale by size and role rather than apply as flat cutoffs. Waldman's own rule of thumb is roughly a 4.59 40 as his floor for an average-size back to grade as a viable NFL starter, while larger backs like [[Malachi Fields]] (4.61 at 6'4'/218) need a size-adjusted bar entirely. He also warned that AI/analytics-driven scouting risks formalizing these flawed flat thresholds -- 'garbage in, garbage out' -- as teams adopt them more broadly.
- 2026-06-25 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-06-25]]): explosive-run-rate as a metric doesn't account for caliber of the opposing defense or the runner's own offensive line, so it's 'too noisy' to use in isolation -- illustrated by [[DeMond Claiborne]] grading worse in college explosive-run-rate than [[James Cook]] despite comparable or better raw speed/explosion. Waldman said he doesn't look at explosive run rate at all when scouting, preferring to separate a runner's conceptual, technical and athletic skills from what the offensive line created, citing [[Ahmad Bradshaw]], Joseph Addai and [[Matt Forte|Bet Forte]] as past examples where that separation proved out regardless of whether the box-score run went for 200 yards or two.
- 2026-08-17 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Harris Football Podcast - 2026-08-17]]): Waldman says [[Emmitt Johnson]]'s NFL outlook hinges on whether his team 'gets past their 40-ism' and actually watches the tape -- Johnson's quickness, footwork, and contact balance grade out better on film than his measurables suggest, and Waldman cites this as a recurring pattern of teams and evaluators overruling scouts based on workout numbers rather than what shows up in games.
