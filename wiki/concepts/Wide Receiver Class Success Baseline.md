## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-03-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-24]]): the reasonable expectation for any receiver class is roughly 4.5 players who deliver at least three years of starter production, beginning within their first four seasons in the league, out of the 25-30 receivers drafted each year. A successful NFL receiver career with starter production spans about three and a half to six years, and most receivers with multiple starter seasons rarely reach top-24 or top-12 production, averaging less than one year of that caliber in a career. Waldman's management conclusion, for real and fantasy rosters alike: because few receivers deliver multiple consecutive years of starter production, you should be adding receivers every single year to keep the influx coming. He also cautions that talent is only one of many drivers of opportunity, and that fans wrongly credit or blame talent alone for hits and misses. Applied to 2025, this is why he rejects the 'bad class' label — it may lack star power, but he expects four to five players to hit that starter-production bar.
- 2025-03-24 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-24 (Feel It or Not with Bob Harris)]]) [Dynasty]: whether 2025 is a bad receiver class depends entirely on the criterion. Waldman says if the test is having four or five primary receivers — guys who beat man coverage on a timing route on third-and-15 when everyone in the stadium knows the ball is going to them — then yes, it is bad, because few if any of those exist here. But if the test is producing a batch of WR2/WR3/WR4 types with long careers, it is a pretty good class. He suggests these prospects are not [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] or [[Malik Nabers]], but could yield mid-range [[Tee Higgins]], [[Mike Williams]] or early-career [[Chris Godwin]] outcomes. His strategic conclusions for dynasty: after the first five receivers there is value in round one to one-and-a-half; alternatively trade back for 2026-27 picks; or stockpile receiver depth and trade it for the running back you actually want, since you keep building value at one position either way.
- 2025-04-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-03]]) [Dynasty]: he expects the 2025 class to be a disappointing fantasy draft relative to its talent, and framed the receiver group as thinner than 2022 or 2023 beyond the first five or six — a large group of prospects who have developed many tools in the toolbox but are not masters of any of them, where past classes had stars with three tools used better than these players use eight. [[Brandon Angelo]] agreed the landscape will push real third-round talents into the sixth. Waldman's strategic conclusion, against his own usual preference for investing in receivers and quarterbacks: if you need only one or two running backs, pick your spots early at tight end and receiver and then load up on backs from round two or three onward, or trade out of 2026-2027 picks for higher current picks and stockpile.
- 2025-12-18 — According to Matt Harmon and James Koh ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-12-18]]): revisiting the star-studded 2024 WR class in year two, the hosts noted this group is on pace to produce zero 1,000-yard receivers this season -- a notable step back in the aggregate box score -- and attributed it primarily to widespread injuries across the first round ([[Malik Nabers]] ACL, [[Rome Odunze]] foot fracture, [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] wrist/shoulder/ankle, [[Xavier Worthy]] shoulder, [[Ricky Pearsall]] recurring issues) rather than a talent-evaluation miss. Both hosts stressed they retain real optimism for [[Malik Nabers]], [[Ladd McConkey]], [[Rome Odunze]], [[Brian Thomas Jr.]], [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] and [[Ricky Pearsall]] heading into year three, while noting the class has been much thinner beyond the top tier -- the second and third rounds in particular produced few hits outside [[Jalen McMillan]], with [[Jermaine Burton]], [[Roman Wilson]] and [[Ja'Lynn Polk]] cited as clear misses so far, and [[Jalen Coker]] (undrafted) outproducing most drafted second/third-rounders.
- 2026-03-28 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-03-28]]): From his own sample study, Waldman expects any given draft class to yield roughly four to five receivers who deliver at least one season of starter-level statistical production within their first four years, and four to six who sustain starter production across a five-to-12-year career (these groups often overlap, not add) -- most only hit that peak once, not annually. Judged purely on potential, he rates the 2026 class similarly to a strong 2025 class and pushes back on takes calling it a step down, arguing the '2026 is worse' narrative was already known and is now being overstated by pundits piling on late. He does think it lacks the NFL's preferred volume of true top-50 prototypes, but rates 55-60 of the 65 scouted as draftable overall.
- 2026-04-07 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2026-04-07]]): on the 2026 WR class overall, Waldman compares it to 2025 -- less top-heavy (only two or three true 'start immediately as the guy' talents versus the deeper prior class) but still strong: 38 prospects earned a score he'd consider NFL-roster-worthy, and roughly 17 have a real shot at meaningful bench-to-starter contribution. He credits improved private/college WR coaching for raising the technical floor across the league (using Chris Conley as the historical bar prospects now commonly clear) but says the bottleneck has shifted to 'artfulness' -- knowing techniques versus deploying them with proper pacing and timing based on coverage, like a music-school grad who knows scales but can't yet play compellingly with a band. Matt Harmon agreed and added a structural concern: the position is being squeezed, since so many recent classes were also deep and the league isn't aging veteran WRs out fast enough to create room, so even good prospects may never get an opportunity to prove it.
