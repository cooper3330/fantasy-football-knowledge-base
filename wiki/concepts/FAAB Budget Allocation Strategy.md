## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-09-02 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-02]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: asked whether spending the majority of your FAAB in the first month is the best strategy, Waldman said it used to be — when a Larry Johnson sitting behind Priest Holmes could be had, you blew the budget. It no longer is, because managers are too savvy and information moves too fast for one waiver add to swing a league. His rule of thumb is to cap any single player at no more than fifty percent of the budget, since accumulating two or three players who give strong performances at lower cost beats spending it all on one and then depending on first-come-first-served for the rest of the year. Both hosts qualified it by manager type: Waldman said all-in early bidding makes sense if you are hyper-active and an active trader, citing Bob Harris' SiriusXM co-host Mike Dempsey, who will spend every penny in Week 1 on a hunch and occasionally lands a [[Kyren Williams]]. Harris (untracked) described himself as a resource hoarder who bids judiciously early to keep a hammer for late-season risers. Waldman also noted first-come-first-served waivers are your best friend where the league allows them, and that large rosters are the argument for stashing lottery tickets you would rather not spend FAAB on.
- 2024-09-05 — According to Adam Harstad (Footballguys co-host, not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-05 (Film and Theory)]]: he urges managers to read back through several years of their own league's claim history, where they will find roughly 90% of waiver claims are spectacular failures returning zero value for the season. The genuine league-winners — he named James Conner during the Le'Veon Bell holdout, Kyren Williams last year and [[Puka Nacua]] — usually go for only 5-10% of budget a week or two before they break out, and they almost always show it very early; the exceptions are a handcuff whose starter goes down around week 13 and midseason quarterback changes in superflex. His conclusion is to pick your best option in the first week or three and fire the entire budget at it — in leagues allowing free pickups after the budget is spent he will drop 100% without thinking twice, since the money was likely to go to waste anyway and spending it does not stop the cheap speculative adds where most value is actually unearthed. The payoff from perfectly optimizing a bid, he said, is a rounding error away from zero. [[Matt Waldman]] added the complementary practice from his Footballguys 'Replacements' column: identify preemptive adds and the on-field keys that tell you when to move.
- 2025-09-09 — According to guest Jake Ciely of The Athletic (not a tracked expert) on [[Chris Harris]]'s show ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-09]]): the size of the bid should track the probability of the outcome you are buying, not the excitement of the name. On [[Kenneth Gainwell]] — his top running back add, whose upside case requires [[Kaleb Johnson]] never becoming a factor — he says he gives that scenario 'a pretty low chance of happening' and so, asked whether he would go crazy with his FAAB budget, answers 'absolutely not': grab him, ride out the RB2-3 range, and accept you may simply own your RB25-30 for the year. Harris's framing of the same week is that there are no great running back answers in normal leagues right now, which is a reason to spend small rather than to overpay for the least-bad option.
- 2025-09-16 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-16]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: a week with no silver bullet and mounting injuries forces unusual FAAB shapes. Both Harris and guest John Daigle (Establish The Run, not tracked) normally resist spending on quarterbacks in one-quarterback leagues because they are so plentiful — Daigle says it is the first time in a really long time he has put a quarterback on his top five — but with [[Joe Burrow]], [[Jayden Daniels]], [[Justin Fields]], [[Brock Purdy]] and [[J.J. McCarthy]] all likely out, they would pay: Daigle 11-14 for [[Daniel Jones]], Harris 15 and maybe 20 to be sure, with [[Jake Browning]] or Carson Wentz as the cheaper fallback if the price runs past that. For a skill-position add like [[Kayshon Boutte]], Daigle's number is 4-6. On defenses the rule is absolute: do not spend FAAB money on defenses, bid zero, and if you do not get one use the one you have, because defenses are kind of all the same. Daigle's list is capped at players rostered 33% or fewer, with the awkward tweener band (like [[Najee Harris]] at 37%) discussed separately.
- 2025-09-23 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-23]]): a flat positional rule on in-season bidding — 'I wouldn't spend any FAAB on any tight end ever' — offered while agreeing that [[Oronde Gadsden II]] had genuinely taken over the Chargers' tight end job, on the grounds that 'the position is obviously hot garbage' so the marginal weekly points bought are small. He applied a softer version of the same logic to [[Ollie Gordon II]]: 'I don't feel like he's a must-add, I don't need to spend a ton of FAAB on him.' Guest SCOTT FISH (Fantasy Cares, NOT tracked) framed the trade-off differently, arguing scarcity at tight end is precisely what makes a stumbled-upon five-to-seven-target starter worth acquiring.
- 2025-09-30 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-30]]): a thin waiver week still deserves bids, but sized to the archetype. Harris and guest Jeff Erickson (RotoWire, not tracked) restrict their list to players rostered in a third of Yahoo leagues or fewer and advise adding names broadly ahead of the first bye weeks while spending almost nothing — the Ben Gretsch $59-60 bid on [[Malik Washington]] is their example of what not to do, versus Harris's own bid of 11 in a 12-team league. The exception is the one player who could be a season-long starter: on [[Woody Marks]], Erickson would go 35-40% of budget in a 12-team league and over half in an NFFC 20-man-roster league, while Harris would consider over 50%. Harris's stated brake is not wanting to be 'a slave to the moment' — an injury like a [[Christian McCaffrey]] absence can put a better player on waivers next week, so budget is itself an option.
- 2025-10-07 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-10-07]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: a worked example of bidding into an ambiguous backfield. Harris says his own tendency is to play it conservative — 'oh, there might be something big coming down the pike' — and that he has tried to curb it over the years because 'one win is worth something', so he would extend for [[Rico Dowdle]] in a shallow league. Guest Brandon Funston of The Athletic (not tracked) applies a ceiling test instead: 'when you're splitting half of a Carolina backfield at the most, that's not what I'm emptying my budget on.' For the Chargers situation Funston demonstrates the contingency bid — roughly $15 of a $100 budget on [[Kimani Vidal]], a dollar or two less on Hassan Haskins, dropping the same player either way, deliberately not trying to land both. Harris agrees: 'I don't think you go ham for either guy.'
- 2025-11-04 — According to Jeff Erickson of RotoWire, a guest and not a tracked expert ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-11-04]]): the shallow-league FAAB question is really an opportunity-cost question. On [[Trey Benson]] at 59% rostered he would not load up, because in a well-managed ten-team roster the corresponding drop is someone more usable than a back who may not even be the full-time guy on return. Great if you get him cheap; a spec add and nothing more. [[Chris Harris]] extends the point to the listener's own bench — an engaged manager already rosters several players at that same speculative tier, so paying up to add another is borrowing problems. The exception both allow is a stated workhorse declaration, which Erickson notes teams never actually give.
