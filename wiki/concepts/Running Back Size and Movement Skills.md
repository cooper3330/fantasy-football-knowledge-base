# Running Back Size and Movement Skills

## Definition

Waldman's film-side case that the running back trait that actually separates
modern producers is **change of direction in tight space at speed**, and that
this trait is partly a function of size — there is a weight above which a back
physically cannot sustain it. The fantasy consequence is that "undersized"
is often a mispriced label rather than a risk factor.

The argument originates with [[Brandon Angelo]] on *Going Deep*. This page holds
the **physical** half; the **cognitive/tempo** half — controlling your own gears
and reading everyone else's — lives on
[[Pace Control and Movement Intellect]]. They were made in the same
conversation and should be read together.

## Expert Takes

### Where the argument originated (*Going Deep*, 2023-12-21)

The bullets below this section are Waldman relaying and extending the argument
on *RSP Film and Theory*. These three are the original conversation, recorded
first — see [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]].

- 2023-12-21 — [[Brandon Angelo]] (co-host of *Going Deep*) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21 (Pacing and Control)]], stating the size claim on film rather than in the abstract. Over a Georgia rep of [[James Cook]] splitting two defenders in a phone-booth-width crease: "this wouldn't be possible if James Cook was 210, 205. This type of movement isn't possible with your 205-plus-pound [back] — you can't move that quickly with that much flexibility in that lower half and then produce force getting up field. That is impossible to do, and also not fall." (The ASR carries no speaker labels through the screen-share; Waldman's own attribution on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21]] identifies the argument as Angelo's.) His projection: "that's what the NFL is moving towards at the running back position," including on shorter down-and-distance where these backs were previously taken off the field. His named have-nots for that movement class: [[Derrick Henry]] and [[Saquon Barkley]], both granted as great movers who "physically cannot get in and out of these positions as quickly."

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode, the counter-example that keeps the rule honest — **[[Nick Chubb]]**. "The one back who is as big as [[Saquon Barkley]], who has gotten away with it and been efficient, has been Nick Chubb." Waldman describes a goal-line rep where Chubb hurdles a fallen body and then cuts outside around a defender at the five, landing with his outside foot already turned to the boundary in extremely tight quarters — a movement he says he has produced more than once. Angelo's mechanism for it: "he doesn't prepare to catch himself, he prepares to re-accelerate — he's one of the best ever at re-accelerating post-contact." Take this as a bound on the framework: the weight ceiling is a tendency, and at least one big back beats it.

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode, on the jump-cut alternative, with [[Saquon Barkley]] as "a more agile version of an Adrian Peterson type of runner" — "jump cuts or bust with him," though violent enough to make it work. This is the same objection he restates in more developed form in the *Film and Theory* bullets below.

### As restated on *RSP Film and Theory* (2023-12-21)

- 2023-12-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-21]]), relaying a point raised by [[Brandon Angelo]] on the *Going Deep* podcast the night before: watch [[Lamar Jackson]], [[James Cook]], [[Christian McCaffrey]], [[Austin Ekeler]] and [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] and the shared skill is navigating "really tight creases at a high rate of speed" while making dramatic changes of direction that bigger backs cannot. His stated mechanism is a size ceiling on the trait: "if you made them 205, 210, 215 pounds they probably wouldn't be able to sustain that kind of movement." He notes [[James Cook]] sits at the lower end of even that weight tier.

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, on the alternative and why it costs you: the big-back version of a hard cut is the jump cut, and Adrian Peterson was its exemplar — "violent, like planting his feet into the ground, cracking the crust of the earth." The problem is that it is space-expensive and therefore inefficient: Peterson would jump two to three yards *forward* toward the line before cutting laterally. Kenyan Drake had the move without the results, partly on decision-making and use of space and partly because the move itself is inefficient. Waldman names [[Saquon Barkley]] as the current holdout — still a valuable fantasy commodity, but "not the most efficient runner on a play-to-play basis" on that class of direction change.

- 2023-12-21 — **Adam Harstad** (co-host — not a tracked expert), same episode: pushes back on the novelty framing. Small backs are not new — Tony Dorsett was listed at 190 pounds, lighter than Tyreek Hill, and produced over 15,000 career scrimmage yards; Walter Payton, Gale Sayers and Warrick Dunn are the same point. "Lots of small, quote-unquote undersized backs have had very strong careers at varying points in the NFL's history. That's not anything new." He asks whether Tarik Cohen was ahead of his time or behind it.

- 2023-12-21 — **Harstad**, same episode, on what *is* new: "a lot of the rise in passing efficiency over the past decade has been increasing efficiency to running backs, and that has necessitated a different breed of running back — your [[Christian McCaffrey]] or [[Austin Ekeler]] type." That demand has been proliferating for a decade and, as of December 2023, is neither accelerating nor reversing. The cyclical caveat lives on [[League Trend Cycles and Market Inefficiency]].

- 2023-12-21 — **Waldman**, same episode, one speculative application: Deuce Vaughn (RB, Dallas — one of the smallest backs in the league) "might have a legitimate chance to be a worthwhile player when Tony Pollard leaves next year." Offered as a possibility rather than a recommendation; no page created for him on the strength of it.

- 2024-03-28 — [[Brandon Angelo]] on *Going Deep* ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-28 (Going Deep - If We Were NFL GMs)]]), applied to [[Braelon Allen]] as a "do not draft": volume-based, rhythm-based backs who need touches to get going are increasingly hard NFL fits, because the league is running out of running-back-centric offenses to feature them in. Names [[Jahmyr Gibbs]], [[Tyjae Spears]] and [[James Cook]] as the modern replacement template — smaller, dynamic backs who fit today's passing-driven offenses over a 245-lb, volume-dependent bruiser.
- 2024-07-04 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-04]]): using [[Saquon Barkley]] as an example, Angelo said Barkley's game was defined by improvisational, unpredictable movement (spin moves, dead-leg cuts) that is harder to sustain at bigger playing weights -- contrasting Barkley's roughly 230-pound frame with [[Jahmyr Gibbs]]'s much lighter build -- and argued there is a real time constraint on how long bigger backs can rely on that style of movement before durability and fluidity of movement decline.
- 2024-07-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-22]]): Waldman previews work he is doing at Footballguys on contact balance graded through what he calls 'process-driven analytics' — taking a specific process and asking how it translates to football and fantasy value. His stated finding is that he has seen only two or three running backs start and perform like 1,000-yard rushers or better while being unable to defeat indirect collisions and bounce out of them, and he uses [[Clyde Edwards-Helaire]] as the case that never had that contact balance and was never going to be the exception. He applies a related movement caveat to [[Audric Estime]], who lacks speed and so must be more timely setting up blocks, and contrasts both with [[Rasheen Ali]], a 200-pounder whose strong contact balance makes him play bigger and faster than he tests.
- 2024-09-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and guest co-host Felix Sharpe of Campus to Canton (Sharpe is NOT a tracked expert) ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-11]]): Sharpe offered a freshman weight heuristic — a true-freshman running back who is over 200 pounds at the start of his freshman season usually reaches about 215, which he used to project [[Sire Gaines]] from 209 up to 217-218 at 6-0 with receiving chops. Waldman argued separately that 5-10, 230-pound backs still have a place in the modern NFL, naming [[James Conner]], [[Joe Mixon]], [[Gus Edwards]] and [[Audric Estime]] as backs winning with mass, and said what matters is the range of skills offered — [[Tahj Brooks]] is a big back with a modern skill set because he can win with moves, with power and with contact balance rather than one note.
- 2024-09-25 — According to guest Felix Sharpe of Campus2Canton (not a tracked expert) with [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-25 (Campus2Canton with Felix Sharpe)]]): listed height can hide how a back actually plays. [[Kalel Mullings]] (6'2') and [[Donovan Edwards]] (6'1') are nominally the same archetype, but Mullings runs smaller with his pads, shrinking his tackling surface and exploding through linebackers and defensive backs to fall forward for 5.4 yards after contact, while Edwards runs tall — Waldman noted a flat-footed 180-pound cornerback can drive him backwards. Waldman tied it to his old Darren McFadden evaluation lesson: watch whether a big back actually runs through small defenders. Sharpe added that he does not personally weight straight-line speed at running back even though the NFL does, which is why he expects Mullings to test poorly and fall to day three regardless of production.
- 2025-03-03 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-03]]): Waldman reiterated that short-area agility, not straight-line speed, is the calling card that identifies a big back who can create — the argument he made for Le'Veon Bell at Michigan State (a roughly 6.75 three-cone) and now applies to [[Tahj Brooks]] (6.9 three-cone plus an elite 20-yard shuttle). The mechanism he describes is combining change of direction and acceleration with vision and contact balance so a runner can both make defenders miss their angles and run through them, converting negative plays into gains — behavior he attributes to Marshawn Lynch and Walter Payton. He also noted that in day-to-day work with Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) he talks far more about the shuttle than about 40 times, to Bob's chagrin.
- 2025-04-07 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-04-07]]): asked by Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) about the mythical 200-pound line of demarcation for running backs, Waldman says he does not really care in the abstract — it depends entirely on the projected ROLE. If a player made his living as a between-the-tackles grinder under 200 or even 210 pounds, you are counting on him adding weight and have to judge whether that is possible. If he is a space player and big-play option who catches well and is smart between the tackles — Waldman's example is [[Austin Ekeler]] — the workload splits evenly enough between running and receiving that you should not worry about him wearing down after a year or two. He applies the same logic to small receivers later, citing Anquan Boldin (4.71) and Le'Veon Bell as slow players whose quickness mattered more, and arguing there is less surface area to hit on a smaller player.
- 2025-09-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-18 (Going Deep Listener Q&A)]]): no position ranges as widely in body type as running back — from a man the size of a cornerback or smaller to one the size of a defensive end — so a metric only sticks if you first identify the movement type it is being applied to. His example is [[Dalvin Cook]], panned out of FSU because his SPARQ-style scores were poor and he could not leap, when his game was curvilinear movement rather than cutting: on direction changes his feet ran in place like little pistons as he rotated, and you rarely saw a lateral or jump cut. Against that he sets Adrian Peterson, still jumping three yards forward then cutting two yards outside in his thirties, and notes Kenyan Drake tried the same thing and could not survive it. He adds [[Derrick Henry]] — dismissed early as just a big man leaning forward for four yards — and Frank Gore after two ACL tears, both making lemonade in ways others could not. Conclusion: match the metric to the skill set, or it tells you nothing.
