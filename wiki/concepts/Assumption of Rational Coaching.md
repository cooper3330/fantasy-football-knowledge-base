## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2025-10-20 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-10-20]]): Discussing why coaches lean on prevent defense late in games, Waldman said it largely comes down to job security -- coaches fear getting fired off one or two aggressive plays that go wrong more than they fear a slower, methodical drive, so they take the ball out of playmakers' hands (like Carolina's Brian Burns) rather than trust the unit that's been playing well. He noted the counter-argument -- forcing a string of smaller completions lowers an offense's scoring odds -- and called it genuinely close to a coin flip, with Denver's prevent look against the Giants working partly because Bo Nix got chunk plays anyway while Burns himself was limping and not fully trustworthy in that spot.
- 2025-11-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-11-06]]): borrowing a phrase from Sigmund Bloom, Waldman warns against assuming a staff will keep doing the obviously good thing it just did. The case in point is Atlanta pairing [[Michael Penix Jr.]]'s best trait — fades and jump balls at every depth — with [[Drake London]]'s ability to go up and take the ball, producing three touchdowns against New England, while never having used [[Kyle Pitts]] the same way despite his Florida rebounding profile. Waldman's test for whether a discovery is real rather than a happy accident is repetition and extension: if it shows up over the next two games and spreads to Pitts, it is genuine; if it appears only occasionally with London, the staff is not taking it seriously. Angelo's supporting evidence for the pessimistic read is that Zach Robinson still runs 'the whole heck out of pistol', and Waldman applies the same skepticism to Chip Kelly's use of [[Ashton Jeanty]] and John Morton's of [[Jahmyr Gibbs]].
- 2026-08-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] and [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2026-08-06]]): both argue NFL front offices frequently reward political conformity over being right, because executives want to be 'the smartest man in the room' and threaten dissent rather than run collaboratively -- Waldman compares it to lockstep political tribalism. Their case study is [[Kayshawn Boutte]] in New England: he's outplaying camp expectations but the team appears set on trading him rather than admit the earlier plan (making him an outside X, bringing in [[Romeo Odunze]]) was wrong, even though Waldman argues the objectively best lineup keeps him in the slot. They also cite Doug Marrone reportedly needing permission to let James Robinson compete for a starting job because the front office wanted [[Leonard Fournette]]'s contract gone, and the counter-example of [[Liam Cohen]] in Jacksonville adapting his scheme midseason toward [[Parker Washington]] when the original plan wasn't producing -- which they hold up as the rare example of a coach prioritizing outcomes over ego.
