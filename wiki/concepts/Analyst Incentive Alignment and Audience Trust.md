# Analyst Incentive Alignment and Audience Trust

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-17 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-17 (Writing Craft and Content)]]), with co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys; not a tracked expert): how an analyst is paid determines how much their recommendations can be trusted. Harstad, offered a Pro Football Reference brand ambassadorship, took the affiliate link but asked that 100% of the roughly couple-hundred dollars a year route to charity, so he never has a financial stake in a site he had already promoted for a decade — 'I never want my audience to think that I'm recommending something to them for my own personal benefit.' Waldman runs no ads on his site or shows and declines gambling affiliates, saying the one thing he asks people to buy is the RSP, and that pricing it exorbitantly would attract customers who think they own him; he has turned down wealthy buyers wanting inside access. Harstad's framing of career satisfaction is incentive alignment rather than success — he does the work he wants and is compensated when society values it — and he credits Footballguys for asking him to write the picks-against-the-spread column as himself, including telling readers they will lose 5-10% of everything they wager. Both stress the rule: write for the audience you want, because you get the audience you write for, and then guard that relationship jealously. Waldman adds that professional jealousy never goes away; the check is asking whether you actually want a different path.
- 2024-10-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-21 (Feel It or Fk It 10.21.24)]]): prompted by reporting that a Steelers quarterback change would trigger a locker-room mutiny — and by the broadcast playing up Mike Tomlin's indecision — Waldman argued 'we have reached a point where the desire to have drama far outweighs the desire to inform the public. Keep that in mind as you're consuming your information... we've been not skeptical enough. So let's all start being a little more skeptical, kids, as we set our lineups and consume the news.' He extended it to the crowd habit of editorializing off a player's facial expression, comparing it to the neighbor who says they always saw evil in the serial killer's eyes — an after-the-fact story, not information. The [[Russell Wilson]] case is his exhibit: a player 'boiled down to a meme' whose actual play contradicted the narrative. Bob Harris (not a tracked expert) agreed and framed it as needing to adjust strategy for a media environment optimized for drama.
- 2024-10-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-23]]): on the [[Anthony Richardson]] panic cycle, Waldman argues that loud film-based development warnings often come from people chasing clicks on their channel — and, less nefariously, from people without experience evaluating players over a long-term horizon. His challenge: show him the tried-and-true data track record establishing that a given on-film trait predicts future development, because when analysts say 'this is a bad sign for development,' usually they are wrong and usually they do not really know; they know what was a good play and what was a bad play plus some anecdotes. He also notes his own choice to publish a Richardson piece containing no film at all is itself a signal — that the player is fine for his trajectory. Related distinction he insists on: inexperienced is not the same as raw.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31]]): former players on TV are paid to create controversy, and that incentive corrupts their analysis. He points to the near-universal ex-player pile-on over the [[Anthony Richardson]] 'tap out' — including Brian Baldinger shutting down a civil disagreement rudely — and to Micah Parsons and JJ Watt saying Richardson should simply have lied, as evidence the industry rewards manufactured outrage and treats vulnerability as weakness. Waldman's separate distinction: football knowledge is not scouting knowledge. Ex-quarterbacks can teach you a lot about play, but they have not studied what projects and how to weight a flaw within a whole developmental arc — he likens their conclusions to a grammarian dismissing Hemingway for ending on a preposition or Cormac McCarthy for skipping commas, and calls it a 'post office mentality' where the way it was done at their one branch becomes the only way. [[Brandon Angelo]] agrees the players themselves privately did not care, and that what actually damages a locker room is the front office's lack of transparency.
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31 (Tapping Out, Archetypes and Modeling)]]): Waldman said what qualifies as trustworthy work is an analyst who declines to present findings with absolute certainty and shows you the pitfalls — pointing to co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) cheerfully admitting his own statistical modeling can be voodoo. Waldman extended it to film work: everyone has holes, there is a lot beneath the iceberg about what makes a good player, and film, models and data each supply context rather than the whole picture. Both applied the same lens to incentives elsewhere — Harstad's rule that you must understand the nature of a relationship (a corporate HR department serves the company, not you; the media serves clicks, not the player), which he framed as [[Anthony Richardson]]'s real mistake and a useful learning experience. Waldman added that some former players railing about the tap-out are being disingenuous because they are paid to manufacture a tempest in a teapot, while others genuinely believe it.
- 2024-12-05 — According to Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) with [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-05]]): treat public statements against the speaker's incentives. Harstad's example was John Runyan delivering the NFL's discipline message on dirty play despite having been repeatedly voted among the league's dirtiest players and having openly dismissed such complaints as turning football into basketball — hypothesis one is a genuine change of heart, hypothesis two is that his salary depends on people believing in one, and 'both of those stories fit the evidence equally well'. He extended it to the split between former players whose paychecks require public outrage and those whose don't. Waldman characterized it as the marketing and PR arm of the league getting too involved in the operations end, so punishment tracks Twitter sentiment rather than the facts on the field.
- 2025-01-07 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-07]]): Harmon described his own analytical posture and its costs. He acknowledged he skews positive, hunting silver linings, and that this sometimes means holding on to players too long — naming [[Jahan Dotson]] and [[Rashad Bateman]] as examples. He said audiences often want him to use route charts to slam players on social media, and he deliberately refuses, because his role is to find how a player's situation could work out rather than cataloguing where they suck, and because young players' outcomes swing on confidence and coaching changes ([[Bryce Young]], [[Quentin Johnston]], [[Jameson Williams]] as end-of-season improvement cases). Co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) described the opposite instinct — cutting a player off fast, which is right more often than wrong but makes you look like an ass — and said the two postures balance out on the show.
- 2025-07-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-21]]) and co-host Bob Harris (not a tracked expert): discussing whether a Chargers beat writer has an axe to grind on [[Quentin Johnston]], Bob Harris invokes Peter King's line from his SI.com days that sportswriters do not root for teams, they root for their stories — and extends it to fantasy analysts, who having staked out a position on a player keep defending or diminishing him, sometimes with good reason, sometimes to look predictive. Waldman declines to judge the specific writer but agrees the dynamic is real and specifically about how media see a player relative to the expectations originally set for him. He separately criticizes analysis that asserts a player 'just needs more work' without backing it with details, and models the alternative by spelling out exactly what [[Tre Harris]] lacks in his release. Waldman also sketches the cost side of career incentives: a big-network job can consume the very working time that made the analyst worth hiring.
- 2025-09-03 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-03]]): Harris describes two failure modes in fantasy analysis that both produce sameness. The first is herd movement — guest Andy Behrens (not tracked) says once an opinion gains gravity, a large share of analysts simply find different ways to express the same take, which is how [[Ja'Marr Chase]] became the universal 1.01. The second is inverted: analysts feel commercial pressure to zig when everyone zags, 'but because everybody zigs in exactly the same way it just comes across as just stupid.' Harris's diagnosis of the mechanism is people learning seven bullet points about a player, taking a deep breath and reciting them fast. Both hosts also concede a charitable alternative — Harris named Jeff Bell and Dave Kluge as analysts who are not cynical or content-driven, just genuinely excited, and said 'a little belief in life can take you a long way.' Behrens attributed much of it to keeping the content machine fed in early August.
- 2025-09-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-22]]): Waldman described a source-cultivation dynamic in insider work, using Jordan Schultz (not a tracked analyst) as the example — a solid insider who gets good information, but who gets it by being a glowing endorser of every single player, so those players see the praise in his timeline and become his sources. His illustration was Schultz calling [[Tetairoa McMillan]] a future All-Pro who 'has been catching everything,' which Waldman said was hyperbole aimed less at accuracy than at building a relationship he can leverage. Waldman was explicit he was not claiming Schultz is wrong about McMillan's future, only that readers should discount praise whose incentive is access. He paired this with Ryan Clark as the other pole — entertainment-driven ESPN monologue analysis — calling the two 'the yin and yang of NFL analysis.'
