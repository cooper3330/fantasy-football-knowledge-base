## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-03 — According to co-host Adam Harstad (not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman]]'s show ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-03]]): 'Harstad's Razor' — anytime a finding strikes you as interesting, cool or compelling, first assume it is selection bias. Surprise means either a true unintuitive result or that somebody screwed up, and the second is far more common. He places it alongside Occam's razor and Hanlon's razor. Two named failure modes: Berkson's paradox, where restricting range manufactures a negative correlation (SAT scores versus grades within a single college; Chase Stuart's finding that among the top 100 career rushers, more yards before 27 predicted fewer after 27 — because low-low players never made the list), and conditioning on a collider, where two variables both influence a third (marital satisfaction and children both affect divorce, creating a fake link between kids and misery). Harstad stresses he has stepped on every one of these rakes himself, and that treating error-hunting as a game is what makes it get done. [[Matt Waldman]] praised the humility of the approach.
- 2024-12-05 — According to Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) with [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-05]]): the running back mileage debate is a textbook selection-bias trap — 'people are not cars', and the car/odometer analogy fails because humans repair themselves while a car only degrades from the lot. Two separate biases stack: not controlling for age (a 1,000-carry back is simply 500 carries older than a 500-carry back), and treating workload as randomly assigned when it is coaches voting with their dollars. Harstad noted the argument was prompted two years earlier by [[Rhamondre Stevenson]] passing [[Josh Jacobs]] in [[Dynasty]] value on 'mileage' grounds despite being only twelve days younger. Waldman agreed the intuitive story is seductive precisely because a real, small short-term effect exists to anchor it.
- 2025-07-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-07-30]]): Harmon openly concedes a selection bias in Reception Perception percentiles — he charts roughly 80 players an offseason and about 600 since 2014, and does not chart every team's fifth receiver, so a 10th-percentile success rate is 10th percentile of a pre-filtered pool of mostly notable receivers, not of all NFL receivers. He pairs that with a second interpretive caution raised by co-host James Koh (not tracked): a low success rate does not mean a player was never open, only that he was open less often than the charted average. Harmon applies both caveats directly to [[Xavier Legette]] — bad numbers, not the worst player in the league.
