# Preseason ADP vs In-Season Production

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-09-05 — According to Adam Harstad (Footballguys co-host, not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-05 (Film and Theory)]] [Redraft (Standard)]: for the first three weeks of a season, preseason ADP is more predictive of rest-of-year production than everything learned so far; at four weeks the two reach parity, and after that results carry progressively more weight. He is therefore very slow to pivot off preseason opinions inside the first month, adjusting in degrees rather than reversing, and is equally skeptical of early-season standouts as of early-season busts. Preseason ADP never falls to zero weight either — even in week 16 he leans toward an underperforming third-round pick over an undrafted alternative, because maybe the evaluation was right and something was holding him back. Dynasty has no equivalent clean threshold, because there you are predicting careers and must ask why a player is or is not producing; he cited Darren McFadden as the same talent throughout, wildly productive in some schemes and unrosterable in others, whom a production-chasing manager would have swung on constantly. The edge comes from moving at a different speed than your leaguemates in whichever direction they are wrong — holding [[Trey McBride]] when they cut him, or buying [[Puka Nacua]] at WR18-19 prices after one or two weeks. [[Matt Waldman]] added his own guideline: a player taken in the top 36 of a rookie draft deserves at least a full year barring a career-ending injury or a colossal maturity failure.
- 2024-09-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-09]]): asked whether week-one utilization data is the word of the fantasy gods, Waldman answered both 'feel it' and 'fuck it' — the fantasy gods are Greek, not monotheistic, so their word is fallible and open to negotiation rather than law. Utilization is a good starting point, but you have to ask how and why that data rose or fell in game context and pair it with the film. He applied it to his own losing [[Zamir White]] call and cited [[Josh Jacobs]] in the 2022 Hall of Fame game — written off on preseason usage, then led the league in rushing and finished RB3. Bob Harris (co-host, not a tracked expert) framed the whole episode the same way, invoking Tom Brady's mid-2020 stretch of failed deep balls before finishing as the league's most efficient deep thrower: what happened in week one is not a permanent condition, and overreaction is the enemy — divorce emotion from process.
- 2024-09-30 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-30]]): asked whether the things we think in May turn out more right than the things we think two weeks into September, Waldman said 'absolutely,' and generalized it into a process argument. His analogy is his own product line: the pre-draft RSP is where the most work goes, and the in-season updates are helpful but secondary — the same is true of fantasy, where the offseason is when you have analyzed the situation, seen results, examined the process and projected from a clear picture. He named [[Malik Nabers]] as the class's best receiver by far, Derrick Henry not being done and a Saquon Barkley revenge tour as calls held since spring. His diagnosis of the overreaction problem: analysts feel obliged to analyze every major point, succumb to needing clicks, and end up believing the infotainment they are producing instead of holding a longer clear-eyed view. He also argued 'right and wrong are not permanent conditions' — declaring yourself right on a player mid-season is made up, since that is settled only after the season; the interim is following a process and sorting things out, then using next year to do it again.
- 2024-12-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-09]]): using [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] at WR28 as the case study, Waldman offers a general drafting rule — the slot where you draft a player should be your baseline expectation, and you should realistically expect a tier above it, so drafting Harrison as the 12th-to-15th player overall means expecting top-three overall production, which Waldman says he has only seen a rookie deliver once (Edgerrin James). If you take an unproven player early, live or die with it; it is on you. Bob Harris (Footballguys, not a tracked expert) framed the same point as never paying a premium — you don't walk into Starbucks and ask to pay double — and cited his own record of taking [[Jahmyr Gibbs]] three rounds cheaper than [[Bijan Robinson]] on the identical draft-capital-and-role arguments, and preferring [[Malik Nabers]] to Harrison this year despite the quarterback concerns, since Kyler Murray turned out to be no bargain either.
- 2025-02-17 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-17]]) [Best Ball]: on the first Underdog ADPs of the year, Waldman argues rookie running backs and receivers are priced so aggressively in February because people want to be able to say they called it early — and then 'when the draft comes suddenly they all turtle', because landing spots arrive and the pre-draft conviction evaporates. He took no rookies in any best ball or mock draft, saying the ones he was comfortable with were gone long before his comfortable price; his position is that it is about the price range, not the rookie. Untracked co-host Bob Harris added the recency half of the same idea — 'people remember the last thing they saw' — citing [[David Montgomery]] being forgotten after a five-game absence and [[Clyde Edwards-Helaire]] going from a single highlight game to RB6 ADP and an RB30 finish.
- 2025-03-10 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-03-10]]) [Best Ball]: uncertainty is itself a pricing force, and free agency resolving it is a tradeable event. Drafting in early March, Waldman said he can feel the weight of uncertainty in the room — he passes on players like [[Rico Dowdle]] presenting as a team's top back when he does not believe he knows what the team is doing. He flagged several prices he considered wrong before the market caught up: [[Aaron Jones]] at RB28 on Underdog (he said RB18 would already be a bargain), [[Mike Gesicki]] at TE27, [[Zach Ertz]] at TE26, [[Sean Tucker]] at RB106, and [[Jakobi Meyers]] going at a WR4 price while producing as a WR2. Conversely he argued [[Bucky Irving]]'s top-10 running back price already contains his upside.
- 2025-05-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-19]]): both hosts used five-week snapshots to argue against judging a receiver on a short window. Bob Harris (Footballguys, not tracked) pointed out [[Ladd McConkey]] sat at WR55 through five weeks of 2024 and finished WR12, [[Jerry Jeudy]] was WR48 before his second-half surge, and [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] was WR20 through five before finishing top-12. Waldman applied it directly to [[Brandon Aiyuk]]: the limited timeline in which Aiyuk was judged before his injury would very likely have been turned around by year's end, so the poor start should not be read as a settled verdict. Waldman also applied the reverse to [[Brock Purdy]] — QB6 in a healthy 2023 and QB13 in an injury-hit 2024 read as consistently top-10 when put in context.
- 2025-06-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-16]]): the June ADP board is full of prices set by story rather than projection. [[Ashton Jeanty]]'s top-10 cost is 'the inflated price of the new running back who's the star' — top-25 is fine, top-10 is premature — with Bob Harris (not tracked) invoking Clyde Edwards-Helaire's 2020 as the last time the market was certain a rookie's role would be robust. [[Ricky Pearsall]]'s WR41 price rests on a narrow band of late-season production, the 'remember the last thing we saw' effect that Waldman grants has some momentum value but should not be overstated. He also notes that he publishes some rankings at Footballguys deliberately ahead of where he would actually draft, to get people's attention on a situation — an admission that a published rank and a buy signal are not the same object.
- 2025-07-10 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-10]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the hosts each name a top-30 ADP player whose price they will not pay, and the shared logic is that ADP lags both age and role change. Angelo fades [[Tyreek Hill]] — nearly 32, wrist injury, going half a round before [[Davante Adams]], in an offense he expects to run more — and would rather move back for the cheaper veteran. Waldman fades [[Bucky Irving]] on an unsustainable 5.4 yards per carry, an offensive line missing Tristan Wirfs early, and a three-way committee. Both also argue the inverse: [[Nick Chubb]] at the end of the 15th round or an off-ADP [[Damien Martinez]] can carry more expected value than the priced-in name, and Waldman notes he would normally never recommend drafting a player for touchdown outliers, as he does with [[DeAndre Hopkins]], but treats it as a deliberate exception.
- 2025-07-21 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-07-21]]) [Best Ball]: news-driven ADP moves are exploitable, but only if your draft volume matches. He quantifies the [[Quinshon Judkins]] arrest cascade — Judkins down 19 spots in seven days, [[Jerome Ford]] up 33, [[Dylan Sampson]] up 38 — and says that because he runs roughly ten simultaneous drafts at any time across hundreds of leagues building portfolios, he wants exposure and will leverage those momentary rises and drops. In a single league the calculus inverts: 'uncertainty is the enemy,' you should want more sure things, and you have the benefit of time because you will most likely draft closer to September when the range of outcomes is clearer. He also warns the market overshoots on the beneficiaries — Sampson deserves a bump but not the full 38 spots, because at his new price there are players with fewer questions.
- 2025-08-13 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-13]]): ADP as an ecosystem is now so completely fractured that there is effectively no such thing — he and guest Dave Kluge of Footballguys (not a tracked expert) use the Footballguys tool averaging about 15 sources, but a listener drafting on Yahoo, ESPN, DraftKings, Underdog or Sleeper will see something entirely different, and nothing can be done about that except drafting from your own list. Harris also cautions that mid-August ADP data is missing perhaps 95% of the volume it will eventually have. Kluge turns the fragmentation into the actionable edge: spend fifteen minutes per platform finding where that specific platform's ADP is an outlier, and make those players your targets there. Harris adds that default app ranks, not ADP itself, often guide the actual picks casual drafters make.
- 2025-08-14 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-14]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the 'offenses we might be wrong about' exercise with guest Sigmund Bloom (Footballguys, not tracked) exists to surface the assumptions underpinning ranks. Harris says he tries to make a player's situation matter as little as possible in his ranks because analysts are bad at predicting situations, yet most draft talk is nothing but situation — depth chart, quarterback, coordinator. Bloom's method is to compare last year's ADP to this year's and ask what changed, why it changed, or why it did not change when it should have, which distills not just your own assumptions but the hive mind's. Both stress that week one shatters the mind palace, and the edge is course-correcting before everyone else: waiting for more evidence in weeks three or four is too late. Bloom's metaphor — the lineman who gives up the sack is often the one positioned to recover the fumble. Harris's counterweight: 'don't overreact, but don't fail to react.' Applied to flag players, Harris limits himself to ten names he is higher on than ADP rather than listing a hundred, because a long list is unfalsifiable.
- 2025-08-15 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-15]]): the platform you draft on matters as much as the consensus ADP, because default ranks prod your leaguemates. He gave concrete spreads — [[Jordan Addison]] at 81 on Football Guys but 98 on ESPN; [[Ricky Pearsall]] at 87-88 on average but 134 on CBS, 110 on ESPN and 111 on Yahoo; [[Josh Jacobs]] correctly priced only on CBS — and advised splitting the difference between your own rank and the platform default rather than paying your own number. He also argued the market systematically pushes RB2s too late because receivers, tight ends and quarterbacks get taken in between, when running back is still where you get the best chance of nailing a draft: any team can concoct a usable fantasy receiver, but only a couple dozen backs will help you and we already know 15 or 16 of their names.
- 2025-08-18 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-18]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harris and guest Patrick Doherty of Rotoworld (not a tracked expert) both describe their bust methodology as finding the largest gaps between their own ranks and a blended ADP — Doherty uses the Footballguys average of many ADPs as a starting point, then layers on gut, defining a bust candidate as a player he has actively recoiled from and never once drafted. Harris says the same of his five. Both flag that there is no longer any single ADP, and Doherty warns that platform ADPs increasingly include bot mock drafts, so the number being used to declare value may itself be an artefact — 'the AI could skew the ADP.' Harris' related discipline is that opinions should be sticky in favour of talent but not so sticky that pride outweighs new information, and that once games start he wants to 'be an eyeball' rather than defend takes.
- 2025-08-29 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-29]]): the risk exercise repeatedly turned on price movement rather than player change. Guest Jeff Bell of Football Guys (not tracked) argued both [[George Kittle]] and [[Mike Evans]] moved from roughly the sixth or seventh round last season to the third this season without the player changing, treating that as risk; Harris split the two, saying ADP was dumb about Kittle before and is 'on the mark' now, while agreeing Evans's price is unjustified. Both also flagged the expert-versus-civilian ADP gap on [[Ashton Jeanty]], who sometimes falls into round two in mocks with industry drafters but went ahead of [[Saquon Barkley]] at 1.06 in a Football Guys event with a civilian lean. Harris closes by warning the late-August market consensus about dead zones and surpluses will simply be wrong a week into the season.
- 2025-09-02 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-02]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harris makes two structural points about how August drafting distorts prices. First, drafts held too early are not serious drafts — he singles out the Fishbowl in the first week of July and an August 4 Reddit experts draft as the reason he has [[Tyreek Hill]], [[Joe Mixon]] and [[Keenan Allen]] on rosters at prices he would not pay now, and says [[Jordan Addison]] at pick 78 pre-suspension is the clearest example of information arriving after the price was set. Second, he argues the market perpetually misreads positional scarcity: you draft the scarce position first, not last, and running back is the slimmest and scariest market, which is why he was happy paying up for [[Josh Jacobs]] and taking [[Chuba Hubbard]] in the late third. He notes he took no receiver in the first round of any of these drafts, calling the modern receiver landscape 'the infinite wide receiver two sadness' — a million guys get targets, so he is not precious about which ones he ends up with.
- 2025-09-03 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-03]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harmon described the market's attention as the eye of Sauron — it moves past proven good players toward the new shiny thing — using [[Michael Pittman Jr.]] as the case study, a receiver with three straight strong charting seasons whom everybody moved on from after one injury year. He argued the flip side is also valuable: being an unhyped consensus second-round pick like [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] is a good thing, because it means you are already viewed as established. James Koh (not tracked) added the running back life cycle he used to discuss with Austin Ekeler — every fantasy back goes from 'get him the ball' to 'get him out of the way for the young guy', which he now sees applied to a 26-year-old [[Travis Etienne Jr.]]. Harmon countered that discounted ambiguity is where fantasy edges live: if one back in a muddled committee pops, you got him cheap.
- 2025-09-08 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-08]]): one data point is worth one data point. On Detroit he says you drafted most of your Lions really high, and this is not the moment you sell them and not the moment you bench them; you hope it looks better. Guest **Sam Gold** (not tracked) makes the same point in reverse, saying he does not want to overreact given that Green Bay is a pretty good defense. Harris applies the symmetric version to the upside: the summer arguments against [[Breece Hall]], his expiring contract and Aaron Glenn's three-backs comment, look dumb after one week, and his own August skepticism about [[Bucky Irving]] was wrong for that week. He also gives [[Ashton Jeanty]] an explicit mulligan for a rain game.
- 2025-09-09 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-09-09]]): Harmon's Week 1 pep talk was that receiver is a violently volatile week-to-week position and that fans forget it every year because the offseason is spent looking at season-long totals. The 2025 Week 1 half-PPR top five was Zay Flowers, Quentin Johnston, Emeka Egbuka, Keon Coleman and Deebo Samuel while Ja'Marr Chase (26 yards), Nico Collins (25), Amon-Ra St. Brown (45), Drake London (55), Brian Thomas Jr. (11 receiving), A.J. Brown (8), Tee Higgins (33), Mike Evans (51) and Terry McLaurin (27) all flopped. His counter-evidence: the 2024 Week 1 top five was Jayden Reed, Cooper Kupp, Allen Lazard, Tyreek Hill and Jameson Williams, and Drake London went 2-15 in that same week before finishing top five in receiving yards. How it starts is not how it finishes.
- 2025-09-11 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-11]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harris and guest Dave Kluge (Football Guys, not a tracked expert) work through how much a single week should move a ranking. Kluge's rule of thumb is to keep the rank sticky and not let one game cause a complete overreaction, but that one game is enough to change everybody's opinion and might turn out to be correct — one week is a huh, two weeks is a trend. Harris's counter is that you have to react some: if every player sits on a risk-reward continuum, one week can legitimately worsen the risk side even if the reward is unchanged, and an NFL season is itself a limited sample, so at some point the big weeks have to start arriving. Harris also notes that linear ranks do a disservice here, since [[Tyreek Hill]] plainly has more upside than [[Keenan Allen]] while Allen now looks like the safer floor.
- 2025-09-12 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-12]]): the Overreaction Theater format is an explicit discipline for updating after one game — you have to adjust based on Week 1 without going crazy, trying neither to overreact nor to completely deny reality, and separating the names you need another data point on from the ones you do not. Two structural notes from the episode: Harris repeatedly stresses that a rank change is a decision for this week only, not a pronouncement for the rest of the season ('the Commanders only have to decide for Week 3'), and that many moves are really commentary on the rest of the field rather than on the player — he raised [[Kyler Murray]] from QB13 to QB8 mostly because other quarterbacks got scarier. Guest cousin Josh (untracked) adds the media-pile-on caveat: when everyone decides to bury a team like Miami there is no retribution and the volume of criticism becomes self-reinforcing, which is its own source of overreaction.
- 2025-09-16 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-16]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: two rookie running backs illustrate opposite market errors. With [[Ashton Jeanty]] at pick six overall, the market never factored in the possibility that he was not ready — and the reaction to 18 snaps of [[Zamir White]] has been Twitter ghouls principal-skinnering and blaming the children rather than accepting rookie growing pains. With [[Omarion Hampton]], Harris notes the market unusually did not treat a first-round rookie back as a top-15 overall pick, letting him fall to the late third — still too rich for Harris's blood, so he owns none — but he insists it is way, way, way too early to call him a bust at that price, because late-third-round picks bust all the time. The common thread is that two games of usage should update your view of readiness without resolving the price question either way.
- 2025-09-22 — According to guest Patrick Doherty of Rotoworld (not a tracked expert) on [[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-22]] [Redraft (Standard)]: two Week 3 cases of a draft-price thesis expiring. [[Tony Pollard]] was drafted specifically for Weeks 1-4 while [[Tyjae Spears]] was on IR; three good workloads produced one touchdown, so the window closed without paying. [[Calvin Ridley]] was drafted around WR26-32 and Doherty re-priced him at WR40-48. [[Chris Harris]] added the general lesson he says people ignore each summer — the 'who else is there in the receiving corps?' argument is empty, because 'every team has 700 receivers' and someone like [[Chimere Dike]] is always out there getting jet sweeps. He would sit or cut a failing mid-round receiver in a shallow league rather than feel obligated by draft cost.
- 2025-09-25 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-25]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: Harris and guest Ben Gretch (Stealing Signals, not tracked) repeatedly circled how many games it takes for bad in-season play to outweigh the case that got a player drafted. On [[Brian Thomas Jr.]] Gretch asked directly at what point last year's tape and production get outweighed, and both agreed three games is not enough — Harris admitted he would be the one who clings too long, and kept Thomas WR11, near his draft rank. The same logic ran the other way on [[Travis Kelce]] (a top-12 tight end by cost who Gretch thinks looks washed on tape) and on [[Ashton Jeanty]], where Harris separated the player's eventual season line from the drafter's already-lost season. On [[Marvin Harrison Jr.]] Gretch drew the key distinction: a player whose problems date to day one is different from a player two weeks removed from an excellent season, so small-sample forgiveness is not distributed evenly.
- 2026-04-23 — According to [[Chris Harris]] and untracked co-host Andy Behrens ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2026-04-23]]): the hosts framed their annual full re-draft of the prior year's rookie skill-position class as a deliberate exercise in showing how wrong pre-draft certainty is -- last year's consensus dynasty rookie #5 (Travis Hunter) and rookie #9-ish hype names (Caleb Johnson) missed both hosts' post-season top-20 entirely, while names outside early hype (Quinshon Judkins, Emeka Egbuka, Luther Burden III) rose into the top 4-9. Harris said even 'draft industrial complex' analysts he respects (three guests over the prior three weeks) openly admit they don't really know outcomes and are only offering 'a small marginal edge,' contrasting that humility with TV personalities who speak in false certainties.
