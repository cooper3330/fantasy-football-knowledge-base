# Role Difficulty and Replaceability

## Definition

A scouting heuristic for splitting credit between two receivers (or backs) in
the same offense: prefer the one doing the *harder* thing — running tougher
routes, drawing tougher coverage, winning contested targets — over the one
whose easier assignment or favorable usage produces similar box-score output.
The practical test is replaceability: an offense misses the receiver doing the
hard thing more than it misses the one benefiting from it, even when their
current numbers look alike.

## Expert Takes

- 2024-01-04 — **Adam Harstad** (co-host — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-04]]: states the heuristic directly — "prefer the guy who's doing the harder thing, because it's easier to learn the easy thing than it is to learn the hard thing." Applies it to [[Nico Collins]] and [[Tank Dell]] (Collins draws the tougher coverage, is harder to replace), with Mike Evans / Chris Godwin, Isaac Bruce / Torry Holt, and Larry Fitzgerald / Anquan Boldin as the historical comps — in each pair, the WR2-labeled player was doing the objectively harder job.
- 2024-01-04 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], same episode, the counter: he explicitly declines to rank by projected role difficulty, preferring to "bet on a guy who I see doing the thing right now than a guy who I think could possibly do the thing in the future." Concedes Harstad's read of who's doing what on the field is usually correct, but treats it as a lower-priority input than proven current production.
- 2024-01-04 — **Harstad**, same episode, extended to [[Rashee Rice]]: playing with Patrick Mahomes "doesn't mean you're going to be good" on its own, but if you are a good receiver next to Mahomes you will look productive regardless — a caution against reading rookie WR box scores in a great quarterback's offense at face value.
- 2024-01-22 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-01-22]]): applies the framework directly to [[Chris Godwin]], the Mike-Evans-comp receiver named in the original 2024-01-04 pairing — being able to play all three alignments at a Pro Bowl level is itself the "harder thing," which is why Waldman doesn't think Tampa Bay's other depth receivers (Trey Palmer, David Moore) can be pieced together to replace him the way Houston's receiver room has survived without [[Nico Collins]] or [[Tank Dell]].

- 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-19]]: extends the framework into locker-room/leadership terms rather than pure route difficulty. Grades [[Calvin Ridley]] and [[Diontae Johnson]] as good starters who both need to be "couched among" other leaders of a receiving corps rather than asked to be the leader themselves — contrasted with [[DeAndre Hopkins]], who Waldman agrees already is that leader.
- 2024-04-30 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] and co-host James Koh (untracked) ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-04-30]]): discussing the Chiefs' selection of Xavier Worthy, both hosts argue Kansas City's approach with Patrick Mahomes is to distribute targets among several 'twos and threes' (Rashee Rice, Marquise Brown, Worthy) behind Travis Kelce rather than establish one true WR1, a strategy Harmon says only an elite-QB offense can afford since 'somebody's going to finish with the most targets' without any single receiver needing a heavy target share to be startable.
- 2024-05-06 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-06]]): Waldman frames 2024 backfield battles as coach-vs-scouting-department value splits rather than clean depth charts -- the Giants' [[Devin Singletary]] as a coaches' hire versus [[Eric Gray]]/[[Tyrone Tracy Jr.]] as front-office-favored change-of-pace types -- and notes even backfields with a clear lead runner, like [[Joe Mixon]] in Houston, still carve out complementary-back work rather than true bell-cow usage.
- 2024-05-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-05-23]]): the outside/vertical X-receiver role is 'objectively the hardest receiver role to play in the NFL' -- it draws more press coverage, more man coverage and more top corners than slot or flanker roles. Harmon uses [[Quentin Johnston]]'s disastrous rookie charting profile as a case study of a prospect whose college profile showed he wasn't ready for that specific role being miscast into it as a rookie, and argues the fix is scheme-driven (manufactured touches, play-action, big-over routes) rather than expecting immediate technical refinement.
- 2024-07-02 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-07-02]]): Harmon and co-host James Koh (untracked) debate why a team would put its 'better' receiver at X instead of an easier motion/slot role, using [[DeVonta Smith]] and [[A.J. Brown]] as the case study -- Harmon argues it's actually correct to put the more explosive, downfield playmaker (Brown) in the motion/big-post role where he can build a head of steam, and the more technical route-runner/press-beater (Smith) on the boundary, since X requires winning press one-on-one while the motion role is comparatively easier to scheme open. The same framework applies to [[Rashee Rice]], whose 47.6% slot/backfield usage as a rookie -- versus 83.6% outside in college -- was a deliberate scheme simplification once Kansas City saw he couldn't consistently win press reps.
- 2024-08-08 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-08]]): Harmon expands his 'sacrificial X' framework — the outside receiver whose job is clearing space and doing thankless dirty work rather than earning targets. [[Alec Pierce]] is the poster boy and arguably a net negative because he neither separates nor wins contested catches; [[Romeo Doubs]] is a tier above because he occasionally wins; [[Jalen Hyatt]] is only 'sacrificial adjacent' because he is rail thin and does not block, unlike true sacrificial Xs. The payoff is elsewhere in the room: a real sacrificial X lets Philadelphia move [[DeVonta Smith]] into the slot and motion [[A.J. Brown]], lets the Giants deploy [[Malik Nabers]] from slot and flanker, and keeps [[Calvin Ridley]] off the line in Tennessee. Harmon's recurring point is that the identity of a team's third or fourth receiver dictates how freely the star can be aligned.
- 2024-08-29 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-29]]): the episode repeatedly evaluates receivers by whether a roster can afford the role catering they require. Harmon's phrase for Kansas City is that [[Kadarius Toney]] made no sense 'from a role catering perspective' — the Chiefs already have to scheme [[Xavier Worthy]] away from press and X-receiver routes, did the same for [[Rashee Rice]], and cannot carry another designed-touch player who cannot simply line up and win. He also flags roster redundancy as a distinct problem: teams stack 'samey samey' profiles, as New England did and as Kansas City now has with [[JuJu Smith-Schuster]] and Rice, and Houston's crowded bottom of the depth chart pushed out [[Noah Brown]]. The positive version is Detroit's, which co-host James Koh (not a tracked expert) praises: rather than betting on one WR2, assemble several hyper-specific role players — [[Tim Patrick]], Allen Robinson, Donovan Peoples-Jones — around [[Amon-Ra St. Brown]] and the tight end.
- 2024-09-02 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-02]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the 49ers backfield is Waldman's cleanest example of a role that is valuable but not held. Kyle Shanahan is a perfectionist who will bench a back for missing one hole on one play and go find another — [[Jordan Mason]] is the guy for now, but a fumble or two bad reads puts [[Isaac Guerendo]] in, and after that the search runs to the practice squad and waiver wire. The fantasy implication Waldman draws is that you do not draft into a replaceable role, you rent it: if you do not have [[Christian McCaffrey]], play the waiver wire and take the one or two good weeks from whoever holds the job. Untracked co-host Bob Harris extended it to handcuff pricing generally, naming [[Alexander Mattison]] as a case where managers paid a handcuff premium for an assumed plug-and-play role that never materialized.
