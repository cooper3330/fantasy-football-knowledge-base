# Right for the Wrong Reasons

## Definition


## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-10-31 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-31 (Tapping Out, Archetypes and Modeling)]]): co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) offered his single strongest trust signal in an analyst. Everyone eventually says 'I was wrong but my process was sound' — but by definition good process produces bad outcomes about as often as bad process produces good ones, so an honest analyst should occasionally say 'I was right about this player and my process was terrible, I got lucky and it bailed me out'. Almost nobody does, because the mind is motivated to justify itself: we interrogate misses rigorously and never audit hits at all. He gave his own version — had [[Drake London]] outproduced [[Zay Flowers]] purely on fluky gadget production, he would want to say he was right for the wrong reasons. Seeing an analyst do this signals a bundle of related traits — humility, curiosity, and wanting to be right rather than wanting to be perceived as right — which Harstad thinks predicts better long-run outcomes. Waldman agreed and cited his own case: he had [[Keenan Allen]] and [[DeAndre Hopkins]] rated highly in that class, but not for the right reasons, which ultimately drove one of two rewrites of his receiver evaluation process over 12 years.
- 2025-09-29 — According to Bob Harris (co-host, NOT a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-09-29]]: he avoided both [[A.J. Brown]] and [[Ladd McConkey]] in the mid-second round and drafted running backs instead — but explicitly says he was 'right for all the wrong reasons.' His actual reasoning was discomfort with the price and an expectation of a run-heavier Chargers offense; what actually happened was [[Keenan Allen]] signing after most drafts and [[Quentin Johnston]] learning to catch, neither of which he foresaw. [[Matt Waldman]] draws the transferable lesson alongside him: players do get coached and work on weaknesses over an offseason, so a fade based on a known flaw can decay without warning.
- 2025-11-13 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-11-13 (Paper Champions and All-Timers Across Eras)]]): untracked co-host Adam Harstad's line is that predictions are very easy as long as you get to decide how to grade them after they have already run their course — and that this is exactly how the draft analyst game works. Waldman agrees and extends it: an analyst ranks a back eighth, then points at one true positive sentence in the blurb and claims a great call, and Waldman admits he did this himself with David Johnson. He draws a distinction he considers legitimate versus illegitimate: saying 'I flagged that this fit or this team context could unlock him' is honest hedging, while spending an entire evaluation trashing a player and later citing a single throwaway compliment is not. Both frame retroactive self-grading as the industry's default accountability failure.
