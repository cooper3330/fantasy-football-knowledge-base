## Expert Takes
<!-- Attributed, dated bullets on how specific experts apply/discuss this concept -->
- 2024-11-22 — According to Adam Harstad, untracked *Film and Theory* co-host ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-22]]) [Dynasty]: Harstad's thesis is that waivers barely matter, arrived at by asking what the world would look like if they did — top dynasty and redraft rosters would be full of waiver gems, and they are not. He tracks acquisition source on a spreadsheet and about 5% of his player starts come from waiver adds despite being historically strong there, and he puts 80-90% of a team's fate as settled by the end of the draft. Screening the top 100 by FantasyCalc dynasty consensus value, only about 11-12 were plausibly on waivers at some point; some ([[Tyreek Hill]], [[Puka Nacua]]) were unpredictable, but two archetypes repeat: **highly drafted backup quarterbacks** and **later-round backup running backs the organization visibly supports**. Running backs spike harder and do not need to be good to produce; quarterbacks carry a multi-year holding cost, which he mitigates by clearing defenses and adding backup QBs once his season is over. His governing line: players are on waivers because we already have plenty of reasons to think they suck, so what you need is a reason to think you are wrong — and organizational behavior is that reason. [[Matt Waldman]] largely agreed while pushing back on the lottery-ticket enthusiasm, and added IDP archetypes Harstad's model misses (aging pass rushers who can still take everyone's lunch money behind bad offensive line play, tackle-heavy safeties on bad defenses, rotating cornerbacks) plus his own skill-position tells: praise about route-running detail or a back getting the yards the play asked for, rather than highlights. Both cited snap counts, roster-bubble reporting and unprompted mentions by a coach or quarterback (Dan Campbell on [[David Montgomery]]) as the strongest signals, and Harstad noted contrasting successful extremes — Jordan McNamara rostering 13-14 running backs versus Dan Hindery rostering 13 receivers — as evidence there is no one-size-fits-all rule, only edges moving you from 50% to 55%.
- 2025-08-21 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-21]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: he defined the super-deep sleeper archetype he has run for nearly two decades — players you should explicitly NOT draft in a 12-team league, nobody inside the top 200 (really not even the top 160), meant as dynasty stashes and as names to recognize before your leaguemates when they start getting bandied about in-season. Hits are not the point: picking outside the top 200, either you miss most of your calls or you are cheating. He also flagged the archetype's practical limit — [[Tahj Brooks]] would be the number one waiver add everywhere the moment [[Chase Brown]] got hurt, so the edge is not really in the obvious injury case but in the intermediate ground where a starter is dealing with something the team is not describing. Guest Jake Ciely (NOT a tracked expert) noted last year's version produced [[Bo Nix]] and [[Jordan Mason]], which he graded as one and a half hits out of five.
- 2025-09-09 — According to [[Chris Harris]] and guest Jake Ciely of The Athletic (not a tracked expert) ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-09]]): league depth should change what archetype you chase. Ciely's rule for shallow leagues is that you want a potential top-20-25 running back, not bench depth — 'I don't want somebody that's getting a third of a touch' — because the difference-maker is what is hard to acquire; a third-of-the-touches back like [[Bhayshul Tuten]] is a deep shot in a 10-team league. In deeper leagues the calculus flips toward stashes like [[LeQuint Allen]] at 1% rostered, where Harris's standard is 'their teams kind of did' use them, so you buy the option and see. Harris also enforces a structural rule for the segment: he lists only players rostered in a third of Yahoo leagues or fewer, and refuses drop advice because it depends on your roster, who else is available and what other teams hold. Ciely's column instead gives graded 'worry factors' rather than drop calls.
- 2025-09-23 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-09-23]]): two rules governed the Week 4 waiver segment with guest SCOTT FISH (Fantasy Cares, NOT tracked). First, positional scarcity beats raw upside — Fish: 'running backs are just so much harder to come by generally on the waiver wire,' and Harris: in a neutral scenario 'you've got to add the running back, even if you're just going to trade him,' which is why [[Trey Benson]] beat [[Chris Godwin]] and why both lists were stacked with backs. Second, receiver adds are structurally low-hit-rate: 'there's 700 receivers... you can't get consistent contribution from [[Justin Jefferson]], you think you're going to get it from [[Tre Tucker]]? This is just the league now.' A corollary Harris applied repeatedly is to distrust a single long run — 'the one I wouldn't be adding is [[Jeremy McNichols]], based on one long run' — and instead buy defined roles and rising snap/route shares.
- 2025-11-04 — According to Jeff Erickson of RotoWire, a guest and not a tracked expert ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-11-04]]): in a bye-heavy, thin waiver week the goal is finding Mr. Right Now rather than Mr. Right — he will take a bog-average player like [[Parker Washington]] who has inherited undeniable volume over a better player in a smaller role. [[Chris Harris]] frames his own list from the opposite direction: he wants to add pieces of good offenses, which is why [[Tory Horton]] tops his list even though he expects one or two catches and to drop him in a couple of weeks. Both agree the two lists collapse into the same test when a start is actually required — Harris says any receiver with quote-unquote guaranteed double-digit targets belongs in a lineup, but you cannot confirm the guarantee until every other body is ruled out, and Erickson concedes several of his adds are emergency break-glass moves only.
- 2025-11-11 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-11-11]]): by November the waiver wire changes character - 'we don't have a lot of surprises anymore about situations', and apple carts get overturned in September, not November, so the weekly list is mostly plug-and-plays of last resort rather than league winners. Guest **Brandon Funston** (The Athletic, not a tracked expert) frames the goal by roster state: if you are playoff-bound you circle the wagons, back up your best assets, identify potential pitfalls and spackle over them - handcuff your starting back, add the backup receiver behind an injury-prone starter, and now that bye weeks are ending, stash a second quarterback you would feel fine actually starting regardless of matchup. If you are fighting to make the playoffs you are instead hunting streamers and plug-and-plays week to week. Harris agreed that is the real mission, and that his list serves 'a different audience' - hence Week 11 adds like [[Blake Corum]] and [[Brian Robinson Jr.]] being framed as insurance rather than starters.
