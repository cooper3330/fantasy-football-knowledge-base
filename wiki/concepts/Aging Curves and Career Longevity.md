# Aging Curves and Career Longevity

## Definition

How long NFL players remain productive, and how that has changed. Central to
[[Dynasty]] valuation: if productive careers are ending earlier than they used
to, historical age curves overvalue older players.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-14 — Presented by **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-14]] [Dynasty]: a sharp, league-wide collapse in productive seasons by older players. Six-year buckets, share of all 1,000-yard receiving seasons produced by players 30+:

  | Period | Share |
  |---|---|
  | 1993–1998 | 33.0% |
  | 1999–2004 | 33.3% |
  | 2005–2010 | 33.1% |
  | 2011–2016 | 20.4% |
  | 2017–2022 | **7.7%** |

  Harstad stressed the first three buckets are "remarkably stable" — that kind of stability over 18 years usually describes a ground truth of the league — making the recent drop more striking. Raw counts fell from ~40 such seasons per bucket to **10**, and several of those 10 belong to one player (Travis Kelce). The pattern repeats elsewhere: 1,000-yard rushing seasons by backs 27+ fell from 43.4% (2011–16) to 15.2% (2017–22); linebackers with 120+ tackles at 30+ fell to 9.7%; offensive linemen starting 14+ games fell from 54/45/43 to 27/24. Pass rushers with 10+ sacks showed **no** comparable decline.

- 2023-12-14 — **Harstad** (same episode): explicitly says he does *not* know the cause, and walked through explanations he had tested and rejected. The 2011 CBA rookie-wage cap (cheap rookies displacing fringe veterans) fails on two counts — the timing differs by position, and squeezed-out roster-fringe veterans were never posting 1,000-yard seasons anyway. "College players arrive more pro-ready" fails because it predicts a gradual shift, not a sudden drop, and because offensive line — where incoming quality is reportedly *worse* — saw the earliest, sharpest decline.

- 2023-12-14 — **Harstad** (same episode), working hypothesis reached live and explicitly untested: careers may be shortening because of rising talent on the *opposing* side of the ball rather than at a player's own position. Faster, more athletic cornerbacks end receiver careers; more athletic defensive linemen end offensive line careers. Predicts declines should show up at safety (facing improved TEs/QBs) but not at defensive end. Harstad flagged this as needing to "survive contact with the enemy."

- 2023-12-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-14]]) [Dynasty]: his first instinct is concussion awareness and rule changes — not that players retire *citing* concussions, but that head-injury history becomes one factor among several (a younger player pushing for the job, an unsatisfying contract offer) that makes extending a career past 30 not worth it. Framed it as "I'm not lengthening my career to get that extra bit of money because of concussions as a factor." Also argued spread offenses cut both ways: they help receivers develop technique earlier while devaluing the middle-of-field processing that sustained older running backs, linebackers, and interior linemen. Suggested the 2023–24 shift back toward 12/13 personnel could produce a partial correction in the next data bucket.

- 2023-12-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]): declines to apply the age discount to elite players. Asked whether [[Christian McCaffrey]] is the RB1 overall for 2024, he answers yes without qualification — "I don't care if he's going to turn 28, he's the top one" — arguing from what McCaffrey did to San Francisco's offense on arrival rather than from any age curve. A useful boundary on the Harstad data four days earlier: the collapse is a population-level fact about *replacement-level* aging, and neither host treats it as a reason to fade an individual top-of-position player. Waldman did raise decline as a live possibility for [[Austin Ekeler]] in the same episode, floating [[James Cook]] as a successor archetype.

- 2024-02-02 — **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert)
  on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-02]], from a Hall of Fame
  discussion, not a fantasy one — logged here because the reasoning is
  general-purpose career-value evaluation: he weighs career value against a
  **league-average baseline over the length of a career**, not a
  replacement-level baseline, because a replacement baseline mechanically
  favors longevity over peak. Applied position-specifically: running back and
  middle linebacker are the two positions where a short career is "most
  forgivable," because the wear and tear at both positions makes long
  careers structurally rare — "middle linebackers are the running backs of
  the defense." Consistent with, and a plain-language restatement of, why
  this page already treats RB rushing-season decline at 27+ as expected
  background rate rather than a red flag on any individual back.

- 2024-02-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-13]]): a much softer age flag than Waldman's December dismissal of it, applied to [[Christian McCaffrey]] heading into 2025 rather than 2024 — McCaffrey is "entering the era of, the kind of window of running backs where you start to get really nervous." Not a ranking or start/sit call, just the first sign this show is tracking the same age-curve question on him that this page's other entries treat as a population-level pattern for running backs generally.

- 2024-03-01 — **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-01]] [Dynasty]: presents a fuller version of the model behind his December aging-curve findings. The population-level "age curve" (ramp, peak at 26 for RBs, decline) is an *ecological fallacy* — true of the group average but rarely descriptive of an individual career, which more often looks flat-to-random until a sudden "age cliff." Tested this using fantasy value (points per game over a calculated replacement baseline, times games played) over a player's final two fantasy-relevant seasons: if careers were curve-shaped, players should almost always be worse in their final relevant year than their second-to-last. At receiver, 50% of players were actually *better* in their final year — consistent with a flat-then-cliff model rather than a gradual decline. At running back it's closer to 33–40% — still evidence against a pure curve, but enough of a gap from 50% that Harstad reads it as real evidence RBs do decline somewhat before falling off the cliff. His hypothesis for the RB/WR difference: RB production is driven more by role, receiver production more by talent, so a RB whose talent has already dipped below threshold can still produce if his role holds up, masking the decline as noise. Bottom line: be wary of a free-agent RB coming off a visible decline year, but don't assume the decline is real — 30–40% of the time it's just noise before a fully-capable season. Applied live to [[Derrick Henry]], [[Josh Jacobs]], [[Tony Pollard]] and [[Austin Ekeler]], and to [[DeAndre Hopkins]] as a still-viable (non-cliff) example against a retired Andre Johnson as an already-over-the-cliff one.

- 2024-03-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], solo cast on the 2024 WR draft class ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-16]]): a receiver-specific career-longevity study, sampling drafted wide receivers 2008–2017. Of 317 receivers drafted in that window, only 13% (40 players) earned at least three years of top-36 fantasy-scoring production — with injury as the dominant reason the other 87% never got the practice time and roster security to develop; the average NFL career is about three years. Extrapolated to recent classes, Waldman estimates roughly 4.5 receivers per class reach 3+ years of top-36 production within their first four seasons, with a typical starter-production span of 3.5–6 years across a 5–12-year career — and even most receivers who do sustain multi-year starter production rarely reach top-24/top-12-level production for more than about a year of it. Cites Ryan Riddle's Bleacher Report piece on career brevity as essential reading. Separately relays a finding **Adam Harstad** (not a tracked expert) raised on their *Film and Theory* podcast: wide receiver career spans as starters are shrinking, which Waldman ties to the same underlying economics he describes elsewhere here — cheaper, younger replacement talent making it easier for front offices to churn the position rather than pay a proven veteran (his live example: [[Tee Higgins]] weighing a second contract/trade market against staying with [[Joe Burrow]] and [[Ja'Marr Chase]] in Cincinnati).


- 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-19]] [Dynasty]: three live "is the cliff coming" cases from the same episode. On [[Keenan Allen]] (turning 32 in 2024, fresh off arguably his best season): "the cliff comes fast and it comes suddenly," and an extreme pessimist could point to a new system/QB as the trigger, but Waldman is "a little more of an optimist," expecting quick rapport with [[Caleb Williams]]. On [[Derrick Henry]] (30, to Baltimore): flags he had the 7th-fastest tracked speed in the NFL last season and one of his best breakaway-run rates, arguing the tape shows "this guy hasn't lost anything" beyond playing behind a mediocre Tennessee offensive line. On [[Calvin Ridley]] (heading into his age-30 season, on a market-topping new contract): skeptical he's a mid-30s-caliber player, expecting only "a year or two left" at a high level.

- 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-19]]: a charted, more granular read on [[Keenan Allen]] (age 32) than Waldman's same-day "no decline" take — Harmon's numbers show real physical erosion (overall success rate vs. man down to 71.9% from a 76.6-77.6% career peak), but frames it as the Chargers proactively narrowing his route diet toward high-percentage underneath routes (dig/curl/slant/screen/flat) where he still grades elite, rather than a player in freefall. Read together: both experts agree Allen isn't washed, but Harmon's version locates the "why" in a deliberate, age-driven role change rather than sustained talent.
- 2024-05-14 — According to Chris Long ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-05-14]]), former NFL defensive end, not a tracked expert: pushed back on treating running back and defensive lineman decline as purely age-based. Long says physical decline for pass rushers is rarely gradual — 'there's different levels of I don't got it anymore' — and argues the more predictive signal is injury timing (he cites his own back-to-back injuries at 27-28 as the real inflection point, not turning 30), plus playing style and mileage: bruising, contact-seeking runners like Steven Jackson 'ran themselves off a cliff' early, while finesse/technique-reliant players can play into their late 30s. He also flags scheme and program history — players who go from physical college programs into Bill Belichick's system get run into the ground — and offers a truck analogy: two identical trucks age totally differently depending on whether they were 'garage kept' or driven on salted roads and neglected, i.e. usage and injury history matter more than raw age. He explicitly reiterated the fantasy rule of not drafting a running back age 29-30-plus, while noting it's a generalization that individual circumstances can override.
- 2024-05-17 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-17]]) [Dynasty]: Angelo ties several rookie evaluations to veteran aging curves — he's targeting [[Michael Penix Jr.]] in trade-ups specifically because [[Kirk Cousins]] will be 36 and coming off an Achilles rupture with maybe two years left, making a rookie QB2 unusually valuable in Superflex; and he's reaching for [[Jaylen Wright]] in Miami because [[Raheem Mostert]] (32 by next season) and aging backs like [[James Connor]] (30, [[Trey Benson]]'s mentor in Arizona) are on borrowed time, opening a clear runway for the rookies behind them.
- 2024-05-20 — According to guest Jagger May (Football Guys, untracked) and [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-05-20]]): discussing [[Matthew Stafford]]'s possible extension into his late 30s, both flagged his back and shoulder injury history and two ACL tears as real durability risk, with Waldman noting Stafford lacks Tom Brady's training-regimen extremes or Brady's offensive lines. Separately, May applied the same lens to [[James Connor]] — good for a long time, but old now and never durable even when young — arguing his role should be read as a bridge for a rookie rather than a continuing building block.
- 2024-07-12 — Discussing DeAndre Hopkins, [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-12]]) said older WRs (year 10+) taken in the middle rounds are actually good value bets due to survivorship bias -- if a WR is still being drafted as a WR3/4 that late into his career, he's probably still good, and evaluators often predict a decline that doesn't materialize for technique-based route runners like Hopkins (compared to Anquan Boldin) as opposed to speed-dependent players who fall off a cliff.
- 2024-07-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-15]]): Discussing whether [[Travis Kelce]] sustains top-three TE fantasy value into his mid-30s, Waldman said elite route runners who understand how to sell a break and get downhill quickly out of it can keep producing well past the point their athletic testing declines, pointing to historical tight ends who aged well on skill (route craft, releasing from a break) rather than raw speed as the template.
- 2024-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-06]]): projecting older receivers is the hardest thing he does with the data, and he says it is a lesson he has to relearn constantly. The pattern he warns against is treating a small dip as noise — he waved off Allen Robinson going from 77% to 74-75% vs man in his final Chicago year and was wrong, and he flags the same shape now in [[Keenan Allen]] (77% to 72%) and [[Cooper Kupp]] (70.6% to 65.2%). His rule of thumb: with older receivers the line does not go up-down-up-down; once it starts to move it is either a steady decline or a cliff. He applies the same framing to [[Tyler Lockett]], whose best season may have been 2022 immediately before the step back.
- 2024-09-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-09]]): 'washed' is close to unusable as an analytical term. Waldman says he took the question to multiple scouts and athletic trainers he knows because he kept hearing it about running backs, some receivers and now [[Mark Andrews]], and was told it is the most ridiculous assessment out there — after 21 years of scouting, he argues it is difficult enough to discern how fast and explosive a player is at his peak, except in obvious cases, and harder still to discern what has been lost. His examples of confidently wrong calls: [[David Montgomery]] declared too slow for the NFL off one game, and [[Nick Chubb]] written off after his three-ligament tear at Georgia, a case Waldman studied on more tape than any player he has ever watched. On [[Joe Mixon]] he adds the counter-frame that 28-29 is generally the athletic prime, and that decline at that age is more plausibly slower recovery than lost ability.
- 2024-09-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-11]]): Waldman argued heavy college workload should not scare evaluators off a prospect. Discussing [[Tahj Brooks]]'s 290 carries in a season — more than [[Derrick Henry]]'s 280 the same year — he said his long look at backs like Cedric Benson, Ray Rice, Frank Gore, Adrian Peterson and Matt Forte shows the mileage was not a factor for any of them. His read is that carrying that load is itself the evidence: backs who can tote the ball at that volume have proven they do not wear down or wear out. He noted the identical question dogged [[Jonathan Taylor]] coming out as a primary ball carrier from his freshman season.
