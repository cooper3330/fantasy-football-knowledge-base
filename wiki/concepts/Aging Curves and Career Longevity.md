# Aging Curves and Career Longevity

## Definition

How long NFL players remain productive, and how that has changed. Central to
[[Dynasty]] valuation: if productive careers are ending earlier than they used
to, historical age curves overvalue older players.

## Expert Takes

- 2023-12-14 — Presented by **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-14]] [Dynasty]: a sharp, league-wide collapse in productive seasons by older players. Six-year buckets, share of all 1,000-yard receiving seasons produced by players 30+:

  | Period | Share |
  |---|---|
  | 1993–1998 | 33.0% |
  | 1999–2004 | 33.3% |
  | 2005–2010 | 33.1% |
  | 2011–2016 | 20.4% |
  | 2017–2022 | **7.7%** |

  Harstad stressed the first three buckets are "remarkably stable" — that kind of stability over 18 years usually describes a ground truth of the league — making the recent drop more striking. Raw counts fell from ~40 such seasons per bucket to **10**, and several of those 10 belong to one player (Travis Kelce). The pattern repeats elsewhere: 1,000-yard rushing seasons by backs 27+ fell from 43.4% (2011–16) to 15.2% (2017–22); linebackers with 120+ tackles at 30+ fell to 9.7%; offensive linemen starting 14+ games fell from 54/45/43 to 27/24. Pass rushers with 10+ sacks showed **no** comparable decline.

- 2023-12-14 — **Harstad** (same episode): explicitly says he does *not* know the cause, and walked through explanations he had tested and rejected. The 2011 CBA rookie-wage cap (cheap rookies displacing fringe veterans) fails on two counts — the timing differs by position, and squeezed-out roster-fringe veterans were never posting 1,000-yard seasons anyway. "College players arrive more pro-ready" fails because it predicts a gradual shift, not a sudden drop, and because offensive line — where incoming quality is reportedly *worse* — saw the earliest, sharpest decline.

- 2023-12-14 — **Harstad** (same episode), working hypothesis reached live and explicitly untested: careers may be shortening because of rising talent on the *opposing* side of the ball rather than at a player's own position. Faster, more athletic cornerbacks end receiver careers; more athletic defensive linemen end offensive line careers. Predicts declines should show up at safety (facing improved TEs/QBs) but not at defensive end. Harstad flagged this as needing to "survive contact with the enemy."

- 2023-12-14 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-14]]) [Dynasty]: his first instinct is concussion awareness and rule changes — not that players retire *citing* concussions, but that head-injury history becomes one factor among several (a younger player pushing for the job, an unsatisfying contract offer) that makes extending a career past 30 not worth it. Framed it as "I'm not lengthening my career to get that extra bit of money because of concussions as a factor." Also argued spread offenses cut both ways: they help receivers develop technique earlier while devaluing the middle-of-field processing that sustained older running backs, linebackers, and interior linemen. Suggested the 2023–24 shift back toward 12/13 personnel could produce a partial correction in the next data bucket.

- 2023-12-18 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2023-12-18]]): declines to apply the age discount to elite players. Asked whether [[Christian McCaffrey]] is the RB1 overall for 2024, he answers yes without qualification — "I don't care if he's going to turn 28, he's the top one" — arguing from what McCaffrey did to San Francisco's offense on arrival rather than from any age curve. A useful boundary on the Harstad data four days earlier: the collapse is a population-level fact about *replacement-level* aging, and neither host treats it as a reason to fade an individual top-of-position player. Waldman did raise decline as a live possibility for [[Austin Ekeler]] in the same episode, floating [[James Cook]] as a successor archetype.

- 2024-02-02 — **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert)
  on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-02-02]], from a Hall of Fame
  discussion, not a fantasy one — logged here because the reasoning is
  general-purpose career-value evaluation: he weighs career value against a
  **league-average baseline over the length of a career**, not a

- 2024-02-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-02-13]]): a much softer age flag than Waldman's December dismissal of it, applied to [[Christian McCaffrey]] heading into 2025 rather than 2024 — McCaffrey is "entering the era of, the kind of window of running backs where you start to get really nervous." Not a ranking or start/sit call, just the first sign this show is tracking the same age-curve question on him that this page's other entries treat as a population-level pattern for running backs generally.

- 2024-03-01 — **Adam Harstad** (Footballguys, guest — not a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-01]] [Dynasty]: presents a fuller version of the model behind his December aging-curve findings. The population-level "age curve" (ramp, peak at 26 for RBs, decline) is an *ecological fallacy* — true of the group average but rarely descriptive of an individual career, which more often looks flat-to-random until a sudden "age cliff." Tested this using fantasy value (points per game over a calculated replacement baseline, times games played) over a player's final two fantasy-relevant seasons: if careers were curve-shaped, players should almost always be worse in their final relevant year than their second-to-last. At receiver, 50% of players were actually *better* in their final year — consistent with a flat-then-cliff model rather than a gradual decline. At running back it's closer to 33–40% — still evidence against a pure curve, but enough of a gap from 50% that Harstad reads it as real evidence RBs do decline somewhat before falling off the cliff. His hypothesis for the RB/WR difference: RB production is driven more by role, receiver production more by talent, so a RB whose talent has already dipped below threshold can still produce if his role holds up, masking the decline as noise. Bottom line: be wary of a free-agent RB coming off a visible decline year, but don't assume the decline is real — 30–40% of the time it's just noise before a fully-capable season. Applied live to [[Derrick Henry]], [[Josh Jacobs]], [[Tony Pollard]] and [[Austin Ekeler]], and to [[DeAndre Hopkins]] as a still-viable (non-cliff) example against a retired Andre Johnson as an already-over-the-cliff one.

- 2024-03-16 — According to [[Matt Waldman]], solo cast on the 2024 WR draft class ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-16]]): a receiver-specific career-longevity study, sampling drafted wide receivers 2008–2017. Of 317 receivers drafted in that window, only 13% (40 players) earned at least three years of top-36 fantasy-scoring production — with injury as the dominant reason the other 87% never got the practice time and roster security to develop; the average NFL career is about three years. Extrapolated to recent classes, Waldman estimates roughly 4.5 receivers per class reach 3+ years of top-36 production within their first four seasons, with a typical starter-production span of 3.5–6 years across a 5–12-year career — and even most receivers who do sustain multi-year starter production rarely reach top-24/top-12-level production for more than about a year of it. Cites Ryan Riddle's Bleacher Report piece on career brevity as essential reading. Separately relays a finding **Adam Harstad** (not a tracked expert) raised on their *Film and Theory* podcast: wide receiver career spans as starters are shrinking, which Waldman ties to the same underlying economics he describes elsewhere here — cheaper, younger replacement talent making it easier for front offices to churn the position rather than pay a proven veteran (his live example: [[Tee Higgins]] weighing a second contract/trade market against staying with [[Joe Burrow]] and [[Ja'Marr Chase]] in Cincinnati).


- 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-03-19]] [Dynasty]: three live "is the cliff coming" cases from the same episode. On [[Keenan Allen]] (turning 32 in 2024, fresh off arguably his best season): "the cliff comes fast and it comes suddenly," and an extreme pessimist could point to a new system/QB as the trigger, but Waldman is "a little more of an optimist," expecting quick rapport with [[Caleb Williams]]. On [[Derrick Henry]] (30, to Baltimore): flags he had the 7th-fastest tracked speed in the NFL last season and one of his best breakaway-run rates, arguing the tape shows "this guy hasn't lost anything" beyond playing behind a mediocre Tennessee offensive line. - 2024-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-03-19]]: a charted, more granular read on [[Keenan Allen]] (age 32) than Waldman's same-day "no decline" take — Harmon's numbers show real physical erosion (overall success rate vs. man down to 71.9% from a 76.6-77.6% career peak), but frames it as the Chargers proactively narrowing his route diet toward high-percentage underneath routes (dig/curl/slant/screen/flat) where he still grades elite, rather than a player in freefall. Read together: both experts agree Allen isn't washed, but Harmon's version locates the "why" in a deliberate, age-driven role change rather than sustained talent.
- 2024-05-14 — According to Chris Long ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-05-14]]), former NFL defensive end, not a tracked expert: pushed back on treating running back and defensive lineman decline as purely age-based. Long says physical decline for pass rushers is rarely gradual — 'there's different levels of I don't got it anymore' — and argues the more predictive signal is injury timing (he cites his own back-to-back injuries at 27-28 as the real inflection point, not turning 30), plus playing style and mileage: bruising, contact-seeking runners like Steven Jackson 'ran themselves off a cliff' early, while finesse/technique-reliant players can play into their late 30s. He also flags scheme and program history — players who go from physical college programs into Bill Belichick's system get run into the ground — and offers a truck analogy: two identical trucks age totally differently depending on whether they were 'garage kept' or driven on salted roads and neglected, i.e. usage and injury history matter more than raw age. He explicitly reiterated the fantasy rule of not drafting a running back age 29-30-plus, while noting it's a generalization that individual circumstances can override.

Separately, May applied the same lens to [[James Connor]] — good for a long time, but old now and never durable even when young — arguing his role should be read as a bridge for a rookie rather than a continuing building block.
- 2024-07-12 — Discussing DeAndre Hopkins, [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-12]]) said older WRs (year 10+) taken in the middle rounds are actually good value bets due to survivorship bias -- if a WR is still being drafted as a WR3/4 that late into his career, he's probably still good, and evaluators often predict a decline that doesn't materialize for technique-based route runners like Hopkins (compared to Anquan Boldin) as opposed to speed-dependent players who fall off a cliff.
- 2024-07-15 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-07-15]]): Discussing whether [[Travis Kelce]] sustains top-three TE fantasy value into his mid-30s, Waldman said elite route runners who understand how to sell a break and get downhill quickly out of it can keep producing well past the point their athletic testing declines, pointing to historical tight ends who aged well on skill (route craft, releasing from a break) rather than raw speed as the template.
- 2024-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-08-06]]): projecting older receivers is the hardest thing he does with the data, and he says it is a lesson he has to relearn constantly. The pattern he warns against is treating a small dip as noise — he waved off Allen Robinson going from 77% to 74-75% vs man in his final Chicago year and was wrong, and he flags the same shape now in [[Keenan Allen]] (77% to 72%) and [[Cooper Kupp]] (70.6% to 65.2%). His rule of thumb: with older receivers the line does not go up-down-up-down; once it starts to move it is either a steady decline or a cliff. He applies the same framing to [[Tyler Lockett]], whose best season may have been 2022 immediately before the step back.
- 2024-09-09 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-09]]): 'washed' is close to unusable as an analytical term. Waldman says he took the question to multiple scouts and athletic trainers he knows because he kept hearing it about running backs, some receivers and now [[Mark Andrews]], and was told it is the most ridiculous assessment out there — after 21 years of scouting, he argues it is difficult enough to discern how fast and explosive a player is at his peak, except in obvious cases, and harder still to discern what has been lost. His examples of confidently wrong calls: [[David Montgomery]] declared too slow for the NFL off one game, and [[Nick Chubb]] written off after his three-ligament tear at Georgia, a case Waldman studied on more tape than any player he has ever watched. On [[Joe Mixon]] he adds the counter-frame that 28-29 is generally the athletic prime, and that decline at that age is more plausibly slower recovery than lost ability.
- 2024-09-11 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-09-11]]): Waldman argued heavy college workload should not scare evaluators off a prospect. Discussing [[Tahj Brooks]]'s 290 carries in a season — more than [[Derrick Henry]]'s 280 the same year — he said his long look at backs like Cedric Benson, Ray Rice, Frank Gore, Adrian Peterson and Matt Forte shows the mileage was not a factor for any of them. His read is that carrying that load is itself the evidence: backs who can tote the ball at that volume have proven they do not wear down or wear out. He noted the identical question dogged [[Jonathan Taylor]] coming out as a primary ball carrier from his freshman season.
- 2024-09-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-09-12]]): using [[Tyler Lockett]] as the case, Harmon argues the fantasy industry collapses a spectrum into a binary — 'there is a difference between a declining player and a washed player,' and analyzing aging receivers is tricky precisely because it is a sliding scale with a big gap between 'done done' and 'not the player they used to be.' The diagnostic he uses is not the snap count but whether the quarterback and staff still trust him in the routes he is given: Lockett played only 52% of snaps yet was targeted on 33% of routes run with all six of his catchable looks first reads. He applies the same logic to [[Stefon Diggs]], conceding a lost step as a vertical separator while pointing to still-strong route success on slants, curls and quick outs.
- 2024-10-15 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-10-15]]): the danger zone for receivers starts once they pass 30, and the way to extend a prime past it is to change role — move from perimeter-dominant number one to inside work and layup routes. He cites [[Stefon Diggs]], 30 and turning 31 in November, doing exactly that successfully in Houston as the clearest current example. The counterweight in the same segment is that the fantasy world pushes older players out too fast: [[Chris Godwin]] (28), [[Terry McLaurin]] (29, whom Harmon calls 'sneaky old') and Diggs were all top-12 fantasy receivers in Week 6 while analysts asked why second- and third-year breakouts had not taken over. Harmon's framing is that Godwin and McLaurin are still in true prime years, and that health explains more of their swings than age does.
- 2024-10-24 — According to co-host Adam Harstad (Footballguys, NOT a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman]]'s show ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-10-24]]) [Dynasty]: people believe in a far more deterministic universe than exists — that careers are curves ascending to a peak and descending. They are not; they are closer to random walks. His examples: Anquan Boldin set his career yardage high as a rookie and never matched it, Cam Newton's first two games were the two highest passing-yardage games of his entire career yet he was plainly a better passer later, Drew Hill had about a thousand receiving yards through age 29 and then became one of the NFL's most productive receivers, and roughly half of the top 50 fantasy receivers of the last 30 to 50 years were better in their last relevant season than the one before it. He ties it to the just-world fallacy. [[Matt Waldman]] adds the bamboo parable — nothing visible for months, then thirty feet in thirty days — and both agree the fantasy profit is in betting on a non-deterministic universe, since everyone pricing the orderly curve is grinding for pennies.
- 2024-11-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-11-05]]): Harmon states his general rule for aging receivers — you never know how the next stage goes, because sometimes the decline stays gentle and steady and other times the player falls straight off the cliff. He applies it in both directions in the same episode: he believes [[Keenan Allen]] is at the cliff after a couple of years of visible decline, while [[Terry McLaurin]] at 29 is producing career bests, with Harmon noting only that they are entering the age zone rather than claiming the cliff is near. McLaurin's case is complicated by his entering the league older than usual, which Harmon treats as a reason to hold the projection loosely rather than as a defense.
- 2024-11-14 — According to Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) on [[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-11-14 (Age Cliffs and Dented Cans)]] [Dynasty]: the 'age curve' is better than the pre-2010 habit of ignoring age entirely, but it is 'completely false' as a description of individual careers. Players improve quickly for a year or two, hit a peak, then fluctuate around that fixed peak until they abruptly fall off a 'cliff' — one year a superstar, the next replacement level or out of the league. Every method (points by age, year-over-year percentage change, counting 250-point seasons) reproduces the same smooth curve because of ecological fallacy: a population relationship need not hold individually. If 32-year-old receivers average 50% of prime output, that can be half producing fully and half producing nothing. Evidence: ~50% of top backs/receivers over 30-40 years were better in their LAST fantasy-relevant season than in the second-to-last, which steady decline cannot explain. Harstad models it as a coin flip weighted more toward tails with age — which is also how a Jerry Rice is possible without being a miracle. [[Matt Waldman]] agreed and added that 'fluctuation' silently absorbs offensive line play, quarterback play, defenses and luck — running back production is skewed by rare 60-80 yard runs, so a cold streak on explosives can look like decline.
- 2024-12-05 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2024-12-05]]): what declines with age is not vacuum athleticism but recovery rate — a player can still perform at a high level once, but takes longer to be ready to do it again, which cascades into looking slower, more worn down and more injury-prone. That reframes the workload question as 'what is this player's sustainable number per game', with the warning sign being a sudden ramp-up rather than a consistently high established load. Adam Harstad (NOT a tracked expert) agreed and pushed it further: steroids don't make you stronger, they make you recover faster so you can train more often; at the top level 'recovery is everything', and it is what separates who is still playing well in December and in the fourth quarter when everyone else is worn down.
- 2024-12-12 — According to guest Patrick Peterson (former NFL cornerback, not a tracked expert) on [[Reception Perception The Show - 2024-12-12]]: 'father time is undefeated' — around year nine a corner loses the athletic mistake-erasers and has to convert to technique and preparation, studying film to be in position before the receiver is, because the receivers keep getting younger, smaller and faster. He applies it directly to the 30-year-old, ninth-year Jalen Ramsey, diagnosing the big play [[Garrett Wilson]] hit as a technique failure — bailing on an angle, keeping his eyes on the quarterback and never relocating the receiver out of his blind spot — a play Peterson says a younger Ramsey never gives up. [[Matt Harmon]] said he will be watching Ramsey's fundamentals for the rest of his career; co-host James Koh (not tracked) countered that Ramsey has always played with a gambler's mentality of maximizing big plays rather than minimizing gains, and the open question is whether that survives at 31 or 32.
- 2025-01-16 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-01-16]]): receiver age cliffs are the single hardest thing to predict and, in his opinion, impossible to predict with Reception Perception — the data does not deliver a clean here, then here, then here descent, because injuries and situation confound it. [[Keenan Allen]] is his example of a player who has hovered at a declined but still usable level for years rather than falling off a cliff, while [[Cooper Kupp]] is the counterexample of a former alpha who has fallen off the map entirely. Harmon noted the Reception Perception database runs back to 2014 and will have ten full years of data after this season, with [[Mike Evans]] and [[Davante Adams]] among the handful of players charted throughout, which is what makes year-by-year jumps and declines legible.

On [[Christian McCaffrey]] he argues the cliff arrives fast once a nagging Achilles is in the picture, and that NFL teams deliberately move on from a player a year early rather than lean on him a year too long — his image is a dragster that only goes up and down the strip so many times before it blows a head gasket. - 2025-02-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-05]]): the [[Cooper Kupp]] situation is the standard shape of how it ends for most veteran receivers, and especially with this franchise — once a team publicly signals it is moving on, the player almost always ends up released rather than traded, because the team has no leverage. Harmon's charting shows the decline is usually role-shaped rather than total: Kupp's zone-coverage excellence largely survived while his man and press wins evaporated (91.4% vs zone in 2021 down to a 65.2% man score in 2023), which narrows an every-position receiver into a pure interior role. He also frames the front-office side — Sean McVay falls in and out of love harder than anyone, citing [[Jared Goff]], Joe Noteboom and Cam Akers, calling him Belichickian but with better PR. [[DeAndre Hopkins]]' 36.4% postseason route share in Kansas City is the same story a year further along.
- 2025-02-10 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-02-10]]): the age cliff is better modelled as a staircase than a cliff — for some players it is 'one step where you go, oh man, I almost fell down that', and for others it is a whole flight. He applies the gentler version to [[Davante Adams]] (probably a step or two, which keeps him a bargain) and argues the market systematically overpays for the fear: [[Travis Kelce]] will be perennially underpriced because drafters keep waiting for him to fall off completely, likely dropping outside experts' top 10-12 tight ends, and Waldman regards that as a mistake even while conceding the dominant-receiver-playing-tight-end version is gone. Bob Harris (Footballguys, not tracked) jokes that people keep waiting for his own cliff and missed it 30 years ago.
- 2025-02-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-02-19]]): the veterans-on-the-move class is a lesson in how quickly decline arrives and how a player's post-football options shape it. Harmon says [[Tyler Lockett]] declined for two years and that once it burned out for him it burned out fast, and that a thriving second career — Lockett's Seattle real estate business — makes the Allen Robinson path of hanging on at the bottom of playoff rosters less appealing, so retirement is a live outcome distinct from decline. - 2025-03-05 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-05]]): the aging curve is the hardest thing to predict at wide receiver — some players fall off a cliff, others decline steadily. - 2025-03-12 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-12]]): Harmon repeats that age curves and age cliffs are the single hardest thing to analyze at wide receiver even with Reception Perception data — some players fall off a cliff and some decline gracefully, and the difference is not reliably visible in advance. His cautionary case is [[Allen Robinson]], whose final Chicago season showed a drop from average to poor success rate vs zone that Harmon waved off as an artifact of role, only for Robinson to be unable to get open the very next season in the same Rams X job. Antonio Brown is raised as the counterexample of astronomic peaks with no longevity.
- 2025-03-13 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-13]]): three late-career case studies. [[Cooper Kupp]], entering his age-32 season, is a declining slot-only player whose man-coverage separation has gone even as red-zone quickness remains. [[DeAndre Hopkins]], turning 33, charted better than Harmon expected but at 65.3% vs man and 66.7% vs press — both below the 50th percentile — and is now a slow, long-developing separator who only wins on a narrow route tree. - 2025-03-19 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-19]]): Harmon says spotting the aging cliff is genuinely difficult, but that in his charting the tell is a specific one — the loss of speed and explosiveness shows up first on big in-cuts over the middle (slants, digs, posts), while free-release out-breaking routes toward the sideline survive longest. [[Cooper Kupp]]'s 2024 is his example: in-breakers gone, out/flat still fine. A second, non-charting signal he weights heavily is organizational behavior — the team that knows a player best pushing him out ruthlessly (the Rams with Kupp) and Rams-offshoot front offices with a positional need showing no interest is, to Harmon, information a signing team should not ignore. He adds that 2024 was 'not a great year of older receivers' generally, and that Kupp was not even the worst veteran profile he charted.
- 2025-03-21 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-21]]): Harmon restates his rule that a major injury is what pushes an already-declining veteran receiver over the cliff — a player in mild decline can hold on, but the catastrophic injury is the accelerant. He applies it to [[Stefon Diggs]], 31 and turning 32, coming off a non-contact Week 8 ACL tear with talk he may not be ready for Week 1: the fact that Diggs is unsigned and visiting a team picking fourth is itself the market's verdict on the medicals. Harmon also notes a shared pattern among the older receivers he charted — Diggs, [[Cooper Kupp]] and [[DeAndre Hopkins]] all grade better on out-breaking routes than working back toward the middle, which is problematic because most NFL concepts and primary-target usage live over the middle in the quarterback's vision. James Koh (co-host, not tracked) adds that PUP-list starts for 32-year-old receivers have poor history.
- 2025-03-28 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-03-28]]): forecasting aging receivers is the single hardest thing to do even with Reception Perception charting, and major injuries are what tip a gradual decline into a cliff. He applies it to [[Stefon Diggs]] at 31 coming off a Week 8 torn ACL — conceding Diggs was already declining in 2023 and still declining, while insisting the 2024 Houston tape (71.6% success versus man, 71.4% versus press, 67th percentile) shows the decline has not yet forced him into a slot-only role. Harmon credits co-host James Koh (not tracked) as the one who leads this point on the show, and pairs the physical curve with a soft-factor argument that veteran leadership retains value in a young room even as athletic output falls.
- 2025-05-19 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-05-19]]): tight ends age more slowly than other skill positions because their value rests on route-running acumen, zone reading and finding the open spot rather than on athleticism — he cites Tony Gonzalez remaining a high-volume underneath target after he slowed, Rob Gronkowski delivering when healthy in Tampa, Antonio Gates producing after foot surgeries, and Travis Kelce, as the case for [[George Kittle]] at 'TE3'. On running backs he holds the opposite tension with [[Derrick Henry]]: if a 31-year-old back gets hurt the crowd will attribute it to age and the argument is unwinnable, but demonstrated workload tolerance, an elite offensive line and the best quarterback of his career are the real drivers. Bob Harris (Footballguys, not tracked) endorsed backing older players while quoting Andy Hicks that 'age is undefeated.'
- 2025-06-23 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-23]]): older tight ends tend to be fine. He names Tony Gonzalez, late-career Jeremy Shockey, a healthy late-career Todd Heap and Antonio Gates as elite tight ends who held a high level of play as athleticism diminished, because they understood how to play the position, get open in zone, and function as reliable check-down options — and he expects [[Travis Kelce]] to at minimum be that, with weight loss possibly restoring some stamina. He applies a parallel argument to [[DeAndre Hopkins]] at 33, arguing that a receiver who never needed massive separation to win ages better in the red zone than the market assumes.
- 2025-06-30 — According to [[Matt Waldman]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-06-30]]): a dip in a great quarterback's bottom-line numbers is usually environmental before it is age. On [[Patrick Mahomes]], he attributes the decline talk to offensive line issues, defenses playing cover three against the scheme, and years of failed receiver acquisitions, and cites trainer Bobby Stroupe's rebuttal of Ben Roethlisberger's claim about quarterbacks who stop working out past age 25. He generalizes the pattern: players pushed out of a team after an All-Pro or MVP run are declared finished, then land somewhere with a good roster and show they have plenty left — meaning the surrounding team, not the aging curve, was doing most of the work. He also flags the incentive behind the narrative, calling decline stats easy clicks in June that lead to conclusions that are not well rounded.
- 2025-07-23 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception - 2025-07-23]]): the earliest reliable charting signal of a receiver's decline is the zone-coverage success rate, not the man or press number. He walks the pattern through Allen Robinson, who fell from 79.1% versus zone in 2020 to 75% in 2021 and was finished the following season in Los Angeles, and applies it to [[Davante Adams]], whose zone score dropped from 84.4% to 77% while his press and man numbers held — a signal Harmon says he refuses to 'yada yada' as he has with previous aging receivers. [[Cooper Kupp]] is the completed version of the same curve, 87.3% to 83.3% to 76% versus zone alongside a man collapse to 50.5%. Harmon's stated caveat is that nobody knows where the wide receiver cliff actually falls, so the point of naming the signal is to avoid being blindsided in weeks one through three rather than to make a confident prediction.
- 2025-08-06 — According to [[Matt Harmon]] ([[Reception Perception The Show - 2025-08-06]]): Harmon says that with aging receivers 'we don't really know what the next step is' once a decline shows up in the charting, and there are very few examples of the trend going down and then back up. He sketches two paths — the cliff (Dez Bryant, Allen Robinson: decline, then nothing) and the steady slide with no total cliff yet ([[Keenan Allen]]) — and applies the framework to [[Tyreek Hill]] at 31, where he would bet against a bounce back to elite. He is also skeptical of the vibes explanation: he is not high on a player who was dogging it because he was unhappy suddenly balling out in a new situation at 31. The same lens frames his [[Stefon Diggs]] take, where he grants a real return would make Diggs an outlier.
- 2025-08-07 — According to [[Brandon Angelo]] ([[Matt Waldman's RSP Cast - 2025-08-07]]): the assumption that bigger backs last longer may be inverting. He argued the new wave of versatile, slot-capable backs will last longer precisely because they take far less pounding between the tackles and can play in space instead of playing big. His example was [[De'Von Achane]], whom he put at roughly 195 pounds soaking wet and said 'could play a decade' if he keeps improving as a receiver, because he can legitimately play two positions. [[Matt Waldman]] applied a related argument to [[Christian McCaffrey]] in the same episode, saying the market is fading McCaffrey on age when he is no more likely to get injured than anyone else absorbing that many touches. Angelo also noted the counter-case in [[Austin Ekeler]], whose injuries have taken away the extra gear that let him separate from backers and safeties earlier in his career.
- 2025-08-18 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-18]]) [Redraft (Standard)]: the [[Tyreek Hill]] and [[Mike Evans]] segments split on how to price receivers past 30. Guest Patrick Doherty of Rotoworld (not a tracked expert) argues a speed-based archetype on the wrong side of 30 does not age well — Hill's 840-yard drop without missing a game, his first sub-2.0 yards per route run season, and 20-plus-yard catches falling from 29 to 13 — and adds that with a 32-year-old, a slow stretch that used to be an injury artefact eventually becomes decline, and that Evans now needs every trick in one of the savviest bags of the past fifteen years. [[Chris Harris]] counters that his own research shows the all-timers hang around far longer than the 'slappies' do, that he has seen plenty of receivers be good at 31, and he will take Hill in the fourth — yet he applies the decline argument himself to Evans, whose separation and speed he thinks visibly faded late last year.
- 2025-08-29 — According to [[Chris Harris]] ([[Harris Fantasy Football Podcast - 2025-08-29]]): guest Jeff Bell of Football Guys (not tracked) invokes Adam Harstad's Football Guys work that receivers do not decline gradually but nosedive, and argues a speed-dependent profile like [[Tyreek Hill]]'s nosedives hardest — giving Hill a 10 out of 10 on risk. Bell frames it as a binary: either the 2024 collapse was the cliff, or it was environmental, in which case nothing has changed because the quarterback and coach Hill dislikes are still there. Harris takes the other side at 9, arguing the film still showed the speed and that the collapse traces to [[Tua Tagovailoa]]'s concussion and a broken offensive line. Harris also publicly recants a prior aging-cliff call, saying he rated [[Derrick Henry]] something like an 8 a year earlier with 'alarm bells going off' and now calls himself an idiot for it; both rate Henry a 2-3 now. Bell separately says [[Mike Evans]] is the first year he actually sees decline on film.
